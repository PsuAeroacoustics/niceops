# Grazing residuals now point at the source

The methods most likely to improve NICEOPS at grazing incidence are now on the source side, and none of them is a new propagation solver. The largest open residual on footprints is the slow, low end of the approaches, about 3 dB too loud ahead. It has a band-by-band structure and a fore/aft sign change, which is how a borrowed source condition shows. The first build is therefore a near-hover, decelerating source condition, tested against blends the model already produces and against the day-283 low hovers that already sample the rim. The fast, high far-ahead shortfall (−2.3 dB) is the second target. Both measurement-side explanations proposed for it fail when the code is read:

- panam grids its spheres in energy, not in dB, so there is no 0.115σ² dB-averaging bias.
- No gate in either code base keeps upward fluctuations. The harness's per-band SNR gates move the residual by 0.08 dB when removed. panam *zeroes* gated bins rather than dropping them, which can only bias the rim low.

What remains for (a) is a source term, most plausibly advancing-tip Mach number or source height, and one path check nobody has run at its exact geometry. The per-line offsets (c) and the 21 dB micro-shadow on run 286288 (d) both have closed-form signatures that cost hours to test: Fresnel-zone hard patches and ground tilt for (c), Airy tunneling through a sub-meter warm layer for (d). Turbulence is unlikely to matter for any of the four residuals in a lit region, and the day-286 approaches are lit. Almost every quantitative estimate below beyond the measured residuals is either the researchers' own unverified calculation or comes from abstracts, and the site has no measured impedance, no wind below 46 ft and no turbulence data.

## Neither measurement hypothesis survives a reading of the code

Two biases were proposed as possible explanations for residual (a), since each produces a frequency-flat, sign-consistent offset. One is dB-domain averaging of scattered sphere samples: a log-normal bin's dB mean sits **0.115σ² dB** below its energy mean, which is 1.0 dB at σ = 3 and 1.8 dB at σ = 4. The other is a level gate that keeps upward fluctuations near its threshold: a Gaussian dB variable truncated at its own mean gains **+0.8σ** (researchers' own calculation; NLR's smaller ≤ 0.6 dB averaging estimate is for its own scatter classes ([Tuinstra & Sijtsma](https://dspace-erf.nlr.nl/bitstreams/10f99d37-2225-43e9-9bf1-ac584ac6cdd7/download))). Both were checked in the public-lineage panam clone (`niceops-validation/external/panam`, commit `83f989f`, the one that built the shipped database) and in the harness. Neither repository was modified.

**panam averages in energy.** In `depropagate_hemisphere` every quantity is gridded as linear power. The adaptive grid multiplies its sparse Shepard weight matrix into the sample powers (`flight_acoustics.py` lines 1626–1627, `g = weights @ power_pts`). The fixed-radius path passes powers to `shepIDW` (lines 1635–1637 and 4890). Conversion to dB comes only after gridding (`power_to_db`, lines 1640 and 1710). The sample powers themselves are linear as well: band power is summed from PSD bins in linear power, and the PSD has been interpolated to emission times in linear power since 24 September (comment and code at lines 1462–1471). The fore-to-aft hover synthesis in `build_empirical_database` averages the spectrum on an energy basis too. Two dB-domain operations remain, both in NICEOPS and both deterministic interpolation rather than averaging of scattered samples. One is the bilinear read between 2° nodes. The other is the barycentric blend across four conditions, whose forward rims on the fast approach segment agree within about 1 dB. A 50/50 dB blend of levels 1 dB apart is 0.03 dB under the energy blend, so that is not the residual either. **Hypothesis 1 is ruled out.**

**The harness's frame gates do not create the shortfall.** Frames are scored when the measured band is at least 10 dB over the microphone's ambient (the median frame of its ambient run) and 3 dB over the record's own 5th-percentile floor. There is no gate on the measured peak (`band_frames_summary.py` lines 44–51), and scores are energy means (`energy_error`, line 68). The gates are per band, not broadband, so the "one broadband selection, the same bias in every band" mechanism does not apply. The measured frames are plain 0.5 s Hann periodograms summed into bands, with no ambient subtraction (`band_frames.py` line 64). Re-scoring the residual (a) selection from `line_vs_angle.py`'s definition on the day-286 board frames (`band_frames_poles_286`, poles excluded) gives:

| Selection, 63–630 Hz | Frame-bands before / after gate | Error, no gate | Gated (shipped) | SNR gate 15 / 20 / 25 dB |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| (a) ≥ 80 kt, 400–2,000 ft, > 4,000 ft, 2–6° | 66,296 / 48,651 | −2.26 dB | −2.34 dB | −2.34 / −2.35 / −2.37 |

Removing every gate moves (a) by **0.08 dB** and (b) by 0.01 dB. The residuals are not gate artifacts. One trap turned up on the way and should be avoided in later analysis. Binning (a) by measured SNR gives +0.06 dB at 10–20 dB SNR and −4.5 dB at 40 dB and above. That is selection on the measured level, the same mechanism as the retired peak gate, and says nothing about SNR.

**panam's gate zeroes, which can only push the rim down.** The sphere build gates each PSD bin against the microphone's ambient. The shipped build passes `band_snr_gate_db = 10` (`spheres/rebuild.py` line 66; `noise_abatement_2017.py` line 750), and bins below it are set to zero power and *kept* in the energy average (`flight_acoustics.py` lines 1372–1374). The absorption cap (line 1392) and the ground-board response cap (line 1537) also zero rather than drop. So there is no upward censoring. There is a downward bias that nobody had sized. Each bin is a single Hann periodogram (line 749), roughly exponentially distributed, and the ambient reference is a per-bin median (line 1503). Gating such a bin at 10 dB over the ambient median discards **26% of its energy (−1.3 dB) if its mean sits 10 dB over ambient, 13% (−0.6 dB) at 12 dB, 4% (−0.2 dB) at 15 dB and 0.5% at 20 dB** (own calculation, 2–4 degrees of freedom). The rim's samples come from the longest ranges in the build, up to 2,000 ft, so they carry the lowest SNR, and the bias steepens the rim's fall-off. Its sign matches residual (a). Its size is probably small, because at 2,000 ft the 63–630 Hz bands are well above ambient, but the broadband bins between rotor harmonics are where it would bite. It is item 5 in the ranking below: nearly free to test.

The PE agrees with both ray models within ±1 dB at fixed emission angle on day-283 lapse profiles. The 1.2 m poles carry the same near-horizon error as the boards beside them. That retires three items from the earlier ranking:

- **The reciprocal PE excess-attenuation table:** it would reproduce what the ray models already give.
- **Band-averaged Q for the point receiver:** pole and board agree within ±0.5 dB.

## Fast high flight: lit paths, so the source is the suspect

### What the shortfall looks like

Re-reading the gated (a) frames sharpens the picture in three ways. The shortfall **grows with distance at fixed angle**: −2.05 dB at 4,000–6,000 ft, −2.17 at 6,000–8,000, −2.92 at 8,000–10,000, −3.51 at 10,000–14,000 and −4.48 beyond. It is **largest straight ahead**: about −2.9 dB within 30° of the vertical plane through the track, falling to −1.5 dB at 60–70° off it. And it is flat only on average over 63–630 Hz: band means run from −0.7 dB (250 Hz) to −3.3 dB (500 Hz) with no trend in frequency. Range and height cannot be separated in this selection, because at a fixed angle of 2–6° range is height over sin ψ. The 700–1,000 ft sources read −3.65 dB against −2.20 dB for 400–700 ft. On the day-283 low passes, the one place the question was tried, the two available methods disagreed: depropagated samples put the trend on height (+1.1 to +1.4 dB per 100 ft at 200–630 Hz), frames mostly on range (−1.5 dB/km). The two-way table in test 2 is what should settle it.

The rim being compared against is narrow. The shipped build caps paths at 2,000 ft, so every sample filed 2–6° below the horizon was emitted from no higher than **70–210 ft** (own arithmetic, straight lines). The far frames hear sources 2–14 times higher. Every published programme treats this regime as unmeasured. NASA states that "data within 10° of the plane of the horizon are not available because the acoustic measurements are made from the ground" ([Greenwood, Sim & Boyd 2016](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160009056/downloads/20160009056.pdf)). The European method keeps only ±60° lateral and the 10 dB-down window, extrapolates as a constant beyond them, and asks for slant ranges under 450 m ([Tuinstra et al. 2018](https://dspace-erf.nlr.nl/bitstreams/86fda2c5-17f9-4cca-87e4-d72bc394ef34/download); [Tuinstra & Sijtsma](https://dspace-erf.nlr.nl/bitstreams/10f99d37-2225-43e9-9bf1-ac584ac6cdd7/download)). The published rotorcraft accuracies, about 2 dBA for NASA's own NICEOPS from a 60 KIAS decelerating approach, are for stations inside about 2 km and mostly above 10–15°, with the outermost microphones flagged as worse ([Pascioni et al. 2020](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205003711/downloads/VFS2020_Paper_Pascioni_V11.pdf); [Pascioni slides](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205008035/downloads/Pascioni_VFS2020.pdf)). Filling the forward rim of fast conditions from low passes at short range has no published precedent. **A ±2 dB uncertainty there is not a failure against standard practice.** It is the edge of what anyone has validated, and accepting it is a defensible fallback if tests 2 and 5 come back empty.

### Path mechanisms that are already excluded

The path explanations fail on the evidence in hand:

- **Refraction.** Straight and curved rays give the same shortfall.
- **Ground interaction.** The poles carry the same error as the boards beside them. A flush plate and a 1.2 m microphone respond very differently to ground and to the warm-layer micro-shadow of run 286288, so either cause would separate them.
- **Absorption.** At 63 Hz it is about a tenth of a dB per kilometer, so it cannot produce the 2.5–2.9 dB shortfall at 63–100 Hz.
- **A homogeneous ground error.** For an elevated source and a flush receiver it is independent of range at fixed elevation, so it cancels between sphere build and prediction.

The PE agreement that supports "not modeled propagation" was established on day-283 lapse profiles with 60–300 ft sources. At day 286's inversion it was checked only to "mostly within ±3 dB" in the far third of the range. That gap is the second half of test 2, and it costs minutes.

### Turbulence is unlikely to matter here

The turbulence literature narrows rather than widens the options. In a lit region the energy mean at a flush receiver is unchanged by turbulence: the mean intensity is conserved, and backscatter extinction is at most about 2×10⁻³ dB over 4 km (researchers' own estimate). At a 1.2 m pole the only lit-region effect is lost coherence between direct and reflected waves. That scales as k², takes a constructive maximum *down*, and reaches only −0.1 to −2.6 dB across 63–630 Hz even at γ_T = 3×10⁻⁵ over 4 km (researchers' own calculation from the HARMONOISE form). Its signature rises steeply with frequency and its sign is wrong.

The blanket claim that "a flat residual cannot be scattering" is not right in general, though. In a refractive shadow, Kolmogorov scattering gives levels that depend only weakly on frequency above a few hundred hertz ([Di et al. 1995](https://consensus.app/papers/details/fe8b084dd4415b7ea95ebe41ab2217bb/?utm_source=claude_desktop); [Gilbert et al. 1996](https://consensus.app/papers/details/691dead398905913b3139ff366ee7368/?utm_source=claude_desktop)). NICEOPS's own PE-fitted flush scattering law has a frequency slope of only −1.55 dB per decade. Large eddies displace rays by a frequency-independent amount ([Lam 2009](https://salford-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1499569)). Upwind prediction errors run about twice the downwind ones because of "the variable position of the shadow-zone boundary" ([Wilson, Pettit & Ostashev 2015](https://acousticstoday.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/06/Sound-Propagation-in-the-Atmospheric-Boundary-Layer-D.-Keith-Wilson-Chris-L.-Pettit-and-Vladimir-E.-Ostashev.pdf)). A receiver just inside a wandering boundary can therefore gain a few dB, flat in frequency.

That mechanism needs a nearby shadow boundary. The day-286 approaches flew a calm morning under a surface inversion of about +7.6 K over 150 m, which refracts downward and lights the ground ahead. Test 7 checks this directly with the boundary column `niceops_ray_geometry` already writes, and the expected result is a kill. Turbulence stays relevant to two of the four steady runs that share the shortfall: 284209 and 284210 flew on windy day 284, where upwind lines can sit near a boundary.

One correction to the earlier research carries over. Lincke, Schumacher and Pieren (2023) were previously described as computing slanted-path coherence for an aircraft over ground microphones. Read in full, the paper concerns coherence *between the microphones of a 238-element phased array* for beamforming, not the coherence of direct and ground-reflected waves. Its height-dependent von Kármán inputs remain a usable parameter set, but it supplies no ground-interference coherence factor ([Lincke et al. 2023](https://elib.dlr.de/194540/2/10.0016847.pdf)).

### Source mechanisms worth building toward

The case for the source rests on the harness's own line analysis. The shortfall appears on every line, in all ten fast approach runs (−1.2 to −4.1 dB), and in only four steady runs at the same geometry: the 85 kt low descents 283144 and 283145, and the 1,000 ft passes 284209 and 284210. The other seven fast steady runs are −0.6 to +3.0 dB. Something about the state or the run, not the path, switches it on.

Two physical candidates come out of the rotorcraft literature.

**Advancing-tip Mach number.** Near the rotor plane ahead, the main rotor's field is thickness-dominated, and Gopalan's effective hover tip Mach number M_He = M_AT/[1 + μ(1 − M_AT)] is "the key scaling parameter for rotor thickness noise" ([Greenwood, Sim & Boyd 2016](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160009056/downloads/20160009056.pdf)). At constant indicated airspeed, levels rise with altitude because TAS rises and the sound speed falls. FRAME's monopole thickness model underpredicts that rise at high M_AT, "the increase ... is likely to be even higher in reality" ([Greenwood & Schmitz 2011](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20110011597/downloads/20110011597.pdf)). The NICEOPS database is indexed on μ, γ and C_T, so it cannot see M_AT at matched μ. NASA's own nondimensionalization for this tool carries M_H as a fourth coordinate ([Pascioni slides](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205008035/downloads/Pascioni_VFS2020.pdf)).

The size of the effect is unknown. Going from 100 to 2,000 ft ISA at 100 kt IAS raises M_AT only from about 0.831 to 0.842 (researchers' own calculation), and no full-text dB-per-ΔM_AT slope near 0.80–0.85 was found. Temperature matters at least as much as height, and its sign is not settled. Day 286's inversion put the high approach states in *warmer* air, which lowers their M_AT. The rim samples came from days 283 and 285. Whether the far states really ran at higher M_AT than the rim's low passes is the first number test 2 should produce. If they did not, this candidate dies before any regression.

**The tail rotor and the plane of the rotor.** The tail rotor's thickness noise radiates straight ahead in forward flight, at a higher elevation than the main rotor's periodic noise, 15–20 dB above the residual at every blade-passage harmonic in an 80 kt case ([Schmitz & Greenwood 2009](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20090035729/downloads/20090035729.pdf); [Rachaprolu et al. 2023](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/72623/dot_72623_DS1.pdf)). That fits the concentration of the shortfall straight ahead. The tip-path plane tilts about 12° nose-down at 110 kt, so a far-ahead observer 2–6° below the horizon is *above* the plane, where ground microphones barely sample. NASA notes that "no data is currently available with which to validate these above-the-plane noise predictions", and that lower-harmonic noise is asymmetric about the plane ([Greenwood et al. 2015](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160005979/downloads/20160005979.pdf)).

The code limits how much this asymmetry can explain. panam files every sample by elevation below the horizon and azimuth from the heading (`hemigen`, `flight_acoustics.py` lines 908–914). NICEOPS looks spheres up in the load-factor frame, which is vertical in steady flight. A steady pass's near samples and its own far frames therefore read the same sphere direction, and asymmetry about the plane cannot separate them. It can enter only through the blend, through Q-SAM's tilt under deceleration, or through a difference in tip-path-plane angle between the rim's low passes and the far states. Schmitz et al. found the plane's angle linear in V², γ and acceleration, with a constant 2° bias against theory ([Schmitz et al. 2007](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20070021458/downloads/20070021458.pdf)). At the rim's measured fall-off of about 1 dB per degree, that 2° bias alone is worth 2 dB.

The build follows the test. If the per-run table puts the shortfall on M_AT, add it as a fourth coordinate. The Delaunay interpolator takes the dimension as a constructor argument, so the code cost is in the database, not the loop. If it puts it on height alone, fit the empirical forward-rim height term the findings suggested. Fit it on steady runs and score it on the held-out approaches, because seven of eleven fast steady runs lack the shortfall, and a term that fits them will not necessarily transfer.

A cleaner separation needs new data. The Wallops design matched emission angles across microphones at different heights and ranges and read the remainder as source ([Volpe Wallops study](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/9817/dot_9817_DS1.pdf)). Repeated passes at several altitudes and a fixed stand-off, received on flush and elevated microphones over the same ground, would separate source height from path range. So would a loudspeaker on a mast at source height, with no helicopter involved. No rotorcraft programme found has done either.

## The approach's slow end is a near-hover condition no sphere was flown at

Residual (b) is the better first build because its cause is better pinned. The excess is +3.15 dB energy-mean over 30,989 gated frame-bands. Its sign follows the emission direction: +3.3 and +4.0 dB on the lines hearing the approach end ahead, +2.3 abeam, and −0.5 and −1.8 aft. That is directivity, not path. It is **not flat in frequency**. The band means are:

| 63 Hz | 80 | 100 | 125 | 160 | 200 | 250 | 315 | 400 | 500 | 630 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| +4.9 | +1.4 | +4.7 | +3.4 | +3.8 | +7.2 | +2.8 | −1.5 | +2.2 | +0.3 | −2.0 |

That swing cannot come from interference at a flush board, which has none. It is what a spectrum borrowed from the wrong condition looks like, with the wrong harmonic content in the wrong bands.

The literature explains how the borrowing happens. Q-SAM maps every 0.1 g of deceleration to a flight path angle 6° steeper ([Pascioni et al. 2020](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205003711/downloads/VFS2020_Paper_Pascioni_V11.pdf)), so a 0.2 g flare on a −7.5° glideslope looks like a −19.5° descent (researchers' own arithmetic). Below the lowest measured μ, the database can return only a replicated level sphere at the closed envelope's edge (γe −24° or +35°), a BVI sphere from a faster condition, or the synthesised hover. NASA's published decelerating validations start at 60 KIAS ([Pascioni slides](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205008035/downloads/Pascioni_VFS2020.pdf)), and no measured-against-Q-SAM comparison below about 40 kt and 100 ft was found. Small accelerations matter a great deal: accelerating at 0.084 g on a glideslope cut BVI by up to 9 dB ([Schmitz et al. 2007](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20070021458/downloads/20070021458.pdf)).

The day-283 low hovers H3–H6 measure exactly the missing directions. They were flown in ground effect at 4–30 ft and heard 0.5–3° below the horizon, and the model was +3 to +7.5 dB too loud against them at 200–630 Hz, the same sign as (b). The hover correction built from the high hovers deliberately leaves the rim alone below 10°, so neither the low hovers nor the approach end moved when it was applied. A rim correction from H3–H6 applied to the hover sphere is the natural build. Rotor ground effect sets its domain. Ground effect "decreases rapidly with increases in either height above the ground or forward speed", most of it below 1.5 times the hover induced velocity ([Cheeseman & Bennett record and summary](https://aerade.cranfield.ac.uk/handle/1826.2/3590?show=full)). For the B407 that is about 29 kt and roughly one rotor diameter, 35 ft (researchers' own estimate, v_h ≈ 10 m/s). An in-ground-effect rim is therefore valid only for the last tens of feet and knots, and the 40–100 ft, 30–40 kt part of (b) needs the Q-SAM diagnosis instead.

Test 1 decides which. If turning Q-SAM off, or reweighting away from a BVI or extended sphere, removes 1.5 dB or more and flattens the fore/aft contrast, the fix is a limit on γe at low μ or a measured decelerating condition. If only the low-hover rim moves it, ship that correction. Score the approaches only: they are held out, and the hover runs are not.

## Line offsets and the warm-ground micro-shadow have closed-form signatures

### Per-line offsets: patches and tilt, both cheap to tell apart

The line offsets repeat between steady runs and approaches: 8 of 9 lines have the same sign at 5–10°, 200–630 Hz. They shrink above the rim, which an installation offset would not do. Two ground mechanisms predict them, and each has its own fingerprint (researchers' own calculations, plane wave, Delany–Bazley at σ = 200).

**Hard or compacted patches.** A fraction w of a flush receiver's Fresnel zone at σ = 20,000 inside σ = 200 raises the level at 2° by 0.6–1.4 dB at 63 Hz, 3.8–6.6 dB at 500 Hz and 5.9–9.1 dB at 1 kHz, for w from 0.10 to 0.25. At 2° the 1 kHz zone is only 34–130 m long and a few meters wide. A vehicle track or graded pad within that distance of microphones 42–44 would produce **line −5,115's 2–6 dB excess at 200–2,000 Hz** with 10–25% hard cover. A patch's excess depends on bearing, because the zone is a thin ellipse pointing at the source, and its band and elevation pattern encodes the patch's distance.

**Ground tilt.** A local mean-plane tilt δ changes a flush level by about ±1 dB at 2° for δ = 0.3° (0.5 m over 100 m) and +3/−5.5 dB for δ = 1°. The effect scales as δ/ψ, so it is three to four times larger at 2° than at 5°. That matches the observed spread of **0.2–0.3 dB at 10–40° against 0.7–1.7 dB at 2–5°**.

The literature offers precedent but no validation at this geometry. sonAIR, the one aircraft model that weights mixed ground and terrain by Fresnel zones on a 5 m DTM with land cover, reports deviations "on the order of only ±1 dB" at receivers 0.7–7.2° up to 2.8 km ([Wunderli et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1121/10.0010419)). Its sensitivity analysis found "the quality and level of detail of the land cover data to be critical", while profiles and buildings were not ([Jäger et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1515/noise-2021-0007)). Semi-empirical step models degrade "nearer grazing incidence", and their multi-discontinuity forms break reciprocity by up to 8 dB ([Lam & Monazzam 2006](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.2216905)), so Fresnel weighting rather than De Jong is the engineering choice. Willshire's flyover fit of a concrete runway at 750 kPa·s/m², against ≥ 20,000 for sealed concrete, shows a fitted σ is a property of the zone and not of the surface under the microphone ([Willshire 1980](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19810006274/downloads/19810006274.pdf)).

The arbiter is cheap. In NICEOPS's finite-difference PE the admittance enters only the ground row, so a range-dependent β changes one row per step, as Craddock and White exploited ([Craddock & White 1992](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.403758)). The board side needs one more piece. A plate's departure from +6 dB depends on the ground around it ([Go et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsv.2024.118432)), so the plate table should be built at two σ values to bound a board standing on a compacted pad.

The data needed are a 1–5 m DEM, imagery of hard surfaces within about 1 km up-path of each line, and RTK checks where the DEM is coarse. Keeping the tilt term under 0.3 dB at 2° needs the zone's mean slope to about 0.1°. Two cautions limit the value of the build:

- No desert-soil impedance measurement was found at any US range, so a fitted σ needs a broad prior of about 200–5,000 kPa·s/m².
- Line offsets sum to zero by construction and average out of footprints.

The build earns its cost mainly as validation hygiene. The same microphones file the sphere rim, and the azimuth-stretched gridding currently averages their offsets away rather than removing them.

### Run 286288: tunneling through a sub-meter warm layer

> **Correction after investigation (2 October, night).** The profile has no warm surface layer. Toward 270 the air is fastest at z = 0 because the log-law headwind assumed below the 46 ft LIDAR gate takes 0.2 m/s off in the lowest foot; the temperature rises there. The geometry is an elevated duct under the ~330 ft inversion whose returning rays turn 1–20 ft above the ground, and the 1.2 m microphone fails too (11–16 dB under the PE), not only the flush board. Tunneling is half of it and the closed form below holds in kind: flattening the wind below 46 ft moves the PE by under 1 dB at 63–250 Hz and about 6 dB at 1 kHz, matching a WKB factor for rays turning at 1–9 ft. The other half is the skip zone before the duct's return, which no ray reaches. On measured day-286 frames where `stratified` predicts ≥ 10 dB under straight rays, it is 10–20 dB too quiet, but none of those frames lies in an SEL window. See the addendum of the same date in `Grazing incidence findings 2026-10-02.md`.

Ray theory sets the field to zero below a ray's turning point, and that silence "has been proved repeatedly in practice" to be unphysical ([Roebuck, Proc. IOA](https://www.ioa.org.uk/system/files/publications/I%20ROEBUCK%20AN%20HEURISTIC%20STUDY%20OF%20THE%20PROPAGATION%20OF%20SOUND%20TO%20LONG%20RANGES%20BELOW%20AND%20WITHIN%20ACOUSTIC%20SURFACE%20DUCTS.pdf); search snippet only). A desert analogue exists: F-18 sonic-boom levels at Edwards AFB were measured 1–2 nm beyond geometric-acoustics predictions during strong near-ground inversions ([Cliatt et al. 2015](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160001338/downloads/20160001338.pdf)).

The leak has a closed form (researchers' own derivation, not found in any source). Below a linear warm layer of depth d, the grazing field at the ground falls by **exp(−(2/3)(d/l)^{3/2})**, where l = (R_c/2k²)^{1/3} is the diffraction length NICEOPS's `ShadowTransition` already computes. That is about 5.8 (d/l)^{3/2} dB. For 3 K over 1 m it is 1.1 dB at 100 Hz, 2.7 at 250, 5.4 at 500 and 10.7 at 1 kHz, growing roughly in proportion to frequency, and it halves with d. A 21 dB ray-model deficit at 8,000–13,000 ft is what a model that zeroes the field produces where the physical field has lost a few dB below 500 Hz.

It also explains why only the flush board fails. ΔT = 1 K turns back every arrival shallower than about 3.3° *whatever the layer's depth* (θc ≈ √(2Δc/c)), while a 1.2 m pole sits above a sub-meter layer.

The build is a uniform Airy continuation of the dipping-ray family below its turning height, one quadrature per pair over layers NICEOPS already holds, joining the lit ray continuously at the turning height. The flush board then needs the ground reflection of an evanescent wave. Weyl–van der Pol at the complex angle is the consistent continuation, but no source establishes it, so it must be checked against the PE. A cruder option is to merge, per band, any layer thinner than about l into the one above it. Cheinet's result that shadow levels rise with surface heat flux says a convective surface layer also scatters into its own micro-shadow, adding a floor above the tunneled field ([Cheinet 2012](https://data-ww3.ifremer.fr/BIB/Cheinet_JASA2012.pdf); abstract only).

The near-surface ensemble (item 6) belongs here and not in front of it. Above ζ ≈ 1 the stability functions in use split three ways: linear, levelling to φ ≈ 7, or SHEBA's ζ^{1/3} ([Cheng & Brutsaert 2005](https://consensus.app/papers/details/0a60909858e05356a253dd61da80e33a/?utm_source=claude_desktop); [Grachev et al. 2007](https://consensus.app/papers/details/e721d71e25a75635aa364a43c3f6b1df/?utm_source=claude_desktop)). Each puts a different gradient below the 46 ft LIDAR gate. Inside the transitional regime a bulk Richardson number from the 2 m station, the first gate and the sonde can classify a run non-iteratively ([Gryanik et al. 2020](https://consensus.app/papers/details/48450b6196d45082bf2c08b956210d18/?utm_source=claude_desktop)). Above Ri_b ≈ 0.25 similarity should be dropped for a Banta-style sub-jet profile, whose shear clusters near 0.1 s⁻¹ when turbulent ([Banta et al. 2003](https://meteor.geol.iastate.edu/windresearch/resources/llj.banta.tke.and.llj.maximum.2003.pdf)). A morning transition is eroded mostly from above ([Angevine et al. 2001](https://ams.confex.com/ams/pdfpapers/43198.pdf)), which argues for a two-layer surface-lapse-under-inversion construction for 286288. Three to five samples suffice for a smooth two-parameter ensemble; polynomial chaos has matched 10,000 Monte Carlo samples with 5 ([Martinelli et al. 2023](https://acousticstoday.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/Uncertainty-in-Acoustical-Modeling-Sheri-L.-Martinelli-D.-Keith-Wilson-Andrew-S.-Wixom-and-Chris-L.-Pettit-1.pdf)).

Its output is a band, not a correction. Even with accurate nearby profiles, single-event predictions carry 6–8 dB of inherent random error ([Wilson 2024](https://acousticstoday.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/sound-predicting-wilson.pdf)). NWP cannot fill the layer: HRRR fails to form strong nocturnal inversions, and the 2017 lidar and balloon already beat it above 14 m.

Turbulence earns a smaller housekeeping item. γ_T estimated from surface-layer similarity falls by an order of magnitude between 1 m and 150 m (researchers' own estimate). That reconciles the pole-pair fit of 3×10⁻⁵, set within meters of a 1.2 m microphone, with the ≤ 3×10⁻⁶ the SEL supports, set by the air tens of meters up. The structural fix is a height-dependent C²(z) with a near-ground outer scale of about 2z ([Lincke et al. 2023](https://elib.dlr.de/194540/2/10.0016847.pdf)) in place of one uniform constant. It changes how the poles are modeled, not residuals (a)–(d).

Several methods were looked at and set aside:

- **ML surrogates.** Training takes hours on toy domains and errors stay at several dB ([Juliust et al. 2025](https://dael.euracoustics.org/confs/fa2025/data/articles/000602.pdf)).
- **Linearised Euler.** It costs about 1,000 times the PE for agreement within 0.5 dB on flat ground ([Colas et al. 2023](https://perso.ensta-paris.fr/~cotte/files/Colas_Emmanuelli_al_jasa2023%20Wind%20turbine%20sound%20propagation.pdf)).
- **Gaussian beams.** No rms against PE at grazing over impedance ground was found.
- **Ray–PE vertical-aperture coupling.** It has no atmospheric implementation, and it is kept in reserve should test 2 find a path effect.

## Conclusion

The grazing problem has changed character. On 30 September the open question was whether NICEOPS propagated correctly near the ground. Curved rays, the PE and the pole/board agreement now say it does, to about a decibel, wherever it has been checked. The two large residuals are what a source database built from one kind of pass shows when another kind is heard: the approach end borrows a condition nobody flew, and the forward rim of fast flight is set by passes 70–210 ft up and heard from passes 400–1,000 ft up. The measurement-side escapes are closed, apart from panam's per-bin zeroing, whose sign matches the shortfall and which costs one rebuild to size. Gains from here come from the source model and from site description, and the propagation solvers are good enough to serve as arbiters rather than suspects.

Every build above is preceded by a test that uses data and tools already in hand, so most of the ranking can be resolved in a week before anything is built. The one gap no test closes is the source's height and Mach dependence at the rim, which the 2017 data confound with range by construction. If a future campaign is in reach, the best use of it is the Wallops geometry rather than more ground microphones: repeated passes at several altitudes past the same stand-off, received on flush and elevated microphones over surveyed ground, with a mast loudspeaker as a source-free check.
