# Trace the city once per receiver

The best way to add street canyons to NICE-OPS is a **per-receiver urban insertion-loss table**, indexed by arrival direction and third-octave band, built offline by a **deterministic low-order image-edge solver**: specular facade reflections to order 2-3, Pierce/UTD-class roof-edge diffraction to order 1-2, a diffuse reverberant tail and a turbulence-scattered floor. The run consumes it as one extra per-band loss per eigenray. Ray tracing is part of the answer, but as the builder's visibility engine (Embree) and at most its diffuse-tail estimator, not as the runtime method. Every published per-pair urban path search costs between 0.14 ms and 1.2 s per source-receiver pair, which is 10^3 to 10^7 times NICE-OPS's 20 ns to 1 µs budget across ~10^8 pairs per run. Gaussian beams, the other popular aerial-source choice, leave building shadows 20-35 dB too quiet because no published UAM implementation diffracts. Reciprocity changes the problem. A helicopter hundreds of meters away illuminates a 20 m canyon almost as a plane wave, so each receiver's urban response depends on where the sound comes from, not on the aircraft's exact position. The geometry can therefore be solved once per city and receiver set, at any cost, and read back at about the cost of a sphere lookup. The run side of this is mostly built already: the `--propagation_table` seam, its `extra_loss` path and its per-band `path_gains` bound are what the urban table needs. The real risks lie elsewhere. One is in the physics the builder must get right: roof-edge diffraction, facade scattering, and everything below about 100 Hz. The other is that no measured data exist for helicopters inside canyons. The design also fails where it is most wanted: vertiports and low approaches, where the source is within a few hundred meters of the buildings and a direction-only table stops being valid.

## Per-pair path search costs 10^3 to 10^7 times the budget

NICE-OPS's hot loop evaluates 94.3 million source-receiver pairs on the demo case: 19.4 ns each broadband, about 150 ns on the spectral path and about 1 µs with the ground model. Any urban method has to be judged first against that budget, because the published geometric methods differ from each other far less than they differ from it. The RWTH image-edge chain, coupled to a curved atmospheric eigenray, took **0.8-1.2 s per source/receiver position** at combined order 3, and its authors note much of that is MATLAB-to-C++ data transfer ([Schäfer, Reich & Vorländer 2022](https://d-nb.info/1262974569/34)). The fastest game-engine diffraction search, with silhouette-edge preprocessing and A* over an edge-visibility graph, needs **0.14-7.5 ms per source** ([Schissler, Mückl & Calamia 2021](https://research.facebook.com/file/4521255027934795/Fast-Diffraction-Pathfinding-for-Dynamic-Sound-Propagation.pdf)), and a 255,000-triangle city with fourth-order diffraction ran at about 5 ms per source per frame on four cores ([Schissler, Mehra & Manocha 2014](https://gamma.cs.unc.edu/HIGHDIFF/paper.pdf)). Taking 1 ms as a representative figure, 10^8 pairs cost about 10^5 core-seconds per run, against the current 2-15 s of loop CPU. At Schäfer's cost it is three core-years.

Raw ray throughput is not what limits this. Embree traces 9-117 Mrays/s of full path tracing on 2014 CPUs ([Wald et al. 2014](https://www.sci.utah.edu/~wald/Publications/2014/embree/embree.pdf)), and an RTX 2070 sustains 50-635 Mrays/s of incoherent secondary rays ([CEUR Vol-2485](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-2485/paper3.pdf)). The expensive part is path validation: the image-source bookkeeping, visibility of each path leg, edge enumeration and per-band diffraction coefficients. A GPU buys 10-100x on that work, not the 10^4x needed. The one aircraft tool that models buildings in production confirms the arithmetic from the other side. Empa's sonAIR precomputes building attenuation at airspace cell corners, and even so needs **about two weeks per airport** on a few workstations. Its authors recommend buildings only for special investigations, not large-scale mapping ([Schalcher et al. 2025](https://www.dora.lib4ri.ch/empa/dload/empa:42543/PDF/view)).

So the question is not which tracer is fast enough. None is. The question is which quantity can be precomputed and reused across the thousands of source states that every receiver sees. Two production systems answer it the same way. Microsoft's parametric directional coding runs its wave simulations *reciprocally*, from probes at the constrained listener positions. It stores about 100-120 KB per probe (80-160 MB per game map) and decodes in about 100 µs per source ([Raghuvanshi & Snyder 2018](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2018/10/paramd.pdf)). sonAIR stores attenuation per receiver at virtual source positions, with cell sizes that grow with altitude and distance, and interpolates by aircraft position ([Schalcher et al. 2025](https://www.dora.lib4ri.ch/empa/dload/empa:42543/PDF/view)). Both are receiver-centered fields over source position. For a source far away compared with the street scale, that field collapses to a function of direction alone, which is what makes a compact table per receiver possible.

## Ray tracing earns a place in the builder, not the hot loop

Once the per-pair budget rules out every on-the-fly path search, the remaining comparison is about what each method contributes to an offline builder or to a narrow near-field mode. The table below condenses the evidence; the discussion that follows explains the verdicts.

| Method | Best published accuracy | Cost | Deterministic, bounded? | Role in NICE-OPS |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Image sources + edge diffraction (BTM, UTD, Pierce) | UTD vs BTM: 0.67 / 3.10 / 3.61 dB at direct / 1st / 2nd-order diffraction receivers | 0.14 ms - 1.2 s per pair | Yes | **Builder kernel**; per-pair mode near vertiports |
| Stochastic ray or particle tracing | 6.69 dB at 1,000 rays vs a 10,000-ray reference; 2.27 dB with diffuse caching | ms per source per frame | No (Monte Carlo noise) | Diffuse tail inside the builder, at most |
| Gaussian beam tracing | No diffraction in UAM codes; shadows over 20 dB too deep | 46.6-122.5 s per case on 36 cores | Yes, but no edges | None |
| 3-D full wave (FDTD, PSTD, BEM) | Reference quality | Scales as f^4; practical to about 1-2 kHz | Yes | A few reference cases |
| 2-D / 2.5-D wave, plane-wave incidence | Reference quality for canyon cross-sections | Cheap below 500 Hz | Yes | Low-band canyon tables |
| Energy and analytic canyon models | Wei A_can: 2.7 dB std. dev. vs 20,740 FDTD runs | Closed form | Yes | Reverberant tail; canyon-class fallback |
| Engineering (ISO 9613-2, Maekawa, CNOSSOS) | Over 10 dB off near facades; 14 dB(A) under in a courtyard | Very cheap | Yes | Fallback only |
| ML surrogates | 0.3-2.6 dB RMSE on fixed 2-D maps | ms per map | No error bound | None |
| **Per-receiver precomputed table** | The builder's accuracy plus interpolation | About 10-50 ns lookup (estimate) | **Yes, with exact max-cell bound** | **Run-time core** |

**Image sources with edge diffraction is the best-validated deterministic family.** Biot-Tolstoy-Medwin (BTM) is the accurate reference. UTD, the cheap asymptotic form, is within 0.67 dB of BTM in the lit zone but 3.10 and 3.61 dB off at first- and second-order diffracted receivers, and it overestimates high-frequency attenuation ([Schissler, Mehra & Manocha 2014](https://gamma.cs.unc.edu/HIGHDIFF/paper.pdf)). Against FDTD, summing *every* UTD path overestimated a fence-like scene by **about 20 dB at 84 Hz**, while keeping only the loudest diffraction path matched far better ([Schissler, Mückl & Calamia 2021](https://research.facebook.com/file/4521255027934795/Fast-Diffraction-Pathfinding-for-Dynamic-Sound-Propagation.pdf)). Half-order filter approximations to single-wedge diffraction reach errors below 0.1 dB ([Ewert et al. 2023](https://consensus.app/papers/details/608d7e1b23015cd19fc0ea8425b6dfd1/?utm_source=claude_desktop)). The order needed is modest. Second-order diffraction to non-shadowed receivers can often be neglected ([Torres, Svensson & Kleiner 2001](https://consensus.app/papers/details/43078de916a65cf6b45e6775e670ef02/?utm_source=claude_desktop)), and canyon levels saturate after about four reflections where horizontal reflections dominate ([Wei, Cooke & Havie-Clark 2015](https://www.apexacoustics.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/Acoustics2015-How-many-reflections-should-I-use.pdf)). For a source above the roofline, the dominant energy path into a shielded canyon is one roof-edge diffraction plus a few facade reflections; that is Wei's A_can term, which "quickly dominates" the bare over-the-roof path ([Wei et al. 2014](https://publications.lib.chalmers.se/records/fulltext/206833/local_206833.pdf)). The 5th-10th-order diffraction that game engines chase is for street-level sources wrapping around blocks.

**Stochastic ray tracing handles diffuse facades naturally but converges too slowly and too noisily for the hot loop.** Path tracing at 1,000 rays was 6.69 dB from a 10,000-ray reference, and radiosity-like caching brought that to 2.27 dB ([Schissler, Mehra & Manocha 2014](https://gamma.cs.unc.edu/HIGHDIFF/paper.pdf)). It also has no diffraction until one is bolted on. Stephenson's uncertainty-relation diffraction, the standard way to do that in a particle tracer, stays within 3 dB of an exact secondary-source model over a wide range but drifts to 5 dB deep in the shadow ([Stephenson, DAGA 2012](https://pub.dega-akustik.de/DAGA_2012/data/articles/000063.pdf)). Monte Carlo noise of several dB is incompatible with NICE-OPS's bit-reproducible goldens and with bounds that must never be exceeded. Inside an offline builder that writes a frozen file, however, the noise is harmless to reproducibility. That is the one place stochastic tracing fits: as an estimator of the higher-order diffuse tail, with a fixed seed.

**Gaussian beam tracing is disqualified by its treatment of edges, not its cost.** The UAM vertiport model that propagates a noise sphere through 3-D buildings and wind "does not account for the diffracted wavefield behind the building blocks". Its straight-ray-versus-beam shadow differences exceeded 20 dB, which the authors concede diffraction would shrink. Runs took 46.6-122.5 s on a 36-core Xeon, against 3.7 s for straight rays ([Yunus et al. 2023](https://iris.polito.it/bitstream/11583/2978683/1/2023YunusAST.pdf)). GPU ports reach 790x in a city, with the beam summation at receivers dominating as rays increase ([Sheng et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.13382v1)). Beams' real advantage, unifying refraction with reflection, is one NICE-OPS already has through its stratified eigenrays, so beams would add a missing-diffraction defect without adding a capability. Riegel's ray-plus-radiosity sonic-boom canyons show the same failure in another code: "strong shadow zones where no sound was detected" ([Riegel & Sparrow 2024](https://doi.org/10.1121/10.0024980)). Any geometric model without edge diffraction is wrong behind buildings.

**Wave methods are references and low-band builders, not production.** Published canyon full-wave runs are almost all 2-D or 2.5-D. Van Renterghem's FDTD used 2.5 cm cells to reach the 1250 Hz band ([Van Renterghem, Salomons & Botteldooren 2006](https://users.ugent.be/~tvrenter/publicaties/streetcanyonsFDTDPE.pdf)). The engineering model fitted to 20,740 FDTD results says outright that its full-wave model "cannot be used for 3D simulations due to computational cost" ([Wei et al. 2014](https://publications.lib.chalmers.se/records/fulltext/206833/local_206833.pdf)). Scaling from those grids, a 3-D FDTD of a 30 m canyon box is about 7 x 10^8 cells at 1 kHz and 10^4 times that at 10 kHz (my extrapolation). So 3-D references stop near 1-2 kHz, below the 2-4 kHz bands that dominate A-weighted helicopter levels. At the low end the economics invert: a 2-D canyon cross-section under plane-wave incidence is trivial at 100-500 Hz, which is exactly where geometric methods fail.

**Energy and engineering models supply structure, not accuracy.** Radiosity, diffusion-equation and transport models reproduce reverberation and decay along a street, and Kang's closed-form level formula tracks his radiosity model "generally within 2 dB" ([Kang, Meng & Brown](https://staffwww.dcs.shef.ac.uk/people/G.Brown/pdf/SS25-034-IP-Kang-UK.pdf)). They describe the field once energy is inside the canyon, not how it enters from above at a given elevation. The ISO 9613-2 barrier term "underestimates the attenuation considerably", and an official noise map underpredicted a measured inner-yard level by more than 14 dB(A) ([Wei et al. 2014](https://publications.lib.chalmers.se/records/fulltext/206833/local_206833.pdf)). ISO 9613-2 and Pierce differ by more than 10 dB near a facade ([Wei, Cooke & Havie-Clark 2015](https://www.apexacoustics.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/Acoustics2015-How-many-reflections-should-I-use.pdf)), and Maekawa's detour law overestimates diffracted energy in the shadow more than any alternative tested ([Stephenson, DAGA 2012](https://pub.dega-akustik.de/DAGA_2012/data/articles/000063.pdf)). sonAIR nonetheless uses Maekawa because it is cheaper, with vertical-plane diffraction only and incoherent third-order reflections checked for visibility but not diffracted ([Ramseier et al. 2024](https://www.dora.lib4ri.ch/empa/dload/empa:38392/PDF/view)). That is a reasonable fallback, not a target.

**Surrogates fail on the property NICE-OPS depends on most.** Image-to-image networks reproduce 2-D urban maps at 0.3-2.6 dB RMSE with speedups near 2000x ([Kim & Lee 2024](https://doi.org/10.1121/10.0024242); [Eckerle et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04510)). Those errors are averages, the size of the effects being modeled, and come with no bound. The only certified-bound noise surrogate (1.2-5.6 dB per sector) models no buildings ([Park et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.20306v1)). The idea worth borrowing is the certification: a table that stores its maximum per cell is a certified bound at no training cost.

## A direction-indexed insertion-loss table rides the existing propagation seam

### What the table stores

Each receiver r gets a table U_r(band, bearing, elevation): the building **insertion loss** in dB at that microphone. It is defined against NICE-OPS's own open-ground prediction for the same source, so it is relative to the existing ground model, not to free field. That definition is the coupling contract (my recommendation). The run computes everything it does today, and then subtracts U along each arrival. Inside a canyon the street surface still reflects, so the builder must model each urban path's ground bounce with the same ground model NICE-OPS uses, by linking `ground_effect.cpp` rather than reimplementing it. Then the ratio of "with buildings" to "without" counts the ground exactly once. Bearings should be compass bearings toward the source, turned to the local frame through `CompassToLocal`, as the propagation table already is.

The direction grid should be piecewise bilinear on a regular grid, not a spherical-harmonic fit. Microsoft chose non-negative cosine-squared lobes over low-order spherical harmonics because harmonics "ring to the opposite hemisphere" ([Raghuvanshi & Snyder 2018](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2018/10/paramd.pdf)). For NICE-OPS, ringing across a shadow boundary would also break the max-over-cell bound. The resolution is set by the sharpest feature, a diffraction shadow boundary, whose angular width is about sqrt(λ/d_edge). That is 7.5° at 1 kHz for an edge 20 m away, and 1.7° at 4 kHz for one 100 m away (my estimate). A 2-3° grid resolves the A-weighted bands near edges; high bands behind distant edges come out step-like, which costs accuracy but not the bound. At 5° (about 825 upper-hemisphere directions), 24 bands and int16 storage, 14,400 receivers take about 0.57 GB; at 3° about 1.6 GB, or about 110 KB per receiver (my estimate). That is the size of Microsoft's per-probe payload, and small enough to stay L2-resident while one observer's state loop runs. sonAIR's own criterion offers a large compression: it treats propagation as unobstructed when the aircraft is more than 15° above the local obstacle horizon ([Schalcher et al. 2025](https://www.dora.lib4ri.ch/empa/dload/empa:42543/PDF/view)). So the grid can be fine only within a band around each receiver's skyline and coarse above it.

### How it is built

The builder works by reciprocity. From each receiver it treats every direction cell as incident plane-wave illumination, plus a few range nodes where needed (below). It enumerates the paths: the direct path if Embree finds it visible, specular images in nearby facades to order 2-3, and single- and double-edge diffraction over roof edges with a Pierce/UTD-class or filter-approximation coefficient, keeping only the dominant path per edge sequence. Paths are summed in energy per band. Two further terms go on top. The first is a diffuse tail for higher reflection orders, Lambertian with scattering coefficient s ≈ 0.1-0.3 and absorption α ≈ 0.02-0.1: the sum of facade absorption and scattering on real streets has been estimated at 0.1-0.25 ([Onaga & Rindel 2007](https://consensus.app/papers/details/7b1aedaadcc350d2965ee9abba3a0afb/?utm_source=claude_desktop)), and diffuse energy grows with reflection order ([Ismail & Oldham 2005](https://consensus.app/papers/details/0887509e867b5b7bb49df13cd4cb6fb5/?utm_source=claude_desktop)). The second is a turbulence-scattered floor after Forssén et al.: single scattering, von Kármán saturation, and a canyon correction ([Forssén et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.3813/aaa.918760)). That floor is not optional. In measured inner yards the multiple-reflection term fixed the low frequencies only, and "adding a turbulent scattering contribution becomes therefore essential" at high ones ([Wei et al. 2014](https://publications.lib.chalmers.se/records/fulltext/206833/local_206833.pdf)). Upwind, turbulence erased almost all of the extra shielding that refraction alone predicted ([Van Renterghem et al. 2006](https://users.ugent.be/~tvrenter/publicaties/streetcanyonsFDTDPE.pdf)). NICE-OPS's `ShadowScattering` already has this shape for refractive shadows, so the building version is an extension, not a new subsystem.

Plane-wave incidence makes each direction solve cheaper than a general pair solve, because the source has no position to search from and the receiver's candidate facades and edges can be enumerated once and reused across all directions. Even at a pessimistic 1 ms per direction, 14,400 receivers x about 1,000 directions is about 4 core-hours once per city and receiver set (my estimate), against sonAIR's two weeks per airport for a source-position grid. Summation should be incoherent from about 100-200 Hz up. Coherent line sources "mostly" overpredict levels at shielded canyon positions ([Hornikx & Forssén 2007](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.2783197)), and Empa's reflection module uses the incoherent model because facades "behave diffusely starting from around 600 Hz and above" ([Ramseier et al. 2024](https://www.dora.lib4ri.ch/empa/dload/empa:38392/PDF/view)). This is also NICE-OPS's existing convention for summing arrivals.

### What the hot loop pays, and how the bound stays exact

At run time, a pair's urban term enters exactly where the propagation table's does today. `spectral_level_along` takes a per-band `extra_loss`, and a table run sets it per covered pair. The urban table adds U_r for the arrival's direction to the same array: one cell location shared across bands, then four corners per band. That is roughly 10-50 ns against the spectral path's 150 ns (my estimate), so the acoustic loop grows by perhaps 10-30% rather than by orders of magnitude. The windowed peak search stays exact by the same mechanism the propagation table already uses. Bilinear interpolation never exceeds its largest corner, so each receiver's per-band maximum of U's gain is an exact bound for the table model, and it enters the bound setup alongside `path_gains`. A per-(receiver, direction-bin) maximum would keep more of `DirectionBounds`' tightness. An independent physical sanity bound exists as well: every reflected path is at least as long as the direct one, so K extra specular paths add at most 10 log10(1 + K) dB in energy, +6 dB for two facades plus the ground (my derivation). Measured gains sit well inside that: +3-4 dB on runway-facing facades ([Ramseier et al. 2024](https://www.dora.lib4ri.ch/empa/dload/empa:38392/PDF/view)) and +5 dB in a beam model's illuminated zone ([Yunus et al. 2023](https://iris.polito.it/bitstream/11583/2978683/1/2023YunusAST.pdf)). Two regression tests come with this. An empty city must reproduce the goldens byte for byte, and an `urban_windowed_is_exact` CTest must check the windowed run against a full march, in the pattern of `propagation_table_windowed_is_exact`.

### How it couples to refraction and the ground

The published precedent for joining curved atmospheric rays to a straight-ray urban model is Schäfer's virtual source method. It finds the curved free-field eigenray, places a virtual source along its arrival direction at c times the travel time, runs the image-edge model from there, and then renormalizes spreading, absorption and launch direction to the curved path. Against curved rays re-traced to every interaction point, its errors were at most 2.5° in arrival elevation, under about 1 dB in spreading and 1.2 ms in delay. They were largest when the launch was nearest horizontal ([Schäfer, Reich & Vorländer 2022](https://d-nb.info/1262974569/34)). NICE-OPS can take a cheaper version of the same idea. Every `Arrival` from `--refraction stratified` already carries its own launch direction, arrival angle, ray-tube spreading and travel time. The urban term is looked up by each arrival's arrival direction and the arrivals are summed in energy, as `layered_arrivals` already does. The sphere is still read at the launch wave normal, and spreading and absorption still come from the eigenray, with no virtual source to place. The extra absorption over an urban detour of a few tens of meters is negligible below a few kHz (my estimate). The stated assumption, that atmospheric effects "do not change significantly among the different urban paths", is inherited along with the method ([Schäfer, Reich & Vorländer 2022](https://d-nb.info/1262974569/34)).

Two practical rules follow from how NICE-OPS places receivers. Grid observers that land inside building footprints have to be masked or moved to the roof, or the table describes a microphone in a wall. And facade receivers need a declared convention. ISO 1996-2 uses +3 dB near a reflecting surface and +6 dB flush on it ([Flores et al. 2019](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0003682X1830598X)). Below 250 Hz, Wei et al. subtract 3 dB from facade-mounted data for direct-reflected coherence ([Wei et al. 2014](https://publications.lib.chalmers.se/records/fulltext/206833/local_206833.pdf)). The table should model free-field-equivalent microphones away from facades and leave facade corrections explicit, rather than baking in a coherent doubling that the incoherent sum cannot represent.

## Validation climbs from wedges to Schiphol courtyards

No dataset exists that validates the whole chain for a helicopter over a city. NASA's 2025 UAM review still lists measurements in urban environments as a need and suggests canonical geometries with simple, well-characterized sources ([NASA/TP-20250011527](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250011527/downloads/NASA-TP-20250011527_v1.pdf)). Validation therefore has to be a ladder in which each rung checks one layer.

The bottom rung is the diffraction kernel against BTM. Svensson's EDtoolbox computes it in the frequency and time domains and is BSD-licensed with only the two redistribution clauses, so its formulas can be ported with attribution ([EDtoolbox](https://github.com/upsvensson/Edge-diffraction-Matlab-toolbox)). Single wedges, thin screens and double edges become Catch2 cases. The second rung checks the table against its own per-pair reference. On a real city block, compare the direction-indexed table with point-source image-edge solves at decreasing range, to measure where the far-field assumption breaks; no published study has done this. The third rung is 2-D canyon physics: the urban benchmark cross-sections of Hornikx et al., whose references came from openPSTD and FDTD ([Hornikx et al. 2017](https://pubs.aip.org/asa/jasa/article/141/5_Supplement/4035/625703/Benchmark-cases-for-urban-sound-propagation)). Since no reference data deposit was found, they would be rerun with openPSTD as an external GPL executable ([openPSTD](https://github.com/openPSTD/openPSTD)). Van Renterghem's parameter study supplies sensitivities to reproduce: rigid against Z = 10 facades shifts shielding by about 20 dB, facade relief adds about 10 dB of shielding from 500 Hz up, and downwind refraction removes about 10 dB of it ([Van Renterghem et al. 2006](https://users.ugent.be/~tvrenter/publicaties/streetcanyonsFDTDPE.pdf)). The fourth rung is measured 3-D: RWTH's EDGE scale-model database, about 31 generic urban scenes with CAD geometry, source directivities and material data, under CC BY-SA 4.0 ([Palenda & Vorländer, EDGE](https://zenodo.org/records/21264202)). Its sources are not aircraft-like, so it tests the builder's geometry, not elevated incidence.

The top rung is field data from real aircraft, and it is thin. Lugten's pilot near Schiphol measured mean front-to-rear shielding of **about 11 dB(A) for landings and 14 dB(A) for take-offs**. In octave bands it was **0-7 dB below 125 Hz and 8-14 dB above** ([Lugten, Steemers & Kang](https://api.repository.cam.ac.uk/server/api/core/bitstreams/044505b4-6042-47fe-a02f-a6ac4a5f70b9/content)). The follow-up courtyard field lab analyzed 2383 flyovers. Line-of-sight versus shielded facades differed by **-0.8 to 4.3 dB(A) in SEL for arrivals but 8.1 to 11.6 dB(A) for departures** ([Lugten et al. 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360132324002427)), and no public data deposit was found. Empa's Meiringen validation adds model-to-measurement evidence. Buildings cut the annual level by more than 2 dB over half the area and by up to 12 dB right behind them; reflections added 3-4 dB on exposed facades; and adding reflections brought a shielded microphone's median error to about 1 dB ([Ramseier et al. 2024](https://www.dora.lib4ri.ch/empa/dload/empa:38392/PDF/view)). At the Paris Issy heliport, neighbors on opposite sides of one block differed by about 10 dB ([Caillet et al. 2025](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-7047184/latest.pdf)). The user's 2017 flight-test data is open-field, so it covers only the no-building regression. A canonical canyon measurement with a well-characterized rotorcraft source would fill the single largest gap, and NASA has said it wants one.

The inputs deserve as much scrutiny as the method, because their uncertainty is the same size as the method error. Facade scattering is the most uncertain parameter. The ISO 17497-1 coefficient is called "the most uncertain standard metric applied in geometrical acoustics" ([Heimes et al. 2023](https://consensus.app/papers/details/2af3cf63851057fd92b528cf73e6b49f/?utm_source=claude_desktop)), and diffuse reflections move canyon levels by -2 to +4 dB, or +10 dB with street fittings ([Can et al. 2015](https://consensus.app/papers/details/5b57a1256924590689a24ba3a6213ec9/?utm_source=claude_desktop)). Building heights come next. Imagery-derived Microsoft heights show 3.6-4.3 m RMSE against lidar, with regression slopes of 0.53-0.72 that systematically underestimate tall buildings, where lidar-based heights reach 2.4-3.1 m ([Liu et al. 2026](https://cartogis.org/docs/conferences/CaGIS_2026/abstracts/research/Liu_et_al_research_abstract_CaGIS_2026.pdf)). That bias flattens H/W and lowers roof edges, so it would systematically understate shielding. USGS 3DEP lidar is public domain ([AWS registry](https://registry.opendata.aws/usgs-lidar/)). 3D BAG LoD2 is CC BY 4.0 ([4TU](https://data.4tu.nl/datasets/94a8f656-f7c9-4cf6-a2e7-c4c823b3db92/1)), and Overture/OSM footprints are ODbL ([Overture](https://docs.overturemaps.org/attribution/)). Under ODbL, using the data as run input imposes nothing on MIT code, while shipping a derived test city would be share-alike on that file.

The license picture permits a clean permissive stack.

## The table fails below 100 Hz and near the source

### Low frequencies are a wave problem

Image sources and rays are high-frequency methods, and a canyon stops being geometric somewhere in the low hundreds of hertz. A modal-plus-image model agreed with a scale model and 2-D FDTD **up to about 100 Hz full scale** for a 5 m wide, 6.75 m high street; above that, leaky modes with mode-dependent losses were needed ([Richoux et al. 2009](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0907.0605)). For streets 10-20 m wide, the first cross-canyon modes fall at roughly 8.5-17 Hz (my estimate), right on the rotor fundamentals and first harmonics at 10-30 Hz. The measurements point to a usable simplification. Shielding below 125 Hz was only 0-7 dB at Schiphol ([Lugten, Steemers & Kang](https://api.repository.cam.ac.uk/server/api/core/bitstreams/044505b4-6042-47fe-a02f-a6ac4a5f70b9/content)). In 2-D Euler simulations of urban sonic booms, canyon resonances changed waveforms but the median perceived level stayed within 1 dB of flat ground ([Dragna et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1121/10.0016442)). I would make two recommendations. Below about 100 Hz, fade the table toward zero insertion loss, since buildings are nearly transparent to energy there. Between about 100 and 500 Hz, take the table from 2-D or 2.5-D wave solves of canyon cross-sections under plane-wave incidence, tabulated by canyon class (H/W, receiver offset, facade impedance) and blended into the geometric table above. No validated low-frequency correction for an elevated source exists, so the crossover is an inference that the second and third validation rungs must confirm. For A-weighted SEL this band matters little; for C-weighted, low-frequency or indoor metrics it dominates.

### Vertiports and low approaches break the far-field assumption

The direction-only table assumes every urban path sees the same source. Two errors grow as the source approaches. One is spreading: a reflector d from the receiver lengthens a path by at most 2d, so applying the direct path's spreading over-predicts that arrival by up to 20 log10(1 + 2d/r). That is 0.56 dB at d/r = 1/30 and 1.1 dB for d = 20 m at r = 300 m (my derivation). The other is direction: scatterers within radius a of the receiver see the source from directions spread over about a/r radians, 5.7° for a = 50 m at r = 500 m, which moves rooftop shadow boundaries. Both suggest the table is good to about 0.5 dB beyond roughly 30-35 times the distance to the farthest significant facade or edge, or about 0.6-1.7 km for 20-50 m street scales (my estimate, untested in the literature). Approach geometry can be covered by adding a log-range axis with 4-8 nodes, which is sonAIR's altitude-adaptive cell grid in receiver-centered coordinates.

Closer in, the table's form fails, not just its resolution. A facade reflection then leaves the rotor at a substantially different emission angle from the direct path. The sphere must be read per path, so the table has to store paths rather than band gains: each image source's position and its loss factors, evaluated against the source state at run time. With the source near or below the roofline, horizontal paths around building corners return, and higher diffraction orders matter "not only in shadow regions but also around edges" ([Palenda & Vorländer 2026](https://zenodo.org/records/19112715)). sonAIR drops lateral diffraction only because fast-moving sources average it out ([Ramseier et al. 2024](https://www.dora.lib4ri.ch/empa/dload/empa:38392/PDF/view)), an argument that a hovering or landing aircraft breaks. The near-field magnitudes are large. ONERA found +8 dB near a corner and up to +14 dB with source and microphone both in a canyon, coherently and with rigid walls ([Malbéqui 2023](https://dael.euracoustics.org/confs/fa2023/data/articles/000104.pdf)). At a rooftop vertiport, wind changed the footprint by up to 23 dB ([Yunus et al. 2023](https://iris.polito.it/bitstream/11583/2978683/1/2023YunusAST.pdf)), and building wake turbulence adds fluctuations above 3 dB ([Goyal et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1121/10.0042402)). The right structure here is a separate per-pair mode. The same image-edge kernel runs per pair through Embree, restricted to a vertiport neighborhood of 10^2-10^3 receivers and to the states the existing bounds cannot cull from the SEL window. At about 1 ms per pair, 10^3 receivers x 10^4 states is about 3 core-hours (my estimate). That is affordable for a site study, not for a regional footprint.

### Grazing incidence, wind in the canyon and long streets

Three further limits remain. Schäfer's coupling error peaks at near-horizontal launch, and in a refractive shadow NICE-OPS's level comes from `ShadowTransition`'s creeping-wave field, which has no well-defined arrival direction. There the urban term needs a direction-agnostic fallback, such as the table's horizon-averaged value. Buildings also reshape the wind, so the stratified assumption fails "at least in the vicinity of the ground" ([Schäfer, Reich & Vorländer 2022](https://d-nb.info/1262974569/34)). Downwind refraction reduced canyon shielding by about 10 dB, and the effects did not add ([Van Renterghem et al. 2006](https://users.ugent.be/~tvrenter/publicaties/streetcanyonsFDTDPE.pdf)), so a still-air table will understate downwind levels in shielded canyons until tables are built per wind class. Finally, along long streets a mostly specular model overstates propagation compared with diffuse boundaries ([Kang 2000](https://consensus.app/papers/details/3d8adc693cac522ca61dd2bc02538b6b/?utm_source=claude_desktop)), which is one more reason the diffuse tail cannot be skipped.

## Conclusion

Urban propagation for NICE-OPS is an amortization problem first and a ray-tracing problem second. Once the geometry is solved per receiver rather than per pair, the run-time side reduces to a feature the code already has: a per-band loss through `extra_loss` with a maximum fed to the bounds. So the engineering risk moves almost entirely into the builder's physics and its inputs. Roof-edge diffraction, facade scattering and the turbulence floor each carry errors of several dB. Height and scattering inputs carry errors of the same size. Those, not intersection speed, set the accuracy the project can claim. The receiver-centered table also turns out to be the common form of the two production precedents: Microsoft's listener probes and sonAIR's source cells are the same field, with and without its range axis.

The evidence also points to a tension worth planning around. For near-overhead events, urban effects on SEL are small: Schiphol arrivals moved only -0.8 to 4.3 dB(A), and simulated median boom levels stayed within 1 dB of flat ground. The effects become large exactly where the cheap table is weakest: lateral receivers at low elevation, low approaches and vertiports, which need range axes, per-path sphere lookups or a per-pair mode. So the first deliverable should be the far-field table with its bound and empty-city regression. The rotorcraft-in-canyon measurement that no one has published should be specified alongside it, because it is what would show whether the near-field machinery is worth building.
