# Refraction outranks impedance at grazing incidence

The grazing-incidence residuals NICEOPS still carries are range- and weather-dependent, so the methods most likely to reduce them are the ones that treat the near-surface atmosphere and its coupling to the ground reflection, not a better ground-impedance formula. Three measured facts drive that: on calm day 286 the model is about 2 dB too quiet beyond 4,000 ft at 2–10° and the 63–630 Hz bands decay 1–2 dB/km more slowly than predicted; on windy run 284153 the measurement falls 4–6 dB/km *faster* than every model; and at 0–2° the flush microphones read 5–10 dB above every ground-aware model from 500 Hz up (`research_notes/.../current_model_and_residuals.md`, quoting the validation harness). A homogeneous ground error cannot produce the first two, because for an elevated source and a flush receiver the ground term depends on elevation angle and not on range (researchers' own calculation), so it is absorbed when the spheres are built and cancels in prediction. The first thing to do is therefore not to build anything: compare the one-reflection ray model against the existing parabolic equation (PE) and normal-mode series under the day-286 inversion, and redo the 0–2° pole/board comparison with curved-ray grazing angles. Only if the PE shows the ray model losing 1–2 dB/km at low frequency is a reciprocal PE table worth building, at an estimated 2 core-hours per profile to 1 kHz. Swapping to a slit-pore ground is cheap and worth one validation run, but against the variable-porosity ground the validation already uses it is worth under 1 dB at its class-center parameters. Much of the quantitative case rests on the researchers' own unverified calculations and on abstracts; the primary texts for the refraction-corrected reflection coefficient, the Nord2000 and Harmonoise reports, and the standard books could not be opened.

## What this adds to the earlier options write-up

The earlier write-up (`NICEOPS Atmospheric and Urban Propagation Options.md`, 26 September 2026) chose curved-ray eigenrays feeding the existing spherical-wave ground model, with a PE as reference and optionally as an offline table below 25–30° elevation. Most of that is now built: linear and layered eigenrays, a shadow transition, a scattering term, a Crank–Nicolson PE and the Attenborough benchmark series. This report does not revisit that choice. It looks only at the 0–10° band over soft ground, and it changes or sharpens the earlier conclusions in six places.

First, the earlier caveat that the spherical-wave coefficient "weakens under refraction" now has a shape. The curved-ray-geometry-into-homogeneous-Q heuristic is the L'Espérance model; Li showed it approximates a ray-theoretic closed form, is limited to a single reflection, and that the profile–ground interaction is strongest when the impedance is high, which for grass means low frequency (abstract only: [Li 1993](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.406738)). Salomons needed an empirical reflection coefficient to match full-wave solutions at low frequency in logarithmic profiles (abstract only: [Salomons 1994](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.409852)). Those are exactly the bands of the day-286 deficit. Second, the earlier write-up named a Green's-function PE with surface-pole subtraction; what was built is a finite-difference Padé(1,1), and the literature read here supports that for soft ground: finite-difference schemes put the impedance into the matrix, whereas the GFPE has documented accuracy problems at large normalized impedance that needed a separate fix (abstract only: [Gilbert 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25618104/)), and Fourier split-step codes at 2.5–10 wavelength steps shift interference patterns by 10–50 m (full text: [Nyborg et al. 2023](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/334925908/1299_1_10.0020562.pdf)). Third, the earlier argument for full moving-medium ray invariants does not apply at grazing: the effective-sound-speed error in the Snell invariant is 0.06% of the wind term at 2° and 1.5% at 10° (own calculation), and near-ground PE comparisons against linearised Euler differ by no more than 0.5 dB beyond 1 km (full text: [Colas et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.09669)). It stays a steep-angle refinement. Fourth, the "20 to 30 dB below free field" shadow plateau quoted earlier could not be confirmed from anything read in this round; only the qualitative statement survives (abstract only: [Gilbert, Raspet & Di 1990](https://pubs.aip.org/asa/jasa/article-abstract/87/6/2428/893181/Calculation-of-turbulence-effects-in-an-upward?redirectedFrom=fulltext)). Fifth, the earlier cost guess for reciprocal PE tables (2–5 core-hours per profile) is refined to about 2 core-hours to 1 kHz and 60 to 5 kHz for 18 azimuths and two receiver heights, from NICEOPS's own 2.7 ns per point per step (own estimate); the conclusion is to stop the table at 1–2 kHz. Sixth, the earlier risk statement that "the near-ground gradient controls the grazing geometry, and it is rarely known well" is now the central finding rather than a footnote.

## The residuals point at range-dependent mechanisms

### What is measured, and how well

All residuals below are read from local files and were not recomputed. With the 2° spheres and the plate receiver, day-286 SEL at 5–10° peak elevation is still +1.4 to +2.0 dB high (62 mic-runs), and no mic-run peaks below 5°, so the SEL tables say nothing about 0–5° (`H/propagation/database_effect_B407_standard.txt`). The frame-level tables show why that bin is misleading: the error is positive inside 4,000 ft on the forward lines (+1.5 to +2.8 dB, attributed to the end of the approach, low and slow) and negative beyond it on every line (−1.3 to −3.1 dB at 2–10°), with a fixed-direction range slope of −2.40 dB/km over 65,634 frames (`H/propagation/band_frames/aweighted.txt`). An SEL-by-peak-elevation statistic therefore mixes a short-range source excess with a long-range propagation deficit of the opposite sign. Three limits on this evidence matter for what follows: the per-band slope table is not stored, only the README's summary of it; the frames were produced under `stratified` with plate and scattering only, so nobody knows how much of the slope the refraction model itself adds or removes; and the slope's standard error treats correlated frames as independent.

### Why a fixed ground cannot be the long-range answer

The impedance note computed the flush-receiver level for an elevated source with the full Weyl–van der Pol formula at 1.5 km and again at 3 km, and found the same numbers to 0.1 dB at 2–10°: the numerical distance is already large, and the level is the plane-wave value 20 log|2 sin ψ / (sin ψ + β)| (own calculation). Two consequences follow. A homogeneous ground error at a given elevation is the same at 600 m as at 3 km, so it is divided out of the spheres (built inside 2,000 ft with the same ground model) and multiplied back in at prediction. And a decay-rate error needs something that changes with range at fixed elevation: refraction, a ground that varies along the path, extra arrivals, or absorption. The notes did not examine absorption as a candidate; at 63 Hz it is too small to matter, but it is not excluded at 500–630 Hz.

### Four tensions in the notes, and where each lands

**Multiple ground bounces versus a single lifted reflection.** The aircraft-practice note ranks downward refraction first and attributes the slow low-frequency decay to extra ground-reflected arrivals beyond the "second reflection distance", following Willshire's Medicine Bow wind-turbine data, where 6–11 Hz harmonics from a roughly 40 m source decayed at 3 dB per doubling from 450 m to 10 km (full text: [Willshire 1985](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19850018007)). The refraction-theory note derives the opposite for day 286. Reading the inversion (+7.6 K over 150 m) as one linear gradient of 0.030 s⁻¹, n-bounce rays are born in pairs at r_n = √(8n(n+1)·R·h_s); the first pair for a 100 m source appears at 4.26 km and 1.34° elevation, and no one-bounce arrival exists above 0.94° at 3 km or 1.57° at 5 km (own calculation, not checked against Embleton's or Salomons' ray-counting rules, which could not be read). So at 2–10° inside 5 km the model's one direct and one reflected ray is the complete ray set. The two notes reconcile once the Medicine Bow conditions are read closely: the elevation there ran down to 0.2°, the wind was 12–15 m/s, and in the same test the 63, 250 and 1000 Hz bands from the ~100 m source stayed spherical to the 1,000 m measured. The aircraft note's extension of that mechanism to 63–630 Hz at 1–5 km is its own inference, and it says so. **The resolution is that multi-bounce is not the leading candidate, but it is not dead:** it needs a gradient 1.3 times the inversion mean at 2°/5 km and 3.2 times at 2°/2 km, which a surface inversion concentrated in the lowest tens of meters reaches. The refraction note offers two replacements. The curved ray steepens the ground angle by r/2R (+3° at 1.2 km, +12.6° at 5 km), which lifts the flush ground term for a 100 m source by 2–14 dB over straight rays and makes it rise about 0.3–0.5 dB/km with range (own calculation, Delany–Bazley 200). That mechanism is already in `stratified` if the gradient is right, and the deficit was measured with it switched on, so by itself it does not close the gap. The second is that the 150 m duct holds only about 6 trapped modes at 63 Hz and 12 at 125 Hz (own WKB estimate), a regime where a two-ray picture is doubtful and where the modes' decay is set by the ground admittance. Only a full-wave comparison distinguishes these, and the code already has both references.

**−40 log|β| versus −20 log|β|.** A project memory note, repeated as an inference in the residuals note, says the flush level "goes as about −40 log|β|" and uses that to suspect the ground near the receiver. The impedance note corrects it: the fourth-power law belongs to source and receiver both on the ground; for an elevated source and a flush receiver the sensitivity is at most −20 log|β|, and doubling flow resistivity raises the flush level by 1.5–3.0 dB at 1°, 1.1–2.2 dB at 2° and 0.3–1.0 dB at 10° (own calculation, checked against the full formula to 0.1 dB where |w| > 3). This is resolved in favour of the correction. Its practical meaning is that the 0–2° residual of 5–10 dB would need flow resistivity raised by a factor of order ten, which is what the pair fits found (σ 3200–6400) and which no other elevation tolerates.

**Calm day slower, windy run faster.** Day 286 decays more slowly than the model at 63–630 Hz; on run 284153 (76 m, no shadow in any model) the measured level falls 4–6 dB/km faster than every model, the turbulent PE included (`H` git log 085b6c1). This is flagged, not resolved. Opposite signs on different days are what a meteorological cause does and a fixed ground error does not: a harder ground flattens the predicted decay, helping day 286 and making 284153 worse. But 284153 is one run on one bearing with eight microphones, and the fact that even the PE misses it points at the input profile, where wind below the lowest LIDAR gate (46 ft) is an assumed log law with z0 = 0.1 ft, or at the source. The aircraft literature supports the reading: at Denver the sign of the low-angle residual followed the wind direction, and with a 10–15 kt downwind component "there is virtually no ground attenuation" (full text: [Plotkin, Hobbs & Bradley 2000](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20000057531/downloads/20000057531.pdf)).

**The 0–2° flush microphones want a far harder ground.** Every ground-aware model scores 6.5–6.8 dB rms at 0–2° against the co-located pole/board pairs while variable porosity σe 200 is best at every elevation from 2° to 40° (`P/docs/ground_plane_corrections.md`). Four explanations are on the table. Refraction is the strongest: the pair comparison uses straight rays, and a ground angle lifted by a few degrees moves the flush level by the 6–14 dB the own-calculation table shows at 1–2° elevation. A range-dependent ground is second: at 2° a flush receiver's Fresnel zone runs 130–900 m toward the source at 1 kHz–63 Hz, against 6–85 m at 10° (own calculation, quarter-wavelength criterion), so the lowest-elevation frames sample different ground, on an airfield, and the centerline pair already fits a different flow resistivity (about 60 against 120–180). Third is the reference: the "measured" transfer function near grazing is the measured board-minus-pole difference plus the pole's *modeled* reflection of −10 to −14 dB at 125–500 Hz, and the pole model takes Q at the band center and treats roughness with a Kirchhoff factor that is unity at grazing. Sub-wavelength roughness acts instead as an effective-admittance change that deepens a 1.2 m pole's 500 Hz dip by 3–9 dB at 1–2° (own calculation from [Attenborough & Taherzadeh 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apacoust.2025.111067), whose eq. 16 was read from a garbled layout). If the pole's true dip is deeper than modeled, the inferred board level is too high; that is this report's inference and no note tests it. Fourth, below 10° the signal-to-noise gate leaves few frames above 1 kHz, so the surviving frames may be the loud ones. The plate itself is ruled out: thickness is a ±1 dB effect and all plate models agree within 0.3 dB there. This tension is unresolved, and the curved-ray rerun is the test that separates the first explanation from the rest.

### The refraction–ground coupling comes first because it is nearly free to test

NICEOPS has no stored PE case at the day-286 geometry; the stored cases are log laws, a linear lapse, `example_windy` and 18 day-284 upwind profiles. It does have the PE, `downward_pressure` for the mode series, and `pe_compare --no_pe` to rescore. The published warning is specific: two single-reflection linear-profile engineering models missed a PE by up to 10 dB at 300 m when the true profile was logarithmic, and agreed perfectly with no wind (full text: [van Maercke & Salomons 2009](https://pub.dega-akustik.de/NAG_DAGA_2009/data/articles/000250.pdf)). Raspet's own check of the heuristic found it realistic at long range over soft ground because the ground removes the rays that violate ray criteria (abstract only: [Raspet, L'Espérance & Daigle 1995](https://pubs.aip.org/asa/jasa/article-abstract/97/1/154/835941/The-effect-of-realistic-ground-impedance-on-the?redirectedFrom=fulltext)). No source at any access level tests the heuristic for a 30–300 m source at 0–10°; every quantitative test found has source and receiver within meters of the ground. One further full-wave feature bears on the two receiver heights: in a nocturnal duct a "quiet height" near 2 m sat about 10 dB below the ground-level field beyond half a kilometer at a few hundred hertz (lay-language paper, read in full: [Waxler et al.](https://acoustics.org/pressroom/httpdocs/152nd/wax.html)). That is a ground-to-ground result, but a single reflected ray cannot produce it, and a 1.2 m pole sits right there.

### The profile is an input problem the literature says will not go away

Monin–Obukhov similarity, the usual way to fill the layer below a LIDAR's first gate, "cannot be used when turbulent mixing is suppressed, as happens in strong inversion conditions" (full text: [Wilson, Pettit & Ostashev 2015](https://acousticstoday.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/06/Sound-Propagation-in-the-Atmospheric-Boundary-Layer-D.-Keith-Wilson-Chris-L.-Pettit-and-Vladimir-E.-Ostashev.pdf)), which is day 286. The same source puts mean-level predictions from mean profiles at under 2 dB rms except in shadows and interference minima, and single-event predictions at 8–10 dB rms even with synchronized local profiles. A helicopter pass is an event, so a 2 dB residual is only a model error if it persists across runs; on day 286 it does, across 25 approaches. Each band senses the profile averaged over roughly (R/2k²)^(1/3), which is 16 m at 63 Hz and 2.6 m at 1 kHz for the day-286 radius (own calculation), so the assumed layer below 14 m matters at every audio frequency. The defensible treatment is an ensemble of near-surface gradients, scored as a band, not a better single profile.

### The impedance model matters less than its reputation suggests here

The Open University and Lyon work is unambiguous that one-parameter Delany–Bazley should go: it is non-causal, predicts negative real parts of complex density at low frequency, and fits grassland worst, with average fitting errors of 9.3 against 8.7 for slit-pore and 6.7 for variable porosity over 26 grass sites (full text: [Attenborough & Taherzadeh 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apacoust.2025.111067)). The difference between admissible and one-parameter models at longer range is "comparable with seasonal variability over grassland" (abstract only: [Dragna, Attenborough & Blanc-Benon 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26520321/)). The impedance note's own table puts that at 2–4.5 dB at 2° in the 250–1000 Hz bands, slit-pore over Delany–Bazley at equal flow resistivity, in the direction of the deficit. **But the validation does not use Delany–Bazley.** It uses variable porosity with σe = 200 and αe = 0, which has equal resistance and reactance like a semi-infinite slit-pore ground. Putting the coded formula through the plane-wave limit at 2° gives −1.8 dB at 250 Hz and −6.2 dB at 1 kHz, against −1.3 and −5.4 dB for slit-pore at the class-5 center (250 kPa·s/m², porosity 0.25); that is this report's own arithmetic at band center, and it says the model swap is worth 0.5–0.8 dB. The lever is the parameters, not the formula: the 2026 paper's class-5 range (150–350, porosity 0.1–0.4) spans 5–8 dB at 2°, its "compacted" class 7 (1,500) sits 3–5 dB higher, and wet-to-dry ratios of 1.2–1.8 in flow resistivity are worth 1–2 dB at 2° (own calculation on full-text parameter tables). Porosity is a parameter the current fit cannot see at all. Against that freedom stands the pair data: doubling σe from 200 to 400 makes 2–5° worse (1.28 to 1.71 dB rms). No source covers desert or dry-lake-bed soil, and the site's impedance was never measured; short-range two-microphone methods would not settle the 63–630 Hz bands anyway, being reliable only above about 400 Hz (full text: [Kruse dissertation](http://www.akustik.uni-oldenburg.de/literatur/Kruse/Dissertation_Kruse_Ohne_Artikel.pdf)).

### What the notes rule out

Local reaction is adequate: substituting an extended-reaction admittance changes the level by ≤ 0.1 dB for grass-class ground at 1–10° and up to 1 dB only for snow-class layers (own calculation; Kruse's full text says the effect is negligible at 100 kPa·s/m²). The Weyl–van der Pol approximation itself is exact enough: against direct quadrature of the complex-image integral it is within 0.011 dB in 108 grass-like cases at 300 m–5 km (own calculation). A surface wave is not excited at or above 2° over grass-like ground; the one thing to check is that `faddeeva`'s documented domain (Im z ≥ −1.2) is not left if a thin soft layer is ever run near 1°. Willshire tested a surface-wave explanation for his slow decay and found it more than 20 dB too small. Small-scale roughness, modeled correctly as an effective admittance, makes the flush prediction 0.5–2.5 dB *quieter* at 2° and below, so it cannot cure a model that is already too quiet; its only role here is in the pole reference. Turbulent decorrelation does nothing for a flush microphone, whose direct and reflected paths coincide, and the calm day had little turbulence to offer.

### Why the reciprocal table is step 7 and not step 1

The numerical-methods note makes a good case for the table's design. Every elevated-source PE study read runs forward from a handful of source heights: seven heights give 0.3–1.2 dB worst-case error and ten give 0.3–0.8 dB, worst near a shadow boundary (full text: [Cotté 2019](https://perso.ensta-paris.fr/~cotte/files/Cotte_jasa145_2019%20Extended%20source%20models%20for%20wind%20turbine%20noise%20propagation.pdf)). NICEOPS has the opposite shape, 270 m of source height and two receiver heights, so one reciprocal march per receiver height, azimuth and frequency yields every source height and range with no height interpolation. Under the effective-sound-speed approximation that swap is exact. No paper was found that does it, which is an absence in a limited search and not a proof; and a receiver on the ground becomes a source on the ground, where the Gaussian starter is poorly defined over soft ground. Factorizing the sphere's directivity out of the PE is validated for wind turbines, and one to five frequencies per third-octave band is the only published sampling rule (full text: [Colas et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.09669)). The table is step 7 because it answers a question step 2 has not yet asked: if the PE agrees with the ray model on the measured profile, the table would reproduce the same 2 dB deficit at greater cost.

### What aircraft practice adds and does not add

Empirical lateral attenuation (SAE AIR 5662, 10.86 dB at grazing beyond 914 m) is an A-weighted fit to 1.2 m pole data over grass that lumps ground, refraction and scattering; it is not a ground-impedance number and should not be applied to a band-resolved model (formulas read in full as reproduced by [Berton 2021](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210011612/downloads/AIAA-Aviation2021-Berton-Manuscript-FINAL.pdf); the SAE documents themselves were not read). The useful lessons are about attribution. Across the Wallops and Denver experiments the mean follows an impedance model, the scatter and the sign of the low-angle residual follow refraction, and the revision from AIR 1751 to 5662 removed about 3 dB of attenuation below 15° because the older fit was too quiet against later data. The best reported physical model at grazing, sonAIR, claims ±1 dB in the mean out to 2.8 km (abstract only: [Wunderli et al. 2022](https://pubs.aip.org/asa/jasa/article/151/5/3140/2838395/Aircraft-noise-in-situations-with-grazing-sound)), which is A-weighted event level, not band decay rate. No study found reports a residual as dB/km by band at 1–5 km; NICEOPS's regime sits beyond what has been measured with a controlled source at audio frequencies.

## What could not be read

The gaps are large enough to change confidence. None of the four standard books was accessible (Salomons' *Computational Atmospheric Acoustics*, Ostashev & Wilson, Pierce, *Predicting Outdoor Sound*), nor the Nord2000 reports (AV 1849/00, AV 1851/00), the Harmonoise HAR29TR report, SAE AIR 1751 and 5662, ESDU 82027, ANSI S1.18 or NORDTEST ACOU 104. The bodies of the papers that matter most for step 2 were behind paywalls: Li 1993, Raspet et al. 1995, Salomons 1994 and 1998, L'Espérance et al. 1992, and Ostashev, Breton & Wilson 2024 on slanted paths. So were the per-site tables of Attenborough et al. 2011 and the range-dependent results of Dragna et al. 2015, the sonAIR grazing paper, the recent ground-board correction papers, and the Volpe reports on the hybrid PE/FFP model. Several formulas in the refraction note (the uniform caustic field, the coherence factors, the similarity profiles) are written from memory and marked as such; none should be coded before it is checked against a text. The 2017 test report itself was unreadable locally, so whether it holds soil or micrometeorological detail is unknown. And the site has no measured ground impedance, no wind below 46 ft and no turbulence measurement, which no amount of reading repairs.

## Conclusion

The earlier write-up treated the ground model as the asset to protect and refraction as the thing to add. After this round the picture inverts at grazing: the ground model's formulation is sound to hundredths of a decibel for locally reacting soil, and what is uncertain is the angle at which refracted sound meets it and the few meters of atmosphere that set that angle. The two residuals that look like ground problems, a flush microphone wanting a tenfold harder ground at 0–2° and low bands decaying too fast in the model, are both what a straight-ray or weakly refracting reflection would do under an inversion, and the windy day's opposite sign is the same dependence seen from the other side.

That makes the next month's work mostly measurement of the model against itself. Two PE runs and one rerun of the pair comparison decide whether the remaining error is in the propagation physics, where a reciprocal PE table is a known and affordable fix, or in the inputs and the spheres, where no propagation method helps and the honest output is an ensemble band. The one purchase worth making regardless of how those tests fall is a two-parameter admissible ground with porosity exposed, because it costs little and removes a model class the literature no longer defends. If a future test campaign is in reach, the flight array itself, at 2–5° and 63–630 Hz, is a better impedance probe than any short-range method, provided the wind and temperature in the lowest 15 m are measured alongside it.
