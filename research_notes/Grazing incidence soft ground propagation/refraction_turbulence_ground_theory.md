## 1. Validity of the homogeneous-atmosphere spherical-wave reflection coefficient with curved-ray geometry

### Takeaway
The heuristic NICE-OPS uses (curved-ray path lengths and ground angle fed to the homogeneous Weyl–van der Pol Q) is the L'Espérance et al. model. Its authors' own checks say it agrees with the FFP at long range over realistic (soft) ground, and Li showed it is an approximation to a ray-theoretic closed-form solution, limited to one reflection and weakest where the ground impedance is high. Against a PE it is good for linear profiles at a few hundred meters and can be 10 dB off at 300 m when the true profile is logarithmic; at low frequency in a log profile Salomons needed an empirical reflection coefficient. I found no published refraction-corrected F(w) that I could read in full.

### Cited Findings
- The heuristic model "uses an analytic ray trace method to calculate ray paths"; "the effects of finite ground impedance are treated by calculating the spherical wave reflection factor as if the atmosphere was homogeneous. The actual reflection angles and times of flight are used"; because the profile is linear "no focusing factors are calculated". It "has been compared to full wave calculations and is found to agree well at long ranges". **[ABS]** — [Raspet, L'Espérance & Daigle, JASA 97, 154 (1995)](https://pubs.aip.org/asa/jasa/article-abstract/97/1/154/835941/The-effect-of-realistic-ground-impedance-on-the?redirectedFrom=fulltext)
- Same paper: "the heuristic model produces realistic predictions even though certain criteria [for ray tracing] are violated. The finite ground impedance is shown to remove those rays for which the ray tracing criteria do not hold." That is: over soft ground the near-grazing (multi-bounce) rays for which ray theory is invalid are also the ones the ground attenuates most. **[ABS]** — [Raspet et al. 1995](https://pubs.aip.org/asa/jasa/article-abstract/97/1/154/835941/The-effect-of-realistic-ground-impedance-on-the?redirectedFrom=fulltext)
- The paper the brief calls Li 1993 "A note on the heuristic…" is indexed as K. M. Li, "On the validity of the heuristic ray-trace-based modification to the Weyl–Van der Pol formula". It obtains "a closed-form solution … for the sound field over an impedance plane in the presence of a sound-speed gradient" from "a ray theory approximation in the wave equation" and shows "the heuristic formula represents an approximation of the solution". It adds: "there is a strong interaction between the sound-speed profile and the ground surface, in particular, when the impedance is high. The solution is limited to a single reflection." **[ABS]** — [Li, JASA 93, 1727 (1993), doi:10.1121/1.406738](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.406738)
- Salomons' semi-analytical ray model for logarithmic and power-law profiles: "Ground reflections are taken into account by either the plane wave reflection coefficient or the spherical wave reflection coefficient … At high frequency both coefficients yield good agreement with numerical solutions of the Helmholtz equation. **An empirical reflection coefficient is introduced to obtain good agreement at low frequency.**" Also: "much of the energy flows along the highest rays, in particular at high frequency", and turbulence effects "are rather small for downwind sound propagation". **[ABS]** — [Salomons, JASA 95, 2425 (1994), doi:10.1121/1.409852](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.409852)
- Li, Taherzadeh & Attenborough's improved ray tracer uses "a generalized Snell's law" for vector wind, Gaussian quadrature and a bracketing eigenray search; "the interray coherence is determined from turning point heights rather than from integrals along the ray paths". The authors acknowledge "inherent limitations of the ray-trace approach, particularly in respect of logarithmic wind profiles". **[ABS]** — [Li, Taherzadeh & Attenborough, JASA 104, 2077 (1998)](https://pubs.aip.org/asa/jasa/article-abstract/104/4/2077/559801/An-improved-ray-tracing-algorithm-for-predicting?redirectedFrom=fulltext)
- Test of two single-reflection, linear-profile engineering models against a PE (source 0.75 m, receiver 2 m, 300 m, ground 100 kPa s m^-2, downward refraction). For the linearised profile (a = 0.177 s^-1) "the high excess attenuation of 15 dB at 2 kHz is not reproduced by HAR and N2K". For the logarithmic profile (b = 1 m/s, z0 = 0.1 m) the PE's "excess attenuations are considerably lower (5–15 dB for 500–2000 Hz)" and the engineering models' "deviations are still up to 10 dB". With no wind "there is perfect agreement". **[FULL]** — [van Maercke & Salomons, NAG/DAGA 2009](https://pub.dega-akustik.de/NAG_DAGA_2009/data/articles/000250.pdf)
- Same paper, on the published accuracy of these models: the "small differences HAR-REF and N2K-REF reported in [Jónsson & Jacobsen] (standard deviations of 2.5 and 3.0 dB, respectively) may be too optimistic in some cases". **[FULL]** — [van Maercke & Salomons 2009](https://pub.dega-akustik.de/NAG_DAGA_2009/data/articles/000250.pdf)
- Guidance from a numerical ray tracer validated on near-ground downward and upward refraction: the choice of equivalent wind profile has to be tuned against measurements ("Guidelines for the suitable simulation of the wind velocity profile are derived by correlating predictions with measurements"). **[ABS]** — [Prospathopoulos & Voutsinas, JASA 122, 1391 (2007), doi:10.1121/1.2764476](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.2764476)

### Inferences
- **[RECALLED]** The homogeneous form being borrowed: p = e^{ikR1}/R1 + Q e^{ikR2}/R2, Q = R_p + (1 − R_p) F(w), R_p = (sin ψ − β)/(sin ψ + β), F(w) = 1 + i √π w e^{−w²} erfc(−i w), w = ((1+i)/2) √(k R2) (sin ψ + β), ψ the grazing angle and β the admittance. The heuristic substitutes the curved-ray ψ, the acoustic path lengths (c0 × travel time) for the phases, and the reflected path length for R2 in w.
- **[OWN CALC]** For a flush microphone there is no path difference and the whole ground effect is 20 lg|1 + Q|, so it depends on the curved-ray grazing angle alone. For a circular arc the ground angle is (chord elevation) + r/(2R). With R = 11.4 km that is +3.0° at 1.2 km and +12.6° at 5 km: at 2° elevation the ground angle is 5.0° at 1.2 km, 9.6° at 3 km, 14.6° at 5 km. Evaluating Q with Delany–Bazley, σ = 200 kPa s m^-2, source at 100 m (scipy `wofz`):

  | f (Hz) | 1.2 km straight / arc | 3 km straight / arc | 5 km straight / arc |
  | --- | --- | --- | --- |
  | 63 | 4.3 / 4.9 dB | 1.9 / 5.1 | −0.5 / 5.4 |
  | 125 | 3.0 / 4.2 | −0.7 / 4.5 | −3.7 / 4.9 |
  | 250 | 1.3 / 3.0 | −3.6 / 3.5 | −7.0 / 4.2 |
  | 500 | −1.0 / 1.3 | −6.8 / 2.0 | −10.6 / 3.1 |
  | 630 | −1.9 / 0.6 | −7.9 / 1.4 | −11.8 / 2.7 |
  | 1000 | −3.8 / −1.0 | −10.2 / 0.0 | −14.3 / 1.6 |

  So under this inversion the flush-microphone ground term is worth 2–14 dB relative to straight rays, and it *rises* with range (about +0.3 dB/km at 250 Hz, +0.5 dB/km at 630 Hz) because the grazing angle grows. Any error in the near-ground gradient goes straight into this term.
- **[OWN CALC]** Sensitivity of that term to an unresolved surface layer. Snell gives θ_g² = θ_fit² + 2 δc / c0, where δc is the sound-speed deficit at the surface that the fitted profile misses. An extra 2 K of surface cooling (δc = 1.2 m/s) adds 0.2–0.8 dB at 1.2–2 km and 0.1–0.4 dB at 3–5 km (largest at 500 Hz–1 kHz). This is the right sign for the brief's "model too quiet" residual but is under 1 dB and falls with range, so by itself it does not explain a 2 dB deficit that grows at 1–2 dB/km.
- Li's remark that the profile–ground interaction is strongest for *high* impedance, and Raspet's that soft ground removes the rays that violate ray criteria, both say the heuristic is safest over soft ground and at mid/high frequency, and least safe at low frequency (where grass is acoustically hard: |β| at 63 Hz is small) and for rays that turn close to the ground. Salomons' need for an empirical low-frequency coefficient is the same statement from the log-profile side.
- The brief's "modified numerical distance" and "Li's Airy-function-based reflection coefficient" are presumably the closed form in Li 1993 and its successors. For a linear (k²-linear) profile the exact field is an Airy-function mode or residue sum, which NICE-OPS already has in `exact_solutions.cpp`; a per-band table of (exact series)/(heuristic) versus grazing angle and numerical distance, built the way `ShadowTransition` is, is the obvious way to get a refraction-corrected Q without the closed form.

### Gaps
- The L'Espérance et al. 1992 paper (Appl. Acoust. 37, 111) has no abstract in any index I could reach, and I could not read the body of Raspet 1995, Li 1993 or Salomons 1994. I therefore cannot give the quantitative accuracy (dB versus FFP, by range and frequency), the ray-validity criteria Raspet discusses, Li's closed-form expression, or Salomons' empirical low-frequency reflection coefficient.
- Ostashev & Wilson (2015) and Attenborough & Van Renterghem (2021) treatments of this question: not accessed.
- I found no source, at any access level, that tests the heuristic specifically for a 30–300 m source and 0–10° elevation. All quantitative tests found are for sources and receivers within a few meters of the ground.

## 2. Downward refraction and inversions: multiple ground-reflected rays, ducting, engineering treatments

### Takeaway
For the brief's inversion read as a linear gradient, my ray count says multi-bounce arrivals cannot reach a ground receiver at 2–10° elevation inside 5 km: the first one-bounce pair appears at r = 4 √(R h_s), i.e. below about 0.4–1.6° elevation. Neglected multi-bounce rays therefore do not explain the 2 dB deficit unless the real gradient near the ground is 1.3–5 times the 150 m mean. The published evidence for slower-than-spherical decay from elevated sources (wind turbines, low-level jets) is at lower angles, longer ranges or infrasonic frequencies. At 63–125 Hz the inversion holds only about 6–12 trapped modes, so a one-arrival ray picture is itself doubtful there.

### Cited Findings
- Elevated-source measurement. An 80 m hub-height, 4 MW turbine, ground microphones 300 m–10 km downwind (elevation angles "from 8.0 to 0.2 degrees"), wind 12–15 m/s at hub: harmonics at 6, 8 and 11 Hz follow "3 dB per doubling of distance"; "the 450 m data point falls nearly on the 6 dB/doubling line, while the remaining data fall roughly on the 3 dB/doubling line". In the same test the 63, 250 and 1000 Hz third-octave bands, corrected for absorption, "exhibit spherical spreading" out to the 1000 m measured. **[FULL]** — [Willshire, NASA TM-86409 (1985)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19850018007)
- Same report, the ray explanation: "For distances less than the shortest second reflection distance, only a single ray is received for a point on the ground … multiple rays are received" beyond it; "When the source height is increased, the ray-tracing pattern is magnified and the closest second reflection distance is increased." The low-frequency sources were at about 40 m and the broadband ones at about 100 m. A surface-wave explanation was tested and rejected: predictions were "more than 20 dB below the measured results". The author notes ray tracing "is not considered applicable to frequencies as low as 20 Hz" and proposes normal modes. **[FULL]** — [Willshire 1985](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19850018007)
- Low-level jet, elevated source: "Close to an elevated point source, such as a wind turbine or an aircraft, spherical spreading results in a 6 dB decrease in sound level per doubling of the distance … at a transition distance, the downwind propagation changes the spherical spreading into a cylindrical spreading with a 3 dB decrease. It is shown how the transition distance and sound intensity depend on the LLJ parameters. The pivotal phenomenon is the non-coherent superposition of ground reflected rays in the turbulent atmosphere." **[ABS]** — [Makarewicz, JASA 139, 1611 (2016)](https://pubs.aip.org/asa/jasa/article-abstract/139/4/1611/662499/Cylindrical-spreading-due-to-downwind-refraction?redirectedFrom=fulltext)
- Harmonoise ignores multi-bounce; Nord2000 corrects for it. "For large distances … multiple ground reflections become important (in particular for hard ground), and these reflections are not taken into account by Harmonoise. In contrast, Nord2000 includes a correction term to account for multiple ground reflections in a downward refracting atmosphere." Yet "Harmonoise performs better than Nord2000 for a case with downward refraction over 300 m flat ground, both for hard ground and for absorbing ground". For the hard-ground log-profile case the authors call it "surprising that HAR follows the reference solutions so well until 500 Hz, as the high excess attenuation levels are partly due to multiple ground reflections … which are ignored". **[FULL]** — [van Maercke & Salomons 2009](https://pub.dega-akustik.de/NAG_DAGA_2009/data/articles/000250.pdf)
- Harmonoise's curved-ground analogy: "a coordinate transformation (conformal mapping) [is applied] to the ground vertices … In the case of downward refraction … a flat ground surface is transformed into a valley-shaped terrain. This approach assumes a linear sound speed profile c = c0 + az … considered as an approximation of the logarithmic profile c = c0 + b ln(1 + z/z0), with parameters b and z0 = 0.1 m." "Both Harmonoise and Nord2000 are restricted to linear sound speed profiles." **[FULL]** — [van Maercke & Salomons 2009](https://pub.dega-akustik.de/NAG_DAGA_2009/data/articles/000250.pdf)
- How Harmonoise picks its one radius: the profile is "the sum of linear and logarithmic components" with coefficients A and B; the gradient is evaluated "for downward refraction, [at] the maximum height of a ray that would travel directly from the source to the receiver, and for upward refraction, [at] the mean of the source and receiver heights. The latter choice leads to relatively small radii of curvature for upwind propagation, which may in turn give excessive attenuations". **[FULL]** — [AAS 2012 conference paper, "The Harmonoise noise prediction algorithm"](https://www.acoustics.asn.au/conference_proceedings/AAS2012/papers/p43.pdf)
- Harmonoise versus Nord2000 generally: "In most cases the models agree very well, but in some cases the predictions deviate significantly from each other." **[ABS]** — [Jónsson & Jacobsen, Acta Acustica 94, 282 (2008), doi:10.3813/AAA.918031](https://doi.org/10.3813/AAA.918031)
- Nord2000 "does not take account of the increase in sound pressure level resulting from multiple reflections caused by downward refraction over a hard surface in a proper way". **[SNIPPET]** (search summary of the FAA/Volpe evaluation; I did not locate the sentence in the text) — [Ahearn et al., DOT/FAA/AEE/2012-5](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/9687/dot_9687_DS1.pdf)
- Modal description of a downward-refracting atmosphere over an impedance plane: the field separates "into a ducted part, expressed as a sum over modes which decrease exponentially with height, and an upwardly propagating part, expressed as an integral over modes which are asymptotically … plane waves"; eigenvalues are complex and are found as a perturbation of a self-adjoint problem; applied "to a model for the nighttime boundary layer". **[ABS]** — [Waxler, JASA 112, 2540 (2002)](https://pubs.aip.org/asa/jasa/article-abstract/112/6/2540/544287/A-vertical-eigenfunction-expansion-for-the?redirectedFrom=fulltext)
- Nocturnal duct, ground-to-ground: "On clear nights, over flat land, a sound duct develops … there is strong dispersion"; an impulse arrives as "a series of arrivals", and "a direct connection is drawn between the meteorological and ground conditions and the structure of the propagated signal". **[ABS]** — [Waxler, Gilbert & Talmadge, JASA 124, 2742 (2008)](https://pubs.aip.org/asa/jasa/article-abstract/124/5/2742/910777/A-theoretical-treatment-of-the-long-range?redirectedFrom=fulltext)
- Follow-up: "a temperature inversion and stable nocturnal wind jet lead to an acoustic duct in the lowest few hundred meters"; the received train is "a series of weakly dispersed distinct arrivals followed by a strongly dispersed low-frequency tail"; the leading arrivals "have been previously shown to be well modeled by geometric acoustics", with "travel times, amplitudes, and caustic structures" from the eikonal and transport equations; "a two parameter quadratic approximation to the effective sound speed profile" fits the meteorology. **[ABS]** — [Blom & Waxler, JASA 131, 3680 (2012), doi:10.1121/1.3699174](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.3699174)
- Rasmussen's J. Sound Vib. 104, 321 (1986) paper presents "a very accurate calculation method" for wind and temperature gradients over an impedance surface and an approximate model verified against it, with measurements over grass "for distances up to 80 m". **[SNIPPET]** — [ADS record](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986JSV...104..321R/abstract)
- A ray-tracing uncertainty study for long-range wind-farm noise found the ground-impedance model matters little at long range, and suggests "this is likely because sound waves are significantly attenuated by the ground after reflecting multiple times"; variation in predicted level grew "linearly between 0.5 and 3.5 km at a rate of 0.8 dB/km" with the wind-profile model. **[FULL]** (HTML text) — [arXiv:2205.13695](https://arxiv.org/html/2205.13695)

### Inferences
- **[OWN CALC] Ray count for a linear profile, elevated source, receiver on the ground.** Small angles, c = c0 (1 + z/R). A ray that meets the ground at angle θ0 must satisfy θ0 ≥ θ_m = √(2 h_s / R) to pass through the source. With s = √(θ0² − θ_m²), a ray with n intermediate ground bounces lands at r = R [(2n+1) θ0 ∓ s]. The minus branch has a minimum in θ0, so n-bounce rays are **born in pairs at a caustic** at

  r_n = √(8 n (n+1) R h_s),  i.e. r_1 = 4 √(R h_s).

  (Checked numerically for h_s = 100 m: 4264.9 m both ways.) There is always exactly one zero-bounce arrival; the number of arrivals at range r is 1 + 2 N with N the number of n ≥ 1 satisfying n(n+1) < r² / (8 R h_s). Equivalently a one-bounce pair exists only if tan(elevation) < r / (16 R).

  | h_s | r_1 (elevation) | r_2 | r_3 |
  | --- | --- | --- | --- |
  | 30 m | 2.34 km (0.74°) | 4.05 km | 5.72 km |
  | 50 m | 3.02 km (0.95°) | 5.22 km | 7.39 km |
  | 100 m | 4.26 km (1.34°) | 7.39 km | 10.5 km |
  | 150 m | 5.22 km (1.64°) | 9.05 km | 12.8 km |
  | 300 m | 7.39 km (2.33°) | 12.8 km | 18.1 km |

  The 300 m row is formal: that ray turns at 338 m, above the 150 m inversion top, and would not return.
- **[OWN CALC]** Maximum elevation at which a one-bounce arrival exists under R = 11.4 km: 0.38° at 1.2 km, 0.63° at 2 km, 0.94° at 3 km, 1.57° at 5 km. **So at 2–10° and 1.2–5 km there are no multi-bounce eigenrays for this gradient, and NICE-OPS's one direct plus one reflected arrival is the complete ray set.** The gradient needed to create one: at 2° elevation, a ≥ 0.095 s^-1 at 2 km (3.2 × the mean inversion), 0.063 at 3 km (2.1 ×), 0.038 at 5 km (1.3 ×); at 5° elevation, 0.24, 0.16 and 0.095 s^-1. A surface inversion concentrated in the lowest tens of meters, or a modest downwind shear, reaches the 2°/5 km figure but not the 5° ones.
- This matches Willshire's measurement qualitatively: his broadband bands from a ~100 m source stayed spherical to 1 km (my r_1 for 100 m under a gradient of that order is several km), while the ~40 m low-frequency source departed from spherical between 450 m and 1 km under a much stronger wind gradient.
- **[OWN CALC]** Trapped-mode count for a linear duct 150 m deep with Δc = 4.6 m/s (WKB, (2/3) k H √(2Δc/c0) / π): about 6 modes at 63 Hz, 12 at 125 Hz, 24 at 250 Hz, 61 at 630 Hz. At 63–125 Hz the field is a handful of modes; interference between them, not ray spreading, sets the range decay, and the lowest modes decay at a rate set by the ground admittance. This is the regime where the brief's bands "decay 1–2 dB/km more slowly" than the model, and where Salomons needed an empirical reflection coefficient. NICE-OPS's `downward_pressure` normal-mode series is the natural per-band reference for it.
- The +0.3 to +0.5 dB/km rise of the flush-microphone ground term with range (section 1) is a second, purely single-ray mechanism for slower decay under an inversion, and it is already in the model if the gradient is right. A stronger near-ground gradient than the fitted one increases it.
- Makarewicz's transition to cylindrical spreading rests on *incoherent* summation of bounce rays, which suits a band-averaged SEL. If multi-bounce arrivals are ever added, energy summation with each ray's own |Q|^n and tube area is the published precedent; over soft ground each bounce at a few degrees costs several dB (Raspet 1995; arXiv:2205.13695), so the series converges fast.

The 1996 tutorial's abstract does not contain the criterion: [Embleton, JASA 100, 31 (1996)](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.415879).

## 3. Caustics and the shadow boundary in ray models

### Takeaway
Ray amplitudes are wrong on both sides of a caustic and of a shadow boundary; the published remedies are a uniform (Airy) caustic field with extrapolation onto the shadow side (Salomons 1998) and the residue/creeping-wave series inside the shadow. Even the exact residue series and geometrical theory, which agree with each other to 0.5 dB at the boundary, miss controlled measurements there by 2–5 dB. A frequency-independent +10 dB cap on focusing has no basis in caustic theory, where the peak gain grows as k^(1/6).

### Cited Findings
- "If caustic diffraction fields are ignored, discontinuities of more than 10 dB occur in the sound-pressure field." The model works for "realistic, nonlinear profiles such as the logarithmic profile", where "an ordered series of rays and caustics is found … including caustics with a cusp". "The field on the illuminated side of a caustic is computed by modifying the geometrical-acoustics solution. The field on the shadow side … is computed by extrapolation of various quantities into the shadow region … horizontal extrapolation gives the best results." Accuracy is "demonstrated by comparison with numerical solutions of the one-way wave equation". **[ABS]** — [Salomons, JASA 104, 3259 (1998)](https://pubs.aip.org/asa/jasa/article-abstract/104/6/3259/559061/Caustic-diffraction-fields-in-a-downward?redirectedFrom=fulltext)
- Controlled curved-surface experiment, 0.3–10 kHz, rigid and finite-impedance, "particular attention … to the region across the shadow boundary": "Deep within the shadow, theory and measurements agree to, typically, 0.5 dB. The same agreement is obtained between measurements and the geometrical theory well above the shadow boundary. **In the vicinity of the shadow boundary, both theories agree to within 0.5 dB but differ from the measured results by 2 to 5 dB.**" **[ABS]** — [Berry & Daigle, JASA 83, 2047 (1988)](https://pubs.aip.org/asa/jasa/article-abstract/83/6/2047/683300/Controlled-experiments-of-the-diffraction-of-sound?redirectedFrom=fulltext)
- Pierce's residue-series solution for a linear gradient has approximate forms valid "when the source and receiver are above the creeping wave layer height, defined as (R_e / 2k²)^(1/3)", R_e being the limiting ray's radius of curvature. "The approximate solution leads to large errors in the effective source levels for sources close to a pressure-release or finite impedance ground" (citing Raspet & Franke 1988). **[FULL]** — [McBride, Bass, Raspet & Gilbert, NASA CP (N91-16703)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19910007390/downloads/19910007390.pdf?attachment=true)
- Same paper, on Berry & Daigle's outdoor comparison (grass, source near the ground, receivers 230 m downrange): the full residue series "still agreed well" at 250 Hz, but the data were "well under-predicted by diffraction theory at 500 Hz and especially at 1000 Hz". **[FULL]** — [McBride et al.](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19910007390/downloads/19910007390.pdf?attachment=true)
- "It seems that the use of a simple linear sound speed gradient is a good approximation to the real sound speed profile directly above the ground for the moderate ranges involved in this study [230 m]. At the longer ranges investigated by Gilbert et al. it was necessary to use a logarithmic sound velocity profile to obtain accurate predictions." **[FULL]** — [McBride et al.](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19910007390/downloads/19910007390.pdf?attachment=true)
- A ray model can be carried into the shadow analytically: "Rays are first diffracted from the shadow boundary, which is determined by the geometrical ray paths. The diffracted rays are then scattered by turbulence … Most of the statistics are determined analytically except one element, which is determined empirically from numerical simulations." Agreement with the PE and with published measurements "is good both with and without turbulence, at distance up to 2 km from the shadow boundary". **[ABS]** — [Lam, JASA 125, 1340 (2009), doi:10.1121/1.3076928](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.3076928)
- Ray models retain "potentially problematic high frequency approximations and caustics"; Gaussian beams reduce the caustic problem but "the problem is not completely addressed". **[FULL]** — [arXiv:2205.13695](https://arxiv.org/html/2205.13695)
- An LES-driven comparison shows a real focus: "a strong peak is visible at 1.7 km, which corresponds to the distance where the focused wave hits the ground", for a 142 m source in a turbine wake, reproduced by PE and linearised Euler alike. **[FULL]** — [Colas et al., JASA 154, 1413 (2023); arXiv:2309.09669](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.09669)

### Inferences
- **[RECALLED]** Uniform fold-caustic field (Ludwig 1966; Kravtsov & Orlov), for two rays with amplitudes A1, A2 and phases φ1 < φ2 that merge at the caustic: define (2/3) ζ^(3/2) = (φ2 − φ1)/2; then p ≈ √π e^{i(φ1+φ2)/2 − iπ/4} [ ζ^(1/4) (A1 + A2) Ai(−ζ) − i ζ^(−1/4) (A1 − A2) Ai′(−ζ) ]. It reduces to the two-ray sum with a −π/2 caustic phase for ζ ≫ 1, is finite at ζ = 0, and continues into the shadow with ζ < 0. The sign and phase conventions need checking against a text before use. It needs only quantities a ray solver already has (two amplitudes, two phases), and on the shadow side needs the analytic continuation that Salomons replaces with "horizontal extrapolation".
- **[OWN CALC]** Because Ai peaks at a fixed ζ, the peak gain at a fold caustic relative to the geometric level scales as k^(1/6): +1.0 dB per octave, about 8 dB between 20 Hz and 5 kHz. A flat cap (NICE-OPS's `max_focusing_gain_db` = +10) is therefore too generous at low frequency and possibly too tight at high frequency; the Airy form supplies the frequency dependence for free.
- Why the PE shows no +10 dB caustic just short of the shadow boundary: when the two merging rays differ in phase by less than about 1 rad (ζ ≲ 1) they are not separate arrivals; the uniform solution gives one smooth Airy lobe whose maximum is modest and which joins the shadow decay continuously. In a logarithmic profile the shadow boundary and the caustic of the near-grazing family coincide in this sense, so treating them as two separate corrections (a capped focus, then a tabulated transition) double-counts. A band is "in the caustic" rather than "lit by two rays" whenever k (path difference) ≲ 1, which is most of the low bands.
- **[OWN CALC]** My ray count (section 2) shows each multi-bounce family is *born* at a caustic. If multi-bounce rays are ever added, each new pair must enter through the Airy form or the level will step.
- **[OWN CALC] A physical basis for the per-band shadow onset.** The creeping-wave layer thickness l = (R / 2k²)^(1/3) is the height over which a band averages the profile. In a log profile c = c0 − b ln(1 + z/z0) the local radius is R(z) = c0 (z + z0)/b; solving l = (R(l)/2k²)^(1/3) gives l = √(c0 / 2b) / k, i.e. about 2.1 wavelengths for b = 1 m/s (11 m at 63 Hz, 5.6 m at 125 Hz, 0.7 m at 1 kHz). Letting the limiting ray graze at height l instead of at the ground lengthens the shadow distance (numerical quadrature, b = 1 m/s, z0 = 0.1 m):

  | source height | geometric r_s | 63 Hz | 125 Hz | 250 Hz | 500 Hz | 1 kHz | 2 kHz |
  | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
  | 30 m | 186 m | × 2.28 | × 2.06 | × 1.78 | × 1.54 | × 1.36 | × 1.23 |
  | 100 m | 546 m | × 2.08 | × 1.79 | × 1.57 | × 1.39 | × 1.27 | × 1.17 |
  | 300 m | 1496 m | × 1.80 | × 1.59 | × 1.43 | × 1.30 | × 1.21 | × 1.14 |

  This is a heuristic (the grazing height is set equal to l with no fitted constant), not a derivation. It reproduces the *kind* of law NICE-OPS fitted against the PE — a multiple of r_s between about 1.2 and 2 that falls slowly with frequency — but with a steeper frequency slope (about f^−0.17 at 100 m against the fitted f^−0.08) and a source-height dependence. It suggests the fitted law could be re-expressed as "limiting ray of the profile truncated below c_l × λ", with one constant c_l of order 1–2 to fit.
- Berry & Daigle's 2–5 dB boundary error is for theories that are exact for their model. The brief's fitted transition is already within that band of the PE (0.1–1 dB on a linear lapse); remaining error near the boundary is more likely in the profile than in the diffraction theory.

## 4. Turbulence at grazing: decorrelation of direct and reflected paths, and scattering into shadows

### Takeaway
The HARMONOISE length h_s h_r /(h_s + h_r) and "half the maximum path separation" are the same quantity for straight rays over flat ground, so the choice between them is not the issue. What is: the inertial-range (ρ^(5/3)) coherence factor has no saturation, the turbulence is strongly height-dependent, and for a flush microphone the two paths coincide so there is nothing to decorrelate. Ostashev, Breton & Wilson (2024) is the current theory for exactly the slanted, height-dependent case. In shadows, every source agrees that above a few hundred hertz the level is set by turbulence and depends weakly on range, height and frequency.

### Cited Findings
- Daigle's original result: jet noise over grass out to 1100 m; "Above 300 Hz the sound levels are up to 10 dB higher than predicted by coherent acoustic theory"; fitted with "standard deviations in phase velocity of about 1 in 1000, and a correlation length of about 1 m"; amplitude covariance matters "at long distances", phase–amplitude covariance "is negligible at all distances". **[ABS]** — [Daigle, JASA 65, 45 (1979), doi:10.1121/1.382265](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.382265)
- Saturation is observed: line-of-sight tones, 250–4000 Hz, 2–300 m: phase variance agrees with theory, but "the measured mean square log-amplitude fluctuations are in general substantially smaller than predicted and … show clear evidence of saturation. The distance to saturation is shown to correspond to the longitudinal coherence length." Consequently "both the transverse and longitudinal coherences are essentially a function of the phase variance only." **[ABS]** — [Daigle, Piercy & Embleton, JASA 74, 1505 (1983), doi:10.1121/1.390152](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.390152)
- Clifford & Lataitis give "a rigorous, mathematical treatment" of the mean-square pressure above a smooth boundary that is "energy conserving", takes "an arbitrary refractive-index correlation function" and needs no "ad hoc … arbitrary parameters". **[ABS]** — [Clifford & Lataitis, JASA 73, 1545 (1983), doi:10.1121/1.389416](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.389416)
- Ostashev et al.: the mean-square pressure contains a coherence factor which "is equal to the normalized coherence function of a spherical sound wave for line-of-sight propagation"; analytical forms follow for "the Kolmogorov, Gaussian, and von Karman spectra of temperature and wind velocity fluctuations"; the von Kármán temperature result "agrees well with experimental data obtained in a laboratory experiment". **[ABS]** — [Ostashev, Salomons, Clifford, Lataitis, Wilson, Blanc-Benon & Juvé, JASA 109, 1894 (2001), doi:10.1121/1.1356022](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1356022)
- Salomons et al.: a Rytov solution for the half-space that "takes into account the ground reflection of scattered waves, which has been neglected in previous work"; rigorous only for rigid ground but "a good approximation for a finite-impedance ground"; checked against a turbulent PE; limited to a non-refracting atmosphere and hence "relatively small distances", but intended "as a basis for a generalized solution for a downward refracting atmosphere". **[ABS]** — [Salomons, Ostashev, Clifford & Lataitis, JASA 109, 1881 (2001)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11386543/)
- The 2024 generalization covers the brief's geometry directly: the theory "is extended for statistically quasi-homogeneous turbulence in which the variances and length scales of the temperature and wind velocity fluctuations depend on the height above the ground", and, where it "was previously formulated only for near-horizontal sound propagation, is generalized to slanted sound propagation as pertinent to elevated sound sources. Numerical results for slanted propagation demonstrate that atmospheric turbulence can significantly increase the sound pressure level at the interference minima." It also treats attenuation of both waves by backscattering. **[ABS]** — [Ostashev, Breton & Wilson, JASA 156, 107 (2024)](https://pubs.aip.org/asa/jasa/article/156/1/107/3302105/Interference-of-the-direct-and-ground-reflected)
- Slanted-path coherence, as implemented for an aircraft at 180 m over ground microphones (their Eq. 5): C_coh(L, r_d) = exp{ −(π² k0² / cos θ) ∫_0^h dz ∫_0^∞ Φ_eff(z, κ) [1 − J0(κ z r_d / h)] κ dκ }, L = h / cos θ, the separation growing linearly along the path. **[FULL]** — [Lincke, Schumacher & Pieren, JASA 153, 456 (2023)](https://elib.dlr.de/194540/2/10.0016847.pdf)
- Same paper, the height-dependent von Kármán inputs (their Eqs. 7–8, after Ostashev & Wilson 2016 and Kamrath et al. 2021): σ_T² = 4 T*² / [1 − 10 z/L_o]^(2/3); σ_v,s² = 3 u*²; σ_v,b² = 0.35 w*²; L_T(z) = 2 z (1 − 7 z/L_o)/(1 − 10 z/L_o); L_v,s(z) = 1.8 z; L_v,b = 0.23 z_i, with T* = −Q_H /(ρ0 c_p u*), w* = (z_i g Q_H / ρ0 c_p T0)^(1/3). The three terms of Φ_eff are temperature (weight 1/4T0², exponent 11/6) and shear- and buoyancy-produced velocity (weight 22/3c0², exponent 17/6). They find coherence loss is "primarily caused by buoyancy-" or shear-produced velocity fluctuations depending on the case. **[FULL]** — [Lincke et al. 2023](https://elib.dlr.de/194540/2/10.0016847.pdf)
- Validation on slanted paths: with "turbulence models incorporating shear and buoyancy instabilities" the theory "correctly predict[s] the measured spatial coherence, which is primarily affected by small-scale isotropic turbulence", but underpredicts cross-frequency and long-time temporal coherence, "which are affected by large-scale anisotropic buoyancy-driven velocity fluctuations". Measured distributions agree "for different regimes ranging from unsaturated to fully saturated scattering". **[ABS]** — [Ostashev, Kamrath, Wilson, White, Hart & Finn, JASA 150, 3109 (2021)](https://pubs.aip.org/asa/jasa/article-abstract/150/4/3109/997212/Vertical-and-slanted-sound-propagation-in-the-near?redirectedFrom=fulltext)
- A general moment theory (Markov approximation, PE) exists for "line-of-sight propagation, multipath propagation in a refractive atmosphere above an impedance ground, and sound scattering into a refractive shadow zone". **[ABS]** — [Ostashev, Wilson, Vecherin & Collier, JASA 136, 2414 (2014)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25373944/)
- Harmonoise has "two separate 'turbulence' effects … controlled by the same 'turbulence strength' parameter C": loss of coherence between direct and reflected sound, and scattering into shielded regions, which "also limits the reduction under negative sound speed gradients". "At 1 kHz and 1000 m, the effective limitation on barrier attenuation is 35 + 10 log(C). The Harmonoise literature suggests a typical value for C would be 5 × 10^-6"; the author used 10^-5 for better agreement with measurements. **[FULL]** — [AAS 2012, "The Harmonoise noise prediction algorithm"](https://www.acoustics.asn.au/conference_proceedings/AAS2012/papers/p43.pdf)
- Shadow zones, PE with turbulence, against Wiener & Keast: "the relative sound-pressure level versus range follows a characteristic 'step' function … In the geometric shadow region, the observed strong dependence on refraction strength and weak dependence on range, height, and frequency are also predicted … for a receiver deep in a shadow zone and frequencies greater than a few hundred hertz, the measured sound-pressure level is due almost entirely to sound scattered into the shadow zone by atmospheric turbulence." No adjustable parameters. **[ABS]** — [Gilbert, Raspet & Di, JASA 87, 2428 (1990)](https://pubs.aip.org/asa/jasa/article-abstract/87/6/2428/893181/Calculation-of-turbulence-effects-in-an-upward?redirectedFrom=fulltext)
- Single-scattering ("turbule") simulation against Daigle et al.'s shadow data at 230 m, with measured ⟨μ²⟩ = 6 × 10^-6 and L = 1.6 m: "the contributions from turbulent scattering and diffractive effects are about equal at 250 Hz … turbulent scattering becomes the major contributor at 500 Hz. At 1000 Hz, turbulent scattering is the predominant mechanism". The Born approximation is required in the shadow (the Rytov form fails where the unscattered field is zero) and is valid for wave parameter D = R / kL² > 1. About 15 % of scatterers were excluded because curved rays could not join them to source or receiver. Four single-scatter paths exist over ground (with and without a bounce before and after). **[FULL]** — [McBride et al.](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19910007390/downloads/19910007390.pdf?attachment=true)
- With flux–profile similarity supplying both the refraction and the turbulence, shadow levels "decrease with the adverse wind, as a consequence of enhanced mean upward refraction. Conversely, they increase with the absolute value of the surface heat flux, as a consequence of enhanced turbulence scattering." **[ABS]** — [Cheinet, JASA 131, 1946 (2012)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22423692/)
- Elevated source (wind turbine), PE with and without turbulence: "atmospheric turbulence has a negligible effect on SPL up to 800 m from the wind turbine, even under upwind conditions" (A-weighted, case-specific). **[ABS]** — [Kayser, Écotière & Gauvreau, Acta Acustica 7, 63 (2023), doi:10.1051/aacus/2023051](https://doi.org/10.1051/aacus/2023051)
- Turbulent scattering "greatly increases sound levels in the refractive shadow zones" in high-wind neutral and clear-daytime cases; it "is weak for the low-wind, clear nighttime case … (However, gravity waves may form in such conditions and significantly scatter sound; modeling of this phenomenon remains a challenge.)" **[FULL]** — [Wilson, Pettit & Ostashev, Acoustics Today 11(2), 44 (2015)](https://acousticstoday.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/06/Sound-Propagation-in-the-Atmospheric-Boundary-Layer-D.-Keith-Wilson-Chris-L.-Pettit-and-Vladimir-E.-Ostashev.pdf)
- Downwind, turbulence effects on the received spectrum "are rather small". **[ABS]** — [Salomons 1994](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.409852)
- In an FAA/Volpe exercise of Nord2000 for aircraft, "strong turbulence" changed the result by 0.1–0.2 dB in the cases run, though "a brief proof of concept investigation into turbulence effects over longer range propagation where grazing angles of the ground-reflected ray are very shallow indicated that turbulence could have a significant effect". **[FULL]** (text searched, not read end to end) — [Ahearn et al., DOT/FAA/AEE/2012-5](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/9687/dot_9687_DS1.pdf)

### Inferences
- **[OWN CALC]** For straight rays over flat ground the direct ray stands 2 h_s h_r /(h_s + h_r) above the specular point, which is the maximum separation of the two paths. Half of it, h_s h_r /(h_s + h_r), is the HARMONOISE γ_p and is also the path-mean separation of the two triangles. The brief's two candidate forms are therefore identical in this geometry. For h_s ≫ h_r it tends to h_r: 1.19 m for a 100 m source and a 1.2 m microphone, whatever the range.
- **[OWN CALC]** For a flush microphone γ_p = 0 and the coherence factor is exactly 1: direct and reflected sound are one path. Turbulence then cannot "fill in" a ground dip, and none exists; the only turbulence effects left for plates are amplitude scintillation (which does not change the mean square to first order), backscatter extinction (Ostashev 2024's first item), and scattering into shadows.
- **[OWN CALC]** Under downward refraction the curved-ground picture raises both effective heights by d²/2R (d the distance from the specular point), so γ_p grows for a 1.2 m microphone but stays zero for a flush one.
- **[RECALLED]** The inertial-range form the brief quotes, Γ = exp[−(3/8) B k² γ_p^(5/3) R γ_T] with B = 0.364 and γ_T = C_T²/T0² + (22/3) C_v²/c0², is the Kolmogorov limit of the spherical-wave coherence; the 3/8 is ∫_0^1 η^(5/3) dη for a separation that grows linearly along the path. It is valid only for separations well inside the outer scale, and has **no saturation**: it tends to zero as k² R grows. Daigle's Gaussian-spectrum form does saturate: T = exp[−σ² (1 − ρ)], σ² = A √π ⟨μ²⟩ k² R L, A = 0.5 for R > kL² and 1 otherwise, ρ = (√π/2)(L/h) erf(h/L), h the maximum separation; its floor is exp(−σ²). A von Kármán form interpolates between the two. Constants should be checked against Salomons (2001, app. I) or Ostashev & Wilson (2015, ch. 7).
- **[OWN CALC]** Magnitudes from the quoted formula, h_s = 100 m, h_r = 1.2 m, R = 3 km: with γ_T = 5 × 10^-6 (Harmonoise's "typical"), Γ = 0.94 at 250 Hz, 0.79 at 500 Hz, 0.40 at 1 kHz, 0.02 at 2 kHz; with γ_T = 5 × 10^-7, Γ = 0.99, 0.98, 0.91, 0.69. So the ground dip of a 1.2 m microphone is erased above about 1 kHz in moderate turbulence and survives to 2 kHz on a calm stable morning.
- **[RECALLED + OWN CALC]** Near-neutral surface-layer scaling C_v² ≈ 2 ε^(2/3) with ε = u*³/(κ z) gives C_v² ≈ 3.7 u*² z^(−2/3); C_T² ≈ 4.9 T*² z^(−2/3) (Wyngaard et al.). Then (22/3) C_v²/c0² = 4.5 × 10^-6 at z = 10 m for u* = 0.3 m/s (matching Harmonoise's 5 × 10^-6), 5 × 10^-7 for u* = 0.1 m/s, 1.3 × 10^-7 for u* = 0.05 m/s; values at z = 1 m are 4.6 times larger. A single γ_T cannot represent a path from 100 m to the ground: the last few meters dominate, and that is where the two paths are separated. This is the argument for the height-integrated form (Lincke Eq. 5, Ostashev 2024) over a constant γ_T.
- The turbulent length scales in the Lincke/Ostashev model are proportional to height (L_T = 2z, L_v,s = 1.8z near neutral). Near a 1.2 m microphone the local outer scale is 2–3 m, comparable to the 1.2–2.4 m path separation. The inertial-range ρ^(5/3) law is therefore being used at or beyond its limit exactly where it matters, which argues for the von Kármán form.
- For the brief's calm inversion day: u* is small, turbulence is weak (Wilson), downwind effects are small (Salomons 1994), and the microphones showing the deficit include flush ones. Turbulent decorrelation is an unlikely source of a +2 dB correction there.
- NICE-OPS's finding that γ_T = 3 × 10^-5 over-fills shadows while ≤ 3 × 10^-6 fits the measured SEL is consistent with the Harmonoise default (5 × 10^-6), with the u* scaling above, and with McBride's use of measured ⟨μ²⟩ = 6 × 10^-6.

## 5. Effective-sound-speed approximation: errors and the exact ray invariant

### Takeaway
The ESSA's error is a phase error that accumulates with range and grows with propagation angle and Mach number. Published comparisons put it at several dB for single-frequency point-source fields and under 1 dB for broadband extended sources, small near the ground and larger aloft. For a ray model at 0–10° elevation the error in the Snell invariant is at most 1.5 % of the wind term, so ESSA is not a credible source of a 2 dB bias here; replacing it is nonetheless cheap.

### Cited Findings
- "The narrow-angle parabolic equation (NAPE) with the effective sound speed approximation (ESSA) … is valid only for angles less than 20° with respect to the nominal propagation direction." "The equations for low Mach numbers and the WAPE with the ESSA are applicable only under rather restrictive conditions on the medium velocity." An ESSA-free wide-angle PE in Padé (1,1) form is given. **[ABS]** — [Ostashev, Wilson & Muhlestein, JASA 147, 3969 (2020), doi:10.1121/10.0001397](https://doi.org/10.1121/10.0001397)
- In the phase-conserving formulation "c_eff = c + v_x is the effective sound speed which is introduced as a convenient notation rather than an approximation" (their Eq. 4); the Padé (1,1) WAPE is valid to about 35° and the NAPE to about 20°; existing Crank–Nicolson codes need "minimal modifications" (coefficients in their Eqs. 8–10). "Although the variations in sound speed and Mach number are often relatively small, omitting second-order small terms … can result in large cumulative phase errors for long propagation ranges." The demonstration is deliberately extreme: source at 50 m, receiver at 2 m, 100 Hz, uniform Mach 0.5, where the ESSA WAPE "significantly deviate[s]" from the analytical solution. **[FULL]** — [Ostashev, Colas, Dragna & Wilson, Forum Acusticum 2023](https://dael.euracoustics.org/confs/fa2023/data/articles/000077.pdf)
- For wind-turbine geometry: "We highlight large phase error (several dB) on monochromatic calculations with a point source. Using an extended sound source representative of a wind turbine, we show small phase error (<1 dB) in a wind turbine noise context where sound level variability far from the source is of several dB. The validity of previous works using WAPE with ESSA is, thus, not questioned". **[ABS]** — [Kayser, Mascarenhas, Cotté, Écotière & Gauvreau, JASA 153, 1846 (2023), doi:10.1121/10.0017653](https://doi.org/10.1121/10.0017653)
- Against linearised Euler, neutral boundary layer with 10 m/s at a 100 m hub, flat ground: for a source at 142 m and a receiver at 2 m, the ESSA PE, the vector-wind PE and the LEE differ by no more than 0.5 dB beyond 1 km at 100 Hz; at 512 and 1000 Hz "a small shift is found between the position of the peaks" for the ESSA PE. Averaged over 35 frequencies and the whole vertical plane, adding the mean flow "greatly increases the error" of the ESSA PE, "with differences going above 3 dB", although "close to the ground, the error is still relatively small". With flat ground that error "is only due to the effective sound speed approach". **[FULL]** — [Colas et al., JASA 154, 1413 (2023); arXiv:2309.09669](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.09669)
- Same paper: for the rotating broadband source the ESSA's phase errors have "a minor" effect on overall SPL, and a moving-source amplitude correction at Mach 0.03 reaches "around 1.5 dB at 1 kHz". **[FULL]** — [Colas et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.09669)
- Godin reduces the wave equation and boundary conditions of a slowly moving inhomogeneous fluid to those of "a quiescent fluid with effective sound speed **and density**"; "introduction of effective density in conjunction with effective sound speed is essential to ensure accurate reproduction of acoustic pressure amplitude"; the effective parameters "depend … on sound propagation direction". **[ABS]** — [Godin, JASA 112, 1269 (2002), doi:10.1121/1.1504853](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1504853)
- Li, Taherzadeh & Attenborough's ray tracer already uses "a generalized Snell's law" for vector wind. **[ABS]** — [Li et al. 1998](https://pubs.aip.org/asa/jasa/article-abstract/104/4/2077/559801/An-improved-ray-tracing-algorithm-for-predicting?redirectedFrom=fulltext)

### Inferences
- **[RECALLED]** Exact invariant for a stratified moving medium (Pierce §8; Ostashev & Wilson ch. 3): with θ the elevation of the wavefront normal and u(z) the wind component along the horizontal projection of the normal, the horizontal trace velocity c(z)/cos θ(z) + u(z) is constant along a ray. ESSA instead conserves (c + u)/cos θ. The ray (group) direction differs from the normal: tan ψ = c sin θ / (c cos θ + u). Travel time uses the group speed along the ray.
- **[OWN CALC]** The two invariants differ by u (1/cos θ − 1): 0.06 % of u at 2°, 0.4 % at 5°, 1.5 % at 10°, 6.4 % at 20°, 15 % at 30°. With 10 m/s of wind difference that is a 0.15 m/s error in effective speed at 10°, negligible against profile uncertainty. ESSA matters for NICE-OPS only on the steep (> 20–30°) part of a flyover, where refraction itself is weak.
- Implementing the exact invariant in the layered solver costs one change: parametrize by p with 1/p = c/cos θ + u, so that within a layer cos θ = p c /(1 − p u). The closed-form layer integrals change, so the existing RK4 checks would need the moving-medium ray equations as reference.
- Godin's effective density is an amplitude correction of order Mach number; at M ≈ 0.03 it is a few tenths of a dB at most and direction-dependent. Colas's 1.5 dB at 1 kHz is a *source* convective effect, not a propagation one.

- The body of Ostashev et al. 2020 (the explicit validity conditions on medium velocity, and the phase-error expressions) was not accessible.
- I found nothing on ESSA error for ray models specifically, or for a helicopter-height source at 0–10°; the estimates above are my own.
- Godin's validity conditions and effective-density formula are in the paper body.

## 6. Near-surface profile representation and sensitivity

### Takeaway
Engineering practice represents the near-ground effective sound speed as c0 + A z + B ln(1 + z/z0) with coefficients from Monin–Obukhov similarity, and then collapses it to one linear gradient; that last step is where the documented 5–15 dB errors arise. Similarity theory fails in exactly the brief's case — a strong, calm surface inversion — so a surface-layer fit below a lidar's 40 m floor is least trustworthy when it matters most. Mean profiles give mean levels to about 2 dB rms except in shadows and interference minima.

### Cited Findings
- Wilson models "the effective sound-speed gradient with Monin–Obukhov similarity theory"; the gradient depends on "just three nondimensional variables": (i) "the ratio of a sound-speed scale (representing the strength of the turbulent fluctuations in the sound speed) to the friction velocity"; (ii) "the ratio of the actual height to a transitional height where contributions from the near-ground wind-speed gradients and the adiabatic lapse rate are roughly balanced"; (iii) "the cosine of the angle between the propagation direction and mean wind direction". "When the magnitude of the sound-speed scale/friction velocity ratio is large, refraction is unconditionally upward or downward … A small value … indicates nearly neutral atmospheric stratification, for which refraction is determined by the wind direction for small values of the nondimensional height and is upward for larger values." Humidity's contribution, via the Bowen ratio, is "significant over wet surfaces". **[ABS]** — [Wilson, JASA 113, 750 (2003)](https://pubs.aip.org/asa/jasa/article/113/2/750/545189/The-sound-speed-gradient-and-refraction-in-the)
- Limits of similarity theory: "MOST cannot be used when turbulent mixing is suppressed, as happens in strong inversion conditions." For clear nights, "because stable stratification suppresses turbulence, conventional turbulence similarity theories such as MOST no longer apply". **[FULL]** — [Wilson, Pettit & Ostashev 2015](https://acousticstoday.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/06/Sound-Propagation-in-the-Atmospheric-Boundary-Layer-D.-Keith-Wilson-Chris-L.-Pettit-and-Vladimir-E.-Ostashev.pdf)
- The same point from a practitioner: "Monin-Obukhov theory breaks down in the case which is generally most important for sound propagation – a stable night-time atmosphere with very low wind. Hence the Harmonoise program incorporates tables that provide estimates of the dimensionless parameters under various conditions, but which do not necessarily accord with the underlying theory … constructed so that the parameter values give values of A and B that are in accord with profile measurements … made in Europe". **[FULL]** — [AAS 2012, "The Harmonoise noise prediction algorithm"](https://www.acoustics.asn.au/conference_proceedings/AAS2012/papers/p43.pdf)
- Predictability with good profiles (CASES-99: stable, weakly turbulent, flat homogeneous ground, 60 m tower at 5 m resolution, microphones at 361–1180 m, 150 Hz): "deep fading episodes exceeding 20 dB are present … sound levels can change by this amount in a matter of minutes … the timing and amplitude of the signal variations … are not accurately predicted in a deterministic sense." **[FULL]** — [Wilson et al. 2015](https://acousticstoday.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/06/Sound-Propagation-in-the-Atmospheric-Boundary-Layer-D.-Keith-Wilson-Chris-L.-Pettit-and-Vladimir-E.-Ostashev.pdf)
- Which profile to use: "Mean vertical profiles produce the most accurate predictions of both mean and event sound levels. Predictions of mean sound levels from mean vertical profiles have relatively low root-mean-square (rms) errors, typically less than 2 dB, except in shadow zones and interference minima … Event propagation is highly random and predictions have rms errors of 8-10 dB, even when they are based on local vertical profile data synchronized to the time of the propagation event." Averaging runs over instantaneous profiles overpredicts in upward refraction (the "plywood atmosphere"). **[FULL]** — [Wilson et al. 2015](https://acousticstoday.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/06/Sound-Propagation-in-the-Atmospheric-Boundary-Layer-D.-Keith-Wilson-Chris-L.-Pettit-and-Vladimir-E.-Ostashev.pdf)
- Cost of linearising: at 300 m, PE results for c0 + b ln(1 + z/z0) (b = 1 m/s, z0 = 0.1 m) and for its linearisation (a = 0.177 s^-1) differ by 5–15 dB at 500–2000 Hz over absorbing ground. **[FULL]** — [van Maercke & Salomons 2009](https://pub.dega-akustik.de/NAG_DAGA_2009/data/articles/000250.pdf)
- Harmonoise's meteorological classes: stability S1 (day, 0–2/8 cloud) to S5 (night, 0–4/8 cloud), with S3 neutral; wind W1 0–1, W2 1–3, W3 3–6, W4 6–10, W5 > 10 m/s; "default values are provided for 25 combinations". **[SNIPPET]** — [Salomons et al., "The Harmonoise sound propagation model" (ResearchGate record)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/233712338_The_Harmonoise_Sound_Propagation_Model)
- Range dependence matters too: a realistic range-dependent wind profile near a 6 m barrier gave "considerably larger levels" than either range-independent profile. **[FULL]** — [van Maercke & Salomons 2009](https://pub.dega-akustik.de/NAG_DAGA_2009/data/articles/000250.pdf)
- A least-squares linear fit to a power-law wind profile V(h)(z/h)^α over 0–h has slope a·V(h)/h with a = 6α / ((1 + α)(2 + α)) (their Eq. 7); quoted shear exponents are 0.15–0.3 (urban) and 0.07–0.55 (rural). **[FULL]** — [Makarewicz, arXiv:1306.2753](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1306.2753)
- The nocturnal duct is often a jet plus an inversion, and a two-parameter quadratic c_eff(z) fitted acoustically agreed with the measured profile. **[ABS]** — [Blom & Waxler 2012](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.3699174)
- Wind-profile model choice alone produced a spread in predicted level growing at 0.8 dB/km between 0.5 and 3.5 km. **[FULL]** — [arXiv:2205.13695](https://arxiv.org/html/2205.13695)

### Inferences
- **[RECALLED]** Similarity profiles: u(z) = (u*/κ)[ln(z/z0) − ψ_m(z/L)], θ(z) = θ0 + (θ*/κ)[ln(z/z_0h) − ψ_h(z/L)]; stable: ψ_m = ψ_h = −5 z/L; unstable (Businger–Dyer): x = (1 − 16 z/L)^(1/4), ψ_m = 2 ln((1+x)/2) + ln((1+x²)/2) − 2 atan x + π/2, ψ_h = 2 ln((1+x²)/2). The effective gradient in the stable case is then dc_eff/dz ≈ (1/κz)(1 + 5z/L)[u* cos φ + (c0/2T0) θ*] − (c0/2T0) Γ_d, i.e. a log term plus a linear term of weight 5/L: this is the origin of the lin-log form, with B ∝ (u* cos φ + (c0/2T0)θ*)/κ and A ∝ 5B/L − (c0/2T0)Γ_d. Constants vary between authors (4.7 or 5; turbulent Prandtl number 0.74–1).
- **[OWN CALC]** The brief's inversion has mean gradient 0.030 s^-1. If it follows a stable similarity shape, the gradient at 2 m is far larger than at 100 m (the log term goes as 1/z), so the 150 m mean under-states the gradient in the layer that fixes the ground angle and the shadow/duct behavior. Section 1's table shows the consequence is under 1 dB for the flush-microphone ground term; section 2 shows it is decisive for whether multi-bounce rays exist at 1–2°.
- What the lowest 1–10 m controls, per band: the band senses the profile averaged over roughly l = (R/2k²)^(1/3) (section 3): 16 m at 63 Hz, 6 m at 250 Hz, 2.6 m at 1 kHz for R = 11.4 km **[OWN CALC]**. So the missing layer below the lidar's 40 m matters at all audio frequencies, and structure in the lowest meter matters only above about 1 kHz. A similarity fit needs to be right on the 2–15 m scale, not at z0.
- Practical blending suggested by the above (inference, not from a source): (1) fit u*, θ* and L to the surface temperature or 2 m temperature, the lowest lidar winds (40–80 m) and the sonde; (2) use the log-linear form only below about z = L (or below the first lidar gate, whichever is lower) and blend linearly to the measured profile above; (3) when z/L > 1 at the lowest lidar gate — the strong-inversion case where MOST is invalid — abandon the fit and interpolate temperature linearly to the measured surface value, carrying the uncertainty as an ensemble of near-surface gradients; (4) project wind on the propagation bearing before fitting, since B changes sign with direction; (5) score the ensemble, not one profile, against the measured SEL, since Wilson's 2 dB rms is for mean profiles and 8–10 dB for single events.
- A helicopter pass lasts tens of seconds. It is an "event" in Wilson's sense, so part of any 2 dB single-day residual is within the irreducible 8–10 dB event scatter unless it persists across runs.
