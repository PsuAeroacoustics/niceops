## 1. SAE AIR 1751 and AIR 5662 lateral attenuation: formulas, data, component split, cut-off angle, and the ~10.9 dB asymptote against an impedance-ground model

### Takeaway
AIR 5662 (2006) is Λ = E_Eng(φ) − g(ℓ)·A_GRS(β)/10.86, with A_GRS = 1.137 − 0.0229β + 9.72·exp(−0.142β) dB for β ≤ 50° (zero above) and g(ℓ) = 11.83[1 − exp(−0.00274ℓ)] saturating at 10.86 dB beyond 914 m; AIR 1751 (1981) was the same shape with a 13.86 dB asymptote and a 60° cut-off. Both are fits to A-weighted/EPNL event levels at 1.2 m poles over grass, lump ground interference with refraction and scattering, and sit well above what a coherent impedance-ground calculation gives except very near grazing; the underlying data scatter by of order ±10 dB below about 10–15°.

### Cited Findings
- [FT] AIR 5662 as reproduced by Berton (Appendix B). Engine installation: underwing jets E = 10·log10[(0.0039cos²φ + sin²φ)^0.062 / (0.8786 sin²2φ + cos²2φ)]; fuselage-mounted jets E = 10·log10[(0.1225cos²φ + sin²φ)^0.329]; propeller aircraft E = 0 (φ = depression angle). Ground plus refraction-scattering: A_GRS = 1.137 − 0.0229β + 9.72 e^(−0.142β) for 0 ≤ β ≤ 50°, zero for 50° < β ≤ 90°. Over-ground factor: g = 11.83[1 − e^(−0.00274ℓ)] for ℓ ≤ 914 m, 10.86 for ℓ > 914 m. Total Λ = E_Eng − g·A_GRS/10.86. — [Berton 2021, NASA/AIAA](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210011612/downloads/AIAA-Aviation2021-Berton-Manuscript-FINAL.pdf)
- [FT] ECAC Doc 29 (4th ed., Vol. 2) uses the same function: Γ(ℓ) = 1.089[1 − exp(−0.00274ℓ)] to 914 m, 1 beyond; "long-range air-to-ground lateral attenuation" Λ(β) = 1.137 − 0.0229β + 9.72 exp(−0.142β) for 0–50°, zero above; when terrain makes β negative, Λ = 10.857 is recommended. It is "assumed to hold true for all aircraft, i.e. propeller aircraft as well as fuselage-mounted and wing-mounted jets". — [ECAC Doc 29 Vol. 2](https://www.ecac-ceac.org/images/documents/ECAC-Doc_29_4th_edition_Dec_2016_Volume_2.pdf)
- [FT] Doc 29 on the data: the method "is built on the substantial body of experimental data on sound propagation from aircraft with fuselage-mounted engines in straight (non-turning), steady, level flight reported originally in AIR-1751"; the total lateral adjustment was fitted and the fuselage-mount lateral directivity then subtracted to isolate the propagation part. It is "confined to the case of sound propagation over soft level ground". — [ECAC Doc 29 Vol. 2](https://www.ecac-ceac.org/images/documents/ECAC-Doc_29_4th_edition_Dec_2016_Volume_2.pdf)
- [FT] Doc 29 on the physics: lateral attenuation "is a reflection effect... It is also very strongly affected by sound refraction, steady and unsteady, caused by wind and temperature gradients and turbulence"; atmospheric and surface non-uniformities "have a profound effect on the reflection effect, tending to 'spread' it to higher elevation angles; thus the theory is of limited applicability". — [ECAC Doc 29 Vol. 2](https://www.ecac-ceac.org/images/documents/ECAC-Doc_29_4th_edition_Dec_2016_Volume_2.pdf)
- [FT] AIR 1751 as reproduced by Plotkin et al.: ground-to-ground G(d) = 15.09(1 − e^(−0.00274d)) dB for d < 914 m, 13.86 dB beyond, "based on the work of Parkin and Scholes" (Radlett and Hatfield jet-engine ground runs); air-to-ground A(β) = 3.96 − 0.066β + 9.90 e^(−0.13β) dB for 0–60°, zero for 60–90°; transition A(β,d) = G(d)·A(β)/13.86. "The relations are considered to include ground effects, atmospheric refraction, and aircraft shielding." — [Plotkin, Hobbs & Bradley, NASA/CR-2000-210111](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20000057531/downloads/20000057531.pdf)
- [FT] AIR 1751's data base: "compiled in the 1960s and 1970s", mostly low-bypass jets, and "a single type of jet aircraft, the older Boeing Model 727-100 dominated the data set"; the single relationship was nonetheless "applied equally to the entire fleet". — [Fleming et al., Volpe, "Ground Effects in FAA's Integrated Noise Model"](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/9996/dot_9996_DS1.pdf)
- [FT] How the AIR 5662 data were reduced: centerline and lateral microphone pairs on 1.2 m poles; for each flyover the maximum level under the flight path was subtracted from the lateral level, spreading and absorption removed, "with the remainder deemed lateral attenuation"; fitted on A-weighted SEL and EPNL. AIR 5662 is "for flat, soft ground and average meteorological conditions". — [Berton 2021](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210011612/downloads/AIAA-Aviation2021-Berton-Manuscript-FINAL.pdf)
- [FT] The USAF NOISEMAP (NMAP) model, from constant-altitude flight tests, is a function of elevation only: constant 10.06 dB below 2°, a 1/β decay above, zero above 45°; "no correlation was found between lateral attenuation and different aircraft types" in its data, so it is treated as pure ground effect. — [Plotkin et al. 2000](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20000057531/downloads/20000057531.pdf)
- [FT] Model-against-model: "the SAE model generally predicts attenuation greater than or (at small angles) comparable to the NMAP model, while ray theory is smaller than both, except at near-grazing angles" (Fig. 1, 1000 and 2000 ft, 4 ft receiver, EGA theory without refraction). — [Plotkin et al. 2000](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20000057531/downloads/20000057531.pdf)
- [FT] Berton's summary of the state of knowledge: "Physics-based solutions of ground reflections tend to agree fairly well with measurements for near-grazing elevation angles under about five degrees. But for higher elevation angles up to about 50 degrees, refraction and scattering caused by wind and varying meteorological conditions tend to dominate lateral attenuation." And: "Lateral attenuation is generally underestimated by these theoretical methods, particularly for elevation angles above grazing incidence." — [Berton 2021](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210011612/downloads/AIAA-Aviation2021-Berton-Manuscript-FINAL.pdf)
- [FT] Double counting: AIR 5662 already contains the ground reflection at the 1.2 m lateral and centerline microphones, so adding a Chien–Soroka term double-counts. Berton's reconciliation is Λ′ = Λ + 10·log10(G_lat/G_cl). The correction term converges with distance: it "changes by no more than 0.1 dB at any point as the separation distance increases beyond 450 m". Spectral distortion from applying it stays under about 1 dB in A-weighted and PNL metrics "until elevation angles fall below about 20 degrees", below which "errors grow to perhaps unacceptable levels". For a flush microphone on a hard surface G_lat = G_cl = 4 and Λ′ = Λ. — [Berton 2021](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210011612/downloads/AIAA-Aviation2021-Berton-Manuscript-FINAL.pdf)
- [FT] Scatter in the underlying kind of data: in the T-38A experiment "a 20 decibel spread is seen in the data for small elevation angles" in EPNL lateral attenuation; slant ranges 180–1530 m. — [Willshire, NASA TP-1747](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19810006274/downloads/19810006274.pdf)
- [FT] Scatter at Denver: "There is large variability between measured and predicted lateral attenuation at small angles... Substantial variations in levels for single events can occur at nominal elevation angles up to 10 to 15 degrees." The scatter plots are drawn on axes from about −15 to +20 dB. — [Plotkin et al. 2000](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20000057531/downloads/20000057531.pdf)
- [SEC] The September 2000 NASA/Volpe Wallops test (Boeing 767-400, DC-9, Falcon 2000, King Air, 20-microphone array) was run to determine whether AIR 1751 "methods are applicable for predicting the engine installation component of lateral attenuation for airplanes with wing-mounted engines"; it fed the AIR 5662 engine-installation terms. — [Volpe/NASA, "Measured Engine Installation Effects of Four Civil Transport Airplanes"](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/9518)
- [FT] Krebs & Thomann confirm the lineage: Logan and Wallops measurements showed lateral directivity depends on engine configuration, prompting the SAE A-21 revision into AIR 5662, which Doc 29 3rd edition adopted; their own 3-D source model agreed with the fuselage-mount correction and agreed poorly for wing-mounted engines. — [Krebs & Thomann, Acta Acustica 95 (2009)](https://dael.euracoustics.org/bin/EAA/aaua_dl?document_id=64884)

### Inferences
- [CALC] Evaluating the formulas above at long range (ℓ > 914 m), with no engine term:

  | β (deg) | AIR 5662 A_GRS (dB) | AIR 1751 A (dB) |
  | --- | --- | --- |
  | 0 | 10.86 | 13.86 |
  | 2 | 8.41 | 11.46 |
  | 5 | 5.80 | 8.80 |
  | 10 | 3.26 | 6.00 |
  | 15 | 1.95 | 4.38 |
  | 20 | 1.25 | 3.38 |
  | 30 | 0.59 | 2.18 |

  So the 2006 revision removed about 3 dB at every angle below 15°. The 5662 function does not vanish at 50° by construction of its terms; it happens to pass through ≈0 there (1.137 − 1.145 + 0.008).
- [CALC] A coherent spherical-wave (Weyl–van der Pol, Delany–Bazley σ = 200 kPa·s/m², 1.2 m receiver, third-octave averaged, no turbulence, no refraction) level relative to free field, ground distance 1000 m:

  | β (deg) | 63 Hz | 125 Hz | 250 Hz | 500 Hz | 1 kHz | 2 kHz |
  | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
  | 0.5 | +3.5 | −5.6 | −16.3 | −17.6 | −9.1 | −2.1 |
  | 1 | +2.9 | −4.2 | −10.7 | −12.3 | −4.0 | +2.5 |
  | 2 | +2.9 | −1.0 | −6.0 | −7.4 | +0.8 | +5.1 |
  | 5 | +4.1 | +2.1 | −2.4 | −2.5 | +3.7 | −0.4 |
  | 10 | +4.6 | +2.6 | −4.6 | +1.6 | −3.2 | +1.3 |
  | 15 | +4.6 | +1.6 | −8.5 | +3.7 | +2.5 | +0.8 |

  Referenced to the roughly +3 dB incoherent gain at a pole microphone under the flight path, the 250–500 Hz bands give an "equivalent lateral attenuation" of about 9–10 dB at 2°, 5–6 dB at 5° and nothing systematic above 10° — the same order as AIR 5662 at 2–5° in those bands, and less than it at 10–15°, consistent with Plotkin's Fig. 1 statement. Below 125 Hz the impedance model gives essentially no attenuation (a gain of 3–5 dB re free field) at any of these angles, whereas AIR 5662, being frequency-independent, removes 3–8 dB from every band. The distance dependence is weak above 1° (the 500 m, 2 km and 4 km tables differ by under 1 dB above 125 Hz), in line with Berton's 450 m convergence remark.
- The 10.86 dB asymptote is therefore not a ground-impedance number. It is the β → 0 limit of an A-weighted fit to 1.2 m data that is dominated by the 200–500 Hz dip for jet spectra, plus whatever average upward refraction the (daytime, lapse) test conditions carried. For a band-resolved helicopter model it should be compared band by band, not applied.
- The direction of the AIR 1751 → 5662 change, and of Plotkin's Denver result, is the same as NICEOPS's residual: empirical fits and models that attenuate strongly at 2–10° turned out too quiet against later data.

### Gaps
- The AIR 1751 and AIR 5662 documents themselves were not read; the number, types and ranges of aircraft in the 5662 fit, and its stated standard deviation, could not be obtained. The NMAP 1/β coefficient could not be confirmed (the scanned equation OCRs ambiguously).
- ESDU 82027 (third-octave lateral attenuation for jets) is cited by Berton and would be the only band-resolved empirical model; it was not accessible.
- No source found that tabulates the 5662 data scatter as a standard deviation by elevation angle.

## 2. Measured lateral / low-elevation attenuation against physical ground-effect models, and what the residuals were attributed to

### Takeaway
Every aircraft study found the same pattern: an impedance-ground model gets the frequency of the dip (200–400 Hz at 1.2 m over grass) and the mean A-weighted attenuation about right, ground effect is near zero above 10–15°, and what is left is large event-to-event scatter that the authors attribute to refraction by wind and temperature gradients, not to impedance or turbulence. Two results bear directly on the NICEOPS residual: in strong downwind conditions at Denver "there is virtually no ground attenuation" at all, and the sign of the mean error at low angle depends on which way the wind blows.

### Cited Findings
- [FT] Willshire T-38A (NASA Wallops, 1980): turbojet flown at 10–160 m over 8 microphones along a 2500 m concrete runway and 12 over grass; 28 flights; slant ranges grouped at 230, 440, 900 and 1600 m; elevation 0.5–40°. "The ground effects were largest in the frequency range of 200 to 400 Hz"; excess attenuation "greater than 20 dB" in those bands; "ground effects measured for angles of incidence greater than 10° to 15° were near zero"; "measured attenuation increased with increasing slant range for slant ranges less than 750 m", and the slant-range dependence "disappeared for slant ranges greater than about 750 m". — [NASA TP-1747](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19810006274/downloads/19810006274.pdf)
- [FT] Same experiment, model comparison: the Pao–Wenzel–Oncley procedure (Chien–Soroka with Delany–Bazley) with grass σ = 62.5 Mg/(s·m³) [62.5 kPa·s/m²] and incoherence coefficient a = 0.1; concrete fitted at 750 Mg/(s·m³). "The predicted frequency of maximum ground attenuation agreed very well with the measured frequency... The amplitudes of the predicted ground attenuation were generally smaller than the measured amplitudes"; "the predicted curves generally fall beneath the majority of the data points". Scatter attributed to "atmospheric parameter gradients and fluctuations, the data sampling rate in the data reduction, and the finite dimensions of the test surfaces". — [NASA TP-1747](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19810006274/downloads/19810006274.pdf)
- [FT] Willshire propeller experiment (Wallops, 1982; four-engine turboprop at 15–500 m over an 1800 m, 22-element array over grass, roughly half ground-level and half 1.2 m microphones): "Scatter in the measured results largely obscured the physics of the low-frequency noise propagation. Variability of the noise source, up to 9.5 dB over a 2-sec interval [at blade-passage frequency], was the major contributor". Weather was judged excellent; "refraction, turbulent scattering, and other propagation anomalies did not play a major role in the observed data scatter". — [NASA TM 89035](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19870008049/downloads/19870008049.pdf)
- [FT] Same: "Lateral attenuation measured in overall sound pressure level (OASPL) was less than 5 dB for elevation angles greater than 10°. For elevation angles less than 10°, the value of lateral attenuation increased to a maximum of 10 dB" (ground microphones). "The ground effects predictions formed an upper bound on the measured values" and "the predicted ground effects were less than the measured ground effects". — [NASA TM 89035](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19870008049/downloads/19870008049.pdf)
- [FT] Same, on method: "The microphones mounted at ground level produced more consistent results with less scatter than those mounted 1.2 m above ground", and "the generally accepted method of computing lateral attenuation utilizing overhead and sideline microphones led to misleading results with the highly directional propeller noise source... Different concepts of lateral attenuation and ground effects may be needed for airplanes dominated by periodic low-frequency noise, particularly for multiengined propeller airplanes and rotorcraft." — [NASA TM 89035](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19870008049/downloads/19870008049.pdf)
- [FT] Plotkin, Hobbs & Bradley (Denver International, in-service departures): 12 microphones at heights 0, 4 and 12 ft and at 666, 1000, 1500 and 2000 ft from the runway centerline; elevation 0–27°; video tracking; 10 m met tower; EGA theory with σ = 200 cgs rayls. "Lateral attenuation models currently used in the INM and Noisemap... tended to overpredict attenuation, i.e., noise levels predicted by these models would tend to be lower than measured. Lateral attenuation computed from modern ground impedance theory agrees well with average measured attenuation." "Above about 5 degrees, average ground attenuation is not a major factor." — [NASA/CR-2000-210111](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20000057531/downloads/20000057531.pdf)
- [FT] Same, attribution: "The cause of the scatter is refraction by wind and/or temperature gradients... Gradients will cause the sound rays to be curved, rather than straight, affecting both the reflection angle and the phase difference between direct and reflected rays." Upwind subset: the mean is negative at small angles (measured lower than EGA). Downwind subset: mean positive. — [NASA/CR-2000-210111](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20000057531/downloads/20000057531.pdf)
- [FT] Same, the strongest single result: for downwind components of 10–15 kt "there is virtually no ground attenuation, so the positive curve clearly shows that noise levels are higher than those predicted using EGA. The scatter in the data is also dramatically less". With no ground model at all applied to that subset "the mean is essentially zero". The authors add that "the normal temperature gradient in the atmosphere is equivalent to upwind propagation of several knots; the empirical models are described as being for 'moderate downwind' conditions to compensate for that." — [NASA/CR-2000-210111](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20000057531/downloads/20000057531.pdf)
- [FT] Volpe's INM revision used the Embleton–Piercy–Daigle model (σ = 150 cgs rayls soft, 20 000 hard), validated in the field for source heights to 1.8 m, receivers to 1.5 m and ranges to 1097 m. A Denver field study showed the theoretical model improving on AIR 1751 by "as large as 5 dB at an emission angle of three degrees". — [Fleming et al., Volpe](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/9996/dot_9996_DS1.pdf)
- [FT] Volpe's practical limits on the soft-ground model: a floor of −20 dB total ground effect, chosen "because in situations where this limit would be reached (i.e., shallow incidence angles and ground-to-ground propagation), atmospheric effects, including turbulence, tend to be the most significant propagation phenomena, effectively masking any ground effects"; range cut-off 4000 or 6000 m and angle cut-off 0.1° because the model "was sometimes found to behave counter-intuitively" beyond 4000 m at grazing; soft-ground equations not invoked at 30° and above, ramped to zero from 20°; soft ground never allowed to increase the level; "in most cases, the acoustically soft-ground effect curve converged to zero at reflection angles between 5 and 20 degrees". — [Fleming et al., Volpe](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/9996/dot_9996_DS1.pdf)
- [FT] Volpe on the residual: the difference between measured 727 lateral attenuation and the theoretical ground-effect curve was attributed to "engine/aircraft installation effects" (tail-mounted engines), i.e. to source directivity rather than propagation. — [Fleming et al., Volpe](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/9996/dot_9996_DS1.pdf)
- [ABS] Wunderli, Meister, Jäger, Schalcher & Zellmann (Empa, JASA 2022): sonAIR (physically based propagation) against AEDT and FLULA2 for grazing incidence; commercial approaches plus F/A-18 departures, "measurement distances up to 2.8 km". "A lateral attenuation term is justified for best-practice models... However, sonAIR yields better results than the two other models, with deviations on the order of only ±1 dB at all measurement locations." A physical model can "account for varying conditions affecting lateral attenuation, like systematic differences in the temperature stratification between day and night or ground cover other than grassland." — [JASA 151, 3140 (2022)](https://pubs.aip.org/asa/jasa/article/151/5/3140/2838395/Aircraft-noise-in-situations-with-grazing-sound)
- [FT] Krebs & Thomann (Empa): with a spectral 3-D source model the A-weighted ground effect over soft ground (300 kPa·s/m²) is about +2 dB for incidence above 30° and falls smoothly toward low angles, to 0 dB at a 1.2 m receiver and +1 dB at 10 m; hard ground gives about +2.8 dB with little angle dependence. Lateral directivity "may differ even for aircraft with identical engine configurations". — [Acta Acustica 95, 1013 (2009)](https://dael.euracoustics.org/bin/EAA/aaua_dl?document_id=64884)
- [SEC] Chessell (JASA 62, 825, 1977) showed that propagation over real soil can be modeled with the Delany–Bazley fibrous-absorbent relations and spherical-wave theory; a companion JSV paper (1978) concluded that finite-impedance ground combined with atmospheric refraction "can adequately explain observed aircraft noise propagation data". Parkin & Scholes' Radlett/Hatfield data are level differences between 1.5 m microphones at 19 m and 347 m from a fixed jet engine over grass. — [ADS abstract, Chessell 1977](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1977ASAJ...62..825C/abstract); [Chessell, JSV 1978](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022460X78800339)
- [FT] ACRP 02-44 repeats a 2004 US Navy/Wyle study at Washington National: 349 departures at elevation 4–6°, over water against over ground; "a nominal 2 dB effect of ground impedance on sound propagation for sources at low elevation angles"; measured minus calculated over-water SEL −0.8, −1.2 and −2.15 (±0.3) dB at three sites. — [ACRP 02-44 final report](https://onlinepubs.trb.org/onlinepubs/acrp/docs/ACRP02-44_FR.pdf)

### Inferences
- The consistent attribution across Willshire, Plotkin and Volpe is: mean → impedance ground; scatter and sign of the low-angle residual → refraction; model-minus-data differences for specific aircraft → lateral source directivity. Turbulence is named as the limit on how deep the dip can get (Volpe's −20 dB floor, Willshire's incoherence coefficient a = 0.1), not as a driver of the mean.
- Plotkin's strong-downwind result is the closest published analogue to NICEOPS's "2 dB too quiet beyond 1.2 km at 2–10°": with a modest downward-refracting gradient the ground dip disappears and a straight-ray (or weakly refracting) impedance model is too quiet by the full depth of its predicted dip. Morning desert flight tests (nocturnal inversion remnants, light winds) are exactly the conditions where that happens, and the effect grows with range because the direct/reflected path difference at the receiver is set by the local grazing angle, which a downward-curved ray steepens.
- The experiments do not agree on the sign of model-minus-measurement: in the T-38A test the model predicts less attenuation than measured, at Denver it matches on average, and the propeller report words its result both ways (predictions "formed an upper bound" and "were less than the measured ground effects"). This is itself evidence that the residual is meteorological and site-specific rather than a defect of the impedance model.
- Flow resistivities used for "grass" range from 62.5 (Willshire, fitted) through 150 (Volpe), 200 (Plotkin, NASA helicopter processing) to 300 kPa·s/m² (Empa). A dry lake bed is far harder than any of these; the published aircraft comparisons give no guidance for it.

### Gaps
- The sonAIR grazing paper's body (its per-site residuals by elevation and its day/night stratification result) could not be read; only the abstract.
- No DLR (Lummer, Olsman), NLR or ONERA helicopter-campaign comparison at low elevation was found in the time available; this is a real gap, not evidence of absence.
- Parkin & Scholes' and Chessell's papers were not read in full.
- None of these studies reports a residual as a decay-rate error in dB/km by band, which is the form of the NICEOPS discrepancy.

## 3. Helicopter-specific practice: how low-elevation data are handled when building source hemispheres

### Takeaway
No published NASA/Army/FAA source states a fixed minimum-elevation cut-off with a propagation justification; what the documents show instead is that coverage near the rotor plane is limited by array geometry and signal-to-noise, that the current AAM/ART tool deliberately leaves unmeasured regions empty instead of extrapolating, and that de-propagation uses Chien–Soroka/Delany–Bazley at 200 cgs rayls with straight rays in an isotropic atmosphere. The one method used to measure down to and above the rotor plane is a separate vertical (crane-suspended) array, which avoids grazing propagation entirely.

### Cited Findings
- [FT] 2019 medium-helicopter test (Coyle Field, NJ; AW139, S-76D and others): 39 GRAS 67AX microphones flush-mounted in ground boards over about 4000 ft (perpendicular) by 2000 ft (parallel); level flights at a target 200 ft AGL; field flat to within 38 ft. — [Pascioni, Greenwood, Watts et al., VFS Forum 76](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205003711/downloads/VFS2020_Paper_Pascioni_V11.pdf)
- [FT] Same, de-propagation: hemispheres built with ART inside AAM; 0.5 s segments, 4 Hz narrowband, a 5 dB signal-to-ambient gate ("high frequencies at emission angles close to the rotor plane may be attenuated beyond ambient levels by the atmosphere upon traveling long distances"); spherical spreading, ANSI S1.26 absorption in an isotropic medium, and ground losses from Chien–Soroka with Delany–Bazley at "200 CGS Rayls, a value typical of soft ground"; straight rays to a 100 ft radius hemisphere; Shepard inverse-distance interpolation with a 30° radius of influence. "The pressure doubling effects of the microphone installation and the ground board are not removed." — [Pascioni et al., VFS Forum 76](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205003711/downloads/VFS2020_Paper_Pascioni_V11.pdf)
- [FT] The data report for the same test words the ground correction differently: "The ground losses would capture the ideal circumstance of pressure doubling (6 dB) on a sound-hard boundary. That is, ground losses approach the 6 dB correction when the acoustic waves are normally incident and are lower at oblique angles." For ground contours the pressure is simply halved: "the assumption of a sound-hard boundary of the ground board is made as opposed to the more thorough ground loss correction applied to the hemispheres." — [NASA/TM-20210011459](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210011459/downloads/NASA-TM-20210011459FINAL.pdf)
- [FT] Hardware in both the 2017 and 2019 tests: GRAS 67AX, a ½-inch microphone flush-mounted by the manufacturer in a 15-inch (38 cm) circular ground board, offset from the center "to minimize edge effects", "based on the SAE Aerospace Recommended Practice ARP4055, but adapted from inverted microphones to flush mounted". The 2017 test also had three 4 ft tripod microphones at −45°, 0° and +45° to emulate certification positions. — [Watts et al., Noise Abatement Flight Test Data Report](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240013572/downloads/Noise%20Abatement%20Flight%20Test%20Data%20Report.pdf); [NASA/TM-20210011459](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210011459/downloads/NASA-TM-20210011459FINAL.pdf)
- [FT] Repeatability of the resulting hemispheres: at 30° below the rotor plane, over seven passes on four days (S-76D, 104 kt), levels "deviate by approximately 1-2 dB, and up to 3 dB at a limited set of observer angles". Predicted against measured SEL at nine stations: "within 2 dBA SEL for all positions except the outmost microphones and the microphone overflown by the helicopter"; farther from the flight path "uncertainty in propagation effects due to variation in atmospheric conditions, background noise, and terrain have a greater influence". — [Pascioni et al., VFS Forum 76](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205003711/downloads/VFS2020_Paper_Pascioni_V11.pdf)
- [FT] A working convention in Greenwood's noise-abatement analysis: "out-of-plane noise levels, defined in this paper as the noise radiated 30° or more below the plane of the horizon", are the ones propagated to the ground for guidance metrics. — [Greenwood, "Estimating Helicopter Noise Abatement Information with Machine Learning", AHS 2018](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20180006308/downloads/20180006308.pdf)
- [FT] RNM/ART history of near-horizon fill: "In previous versions of ART, the region of the semi-sphere beyond which there is no data was populated by assuming that the levels are constant near the rotor tip-path plane. The level from the nearest angle below the rotor tip-path-plane for which data were measured was used... The results obtained using this assumption may or may not be acceptable... the current version of ART does not attempt to extrapolate to regions of the semi-sphere beyond which there is no measured data. To identify these regions, ART now assigns a value of 1x10^35... This set value instructs RNM not to use that part of the semi-sphere when propagating the source to the ground." — [Conner, Burley & Smith, "Flight Acoustic Testing and Data Acquisition for the Rotor Noise Model (RNM)", AHS 2006](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20060020242/downloads/20060020242.pdf)
- [FT] The AAM manual says the same: "areas for which no reasonable source data exists are not generated. The source values are set to 1E35 by the ART utility and such regions on the sound sphere are not propagated." Gridding is by ZGRID with a user-selectable Laplace/spline blend. — [Page et al., AAM Technical Reference and User Manual, Wyle WR 08-12](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/ADA536390.pdf)
- [FT] Separate lateral measurement: at Eglin (2003, and the 2005 heavy-lift test) a "goal-post" array of four microphones suspended from each of two cranes 800 ft apart, the highest at 175 ft AGL, with 12 ground-board microphones between them, gave "approximately equal angular resolution for the acoustic measurements (up to and even slightly above the rotor plane)" for an aircraft flying between the cranes at 150 ft. A "north-pole" crane 5000 ft down range (six microphones, 30–175 ft AGL) covered the direction straight ahead. For a 150 ft level flyover "measured data were acquired laterally up to the rotor tip-path plane (φ = 90° and −90°) except in the region near the tail"; for a 6° approach at 250 ft "a significant number of semi-sphere grid points lie outside the region where measured data was acquired, especially near the rotor tip-path-plane". — [Conner, Burley & Smith 2006](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20060020242/downloads/20060020242.pdf)
- [FT] EASA/NLR rotorcraft modeling guidance: hemispheres are "required to cover at least" lateral emission angles −60° ≤ φ ≤ 60° (i.e. to 30° below the horizon each side) "for both practical reasons and data quality-related issues"; "measurement of higher lateral angles or polar angles approaching 0°/180° is possible but require complex measurement setups"; outside the measured range "constant value extrapolation should be applied from the nearest filled data bin". Hover offsets in the same document "were derived from measurements with inverted microphones on ground plates. Recently EMPA found that these values may not be valid for other microphone setups (e.g., 4 m height over hard or soft ground)." — [EASA D1.5d Rotorcraft noise modeling guidance](https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/downloads/132005/en)
- [FT] AAM's propagation: Chien–Soroka/Delany–Bazley ground with optional Chessell turbulent decoherence (typical parameter quoted in the manual), broadband bands summed incoherently over one-third octaves, a curved-ray module for a horizontally stratified atmosphere with wind since RNM 4.0. "For small elevation angles between the source and receiver, attenuation is largely influenced by ground effects and reflection losses." — [AAM Technical Reference, WR 08-12](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/ADA536390.pdf)
- [FT] ACRP 02-44 cross-comparison with an omnidirectional source at 492, 1000 and 5000 ft AGL out to 40 000 ft lateral: AAM and HELENA agree with each other; against INM they "do not agree well with increasing lateral distance" for the 492 ft flight, the gap closing as altitude rises; "these differences... may be attributed to ground effect modeling". — [ACRP 02-44 final report](https://onlinepubs.trb.org/onlinepubs/acrp/docs/ACRP02-44_FR.pdf)
- [SEC] In the Bell 430 maneuvere test, microphones were sometimes placed closer to the flight path than planned to keep signal-to-noise, "thereby limiting angular coverage on the acoustic hemisphere data at or near the in-plane position". — [Watts, Snider, Greenwood et al., AHS 2012](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20120008937/downloads/20120008937.pdf)
- [SEC] The project's own sphere builder (panam) has already replaced the flat −6 dB board factor with a boundary-element model of the 15-inch plate on soft ground for the 2017 spheres; on the Bell 407 this raised sphere levels by 0.2–0.4 dB below 100 Hz and 0.8–2.6 dB at 0.5–2 kHz, "most toward the sidelines". — [panam pull request 7](https://github.com/PsuAeroacoustics/panam/pull/7)

### Inferences
- [CALC] With level flyovers at 200 ft AGL and the outermost microphones 2000 ft to the side, the lowest elevation the 2019 array could measure at closest approach is atan(200/2000) ≈ 5.7°; earlier and later in the pass it is lower. Any hemisphere content below roughly 6–10° therefore comes from the longest ranges, the lowest signal-to-noise and the most uncertain ground correction in the data set — the three reasons the published documents give for leaving near-plane bins empty.
- Two hemispheres built from the same data but with different handling of the ground term will differ most at the sidelines: the VFS paper says the board's pressure doubling is "not removed" while the ground loss is, the TM says the loss tends to 6 dB at normal incidence, and the contours use a flat halving. A reader cannot tell from the published text whether a hemisphere bin at 10° has had +6 dB, a Chien–Soroka soft-ground value or both taken out. This ambiguity is itself a candidate source of a 1–3 dB bias at low elevation.
- A Chien–Soroka correction at 200 cgs rayls for a flush receiver is a correction for a microphone lying on the soil. It over-corrects a microphone on a rigid plate at mid and high frequency (where the plate is acoustically large) and is wrong over a hard lake bed at all frequencies.
- The only published ways to get trustworthy source levels at or near the horizon are (a) vertical arrays at short range, (b) constant-value extension from the lowest trusted bin (old ART, EASA guidance), or (c) not using those bins. No physics-based fill (e.g. from a rotor-noise prediction blended to the measured bins) was found in the rotorcraft flight-test literature reviewed.

### Gaps
- No source found that states "data below 10° (or 15°) elevation were excluded" for the 2017 or 2019 NASA tests, or gives a justification. The cut-off in the NICEOPS sphere build appears to be a processing choice in panam, not a documented NASA convention.
- FRAME (Greenwood's thesis) and HELENA documentation were not read; HNM is covered only through Volpe's remark that its NPD data came from Volpe helicopter measurements.
- RNM/AAM validation reports with residuals broken out by elevation angle were not found.
- Sickenberger's "Modeling Helicopter Near-Horizon Harmonic Noise" (cited in the 2017 report and ACRP 02-44) is the obvious candidate for a physics-based near-horizon fill; it was not read.

## 4. Ground-plane (flush, inverted, ground-board) microphones at grazing incidence

### Takeaway
The +6 dB assumption is documented to fail for a small plate on soft ground at low frequency and low elevation, by more than 3 dB in narrow bands over grass, because the plate is smaller than the reflection's Fresnel zone and sound diffracts around and under its edges. Quantitative correction curves exist only in recent paywalled papers; the standards (ARP 4055, Annex 16) were written for certification geometries near overhead and do not supply a grazing correction.

### Cited Findings
- [SEC] Journal of Sound and Vibration 2024: "Diffraction of sound at the edges of the ground-board can result in the sound pressure level varying over the top surface of the board and deviating from the 6 dB increase above the incident sound field"; narrow-band measurements with a circular ground board on grass "can deviate by more than 3 dB from the nominal 6 dB increase"; the variation "is dependent on the ground impedance, angle of incidence and frequency". — ["On the use of ground-board mounted microphones for outdoor noise measurements", JSV 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022460X24001950)
- [SEC] AIAA 2023: ground-plane microphone measurements "can deviate from the ideal 'pressure doubling' case by an amount depending on both the impact angle of the sound wave and its wavelength", through propagation around and under the plate edges. — [AIAA 2023-4165](https://arc.aiaa.org/doi/10.2514/6.2023-4165)
- [SEC] The certification-style installation studied in that literature is an inverted microphone 15 mm above, and at three-quarters radius on, a 40 cm heavy metal plate. — [Correction Curves for Ground-Board Mounted Microphone Measurements, Acoustics Australia](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40857-026-00407-0)
- [SEC] Willshire & Nystrom (NASA, 1982): a 12.7 mm pressure microphone inverted at several heights and side-set, at several incidence angles, agreed with a flush-mounted microphone below 4 kHz and was insensitive to height above the board; above that a dip appeared from interference between the direct wave and the board reflection. (This is a statement about the microphone-on-board installation, not about the board-on-soil problem.) — [search summary of Willshire & Nystrom via JSV 2024 listing](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022460X24001950)
- [FT] Flight evidence that ground-level microphones behave better than poles for tonal low-frequency sources: in the Wallops propeller test the ground microphones "produced more consistent results with less scatter than those mounted 1.2 m above ground"; the 1.2 m results showed "a lowering of lateral attenuation values, including negative values... attributed to the interference pattern". — [NASA TM 89035](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19870008049/downloads/19870008049.pdf)
- [FT] Berton's limiting case: for a flush microphone on a genuinely hard surface G = 4 at all frequencies and angles, but "theoretical hard surfaces are elusive"; Embleton's practical upper limits on flow resistivity are about 3×10^8 mks rayls/m at 100 Hz to 1×10^9 at 10 kHz, and the surface-receiver factor departs from 4 "until the ground becomes more reactive or until elevation angles become small". — [Berton 2021](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210011612/downloads/AIAA-Aviation2021-Berton-Manuscript-FINAL.pdf)
- [FT] Plotkin's Denver array mixed surface microphones with 4 ft and 12 ft ones; the end-of-runway reference "was on a hard block on the ground" and "adjusted by 6 dB". — [NASA/CR-2000-210111](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20000057531/downloads/20000057531.pdf)
- [FT] NASA practice is a 15-inch board after ARP 4055 "adapted from inverted microphones to flush mounted", with the ground-contour metric computed by halving pressure. — [NASA/TM-20210011459](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210011459/downloads/NASA-TM-20210011459FINAL.pdf)
- [SEC] EASA guidance flags that hover-noise offsets derived with inverted microphones on ground plates "may not be valid for other microphone setups". — [EASA D1.5d](https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/downloads/132005/en)

### Inferences
- [CALC] The low-frequency limit of a small plate is a microphone lying on the soil itself. For that case (σ = 200 kPa·s/m², spherical-wave reflection, third-octave averaged, 1000 m) the level relative to free field is:

  | β (deg) | 125 Hz | 250 Hz | 500 Hz | 1 kHz | 2 kHz |
  | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
  | 2 | −0.2 | −3.3 | −6.5 | −9.9 | −13.2 |
  | 5 | +3.2 | +1.5 | −0.8 | −3.5 | −6.4 |
  | 10 | +4.5 | +3.6 | +2.2 | +0.2 | −2.0 |
  | 15 | +5.0 | +4.4 | +3.3 | +1.9 | 0.0 |
  | 30 | +5.5 | +5.2 | +4.6 | +3.7 | +2.5 |

  That is, the bare-soil gain is 1.5–4 dB short of +6 dB at 10° and 3–9 dB short at 5° across 125 Hz–1 kHz. A 38 cm plate recovers the +6 dB only where it is large compared with the Fresnel zone, which at 5° elevation is elongated by 1/sin β ≈ 11 along the propagation direction; at 250 Hz (λ = 1.4 m) the plate is far smaller than the zone and the response should sit near the bare-soil row, while at 2 kHz it should approach +6 dB. The transition band is where the measured transfer function matters, and it coincides with the 63–630 Hz bands of the NICEOPS residual. (Over a hard lake bed the soil row itself is close to +6 dB and the plate matters much less.)
- If spheres are built assuming +6 dB and the forward model also assumes +6 dB (or a consistent plate table), the plate error cancels at the elevation where a sphere bin was measured and reappears only where the forward geometry differs from the measurement geometry — i.e. at longer range and lower elevation than the sphere-building passes, which is where the residual is.

### Gaps
- No full text with plate response versus incidence angle, frequency and plate diameter was obtained. The JSV 2023/2024 papers ("On the modeling of..." and "On the use of ground-board mounted microphones...") and the Acoustics Australia correction-curve paper are the primary sources and should be read directly.
- ICAO Annex 16 / Doc 9501 and SAE ARP 4055 text was not accessed; what corrections they prescribe at low elevation (if any) is unconfirmed. Payne, Fields and Smith were not located.
- The 1993 Acoustics '93 paper "A comparison of the results from ground-plane microphones with 1.2 m microphones for aircraft noise measurement" (DOI 10.25144/20567) exists and was not read.

## 5. Engineering models used for aircraft at low elevation: what they include and what accuracy is reported

### Takeaway
The integrated airport models (AEDT/INM, Doc 29, and FLULA2's equivalent) carry low-elevation propagation as the single frequency-independent AIR 5662 term; the simulation models (AAM/RNM, sonAIR, ANOPP, HELENA) use spherical-wave impedance ground, and only AAM and sonAIR add refraction. Reported accuracy at grazing is ±1 dB in the mean for sonAIR out to 2.8 km; Nord2000 is validated to 1000 m (flat) and 400 m (terrain) and has not been validated for aircraft geometry; none reports accuracy by band at 1–5 km.

### Cited Findings
- [FT] AEDT: "AIR 5662 is currently the preferred lateral attenuation model in AEDT, though research is ongoing to upgrade its propagation accuracy by adding classical reflection predictions." NPD-based tools carry the 1.2 m centerline reflection implicitly and so have no bookkeeping conflict. — [Berton 2021](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210011612/downloads/AIAA-Aviation2021-Berton-Manuscript-FINAL.pdf)
- [FT] ANOPP: straight-line propagation, Chien–Soroka with Delany–Bazley, third-octave bands split into (default) five sub-bands, a Gaussian coherence coefficient C = exp[−(a·k·Δr)²]; validated by Zorumski against Parkin & Scholes and recommended by NASA in 1978. It has no refraction or scattering. — [Berton 2021](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210011612/downloads/AIAA-Aviation2021-Berton-Manuscript-FINAL.pdf)
- [FT] INM 6.1 validation: over 700 events near a major airport, average agreement within 3.2 dB with 2.0 dB standard deviation (as quoted by Volpe's Nord2000 report). — [Ahearn et al., DOT/FAA/AEE/2012-5](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/9687/dot_9687_DS1.pdf)
- [FT] Nord2000 for aircraft (Volpe, 2012): Nord2000 computes higher L_Amax than INM in nearly every case, the gap growing with range. With turbulence enabled Nord2000 was 5.3 ± 2.6 dB above INM (max 8.9 dB at the farthest receiver, 3000 m) — but the change within Nord2000 from turning turbulence on was only 0.1 dB, so the gap is the models' baseline difference. Effective-flow-resistivity case: −7.0 ± 2.4 dB. Part of the baseline gap (2–4 dB) is bookkeeping: INM's lateral attenuation is zero under the source and assumes the reflection is in the source data, Nord2000 adds a reflection. — [Ahearn et al., DOT/FAA/AEE/2012-5](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/9687/dot_9687_DS1.pdf)
- [FT] Same, on grazing: "The effect of turbulence in raising levels has been seen to be substantial for longer range propagation where grazing angles of the ground-reflected ray are very shallow however; turbulence had only a small effect for any condition with the source-receiver geometries used in this analysis." "The largest deviations... occur when a shadow zone is formed." — [Ahearn et al., DOT/FAA/AEE/2012-5](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/9687/dot_9687_DS1.pdf)
- [FT] Same, on validity: Nord2000 "has been validated for propagation distances up to 1000 m"; 0–400 m with terrain and weather, 400–1000 m flat terrain and weather only. It "does not include an aircraft source model, nor has it been validated beyond 400 m [for terrain]... Further validation and analysis are needed to determine the accuracy of Nord2000 beyond 400 m." Volpe recommended adopting specific adjustments, not the full method. — [Ahearn et al., DOT/FAA/AEE/2012-5](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/9687/dot_9687_DS1.pdf)
- [FT] Harmonoise against Nord2000 (Salomons et al., 16 benchmark cases): both engineering models are "restricted to linear sound speed profiles"; "for propagation over 300 m we found considerable differences between reference solutions for a logarithmic profile and the linearized profile"; Harmonoise omits the multiple ground reflections that matter at large distance in downward refraction, Nord2000 has a correction for them; the published 2.5 and 3.0 dB standard deviations against reference solutions "may be too optimistic in some cases". — [Salomons et al., NAG/DAGA 2009](https://pub.dega-akustik.de/NAG_DAGA_2009/data/articles/000250.pdf)
- [SEC] Harmonoise validation against long-term in situ measurements: 95 % confidence intervals of ±1 to ±5 dB(A) depending on terrain. — [van Maercke & Defrance, Harmonoise reference model](https://www.academia.edu/20571350/Outdoor_sound_propagation_reference_model_developed_in_the_European_Harmonoise_project)
- [ABS] sonAIR: physically based propagation that "claims to implicitly account for the phenomena condensed in [the lateral attenuation] correction"; deviations "on the order of only ±1 dB at all measurement locations" out to 2.8 km at grazing, better than AEDT and FLULA2. — [Wunderli et al., JASA 2022](https://pubs.aip.org/asa/jasa/article/151/5/3140/2838395/Aircraft-noise-in-situations-with-grazing-sound)
- [FT] AAM: impedance ground with optional turbulent decoherence, curved rays in a stratified windy atmosphere, terrain; agrees with HELENA and diverges from INM at low altitude and large lateral distance. — [AAM Technical Reference, WR 08-12](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/ADA536390.pdf); [ACRP 02-44](https://onlinepubs.trb.org/onlinepubs/acrp/docs/ACRP02-44_FR.pdf)
- [SEC] ISO 9613-2:2024 refines the determination of the ground factor G and adds an informative Annex D on wind turbines; it remains a downwind, octave-band engineering method. — [ISO 9613-2:2024](https://www.iso.org/standard/74047.html); [CadnaA documentation](https://doku.datakustik.com/CadnaA/en/Industrie_ISO24.html)
- [ABS] ISO 9613-2 (1996) at 63 Hz for elevated sources: "Both measurements and FFP calculations showed that beyond 1 km, ISO based calculations could underestimate sound pressure levels." — [Keith et al., JASA 144, 981 (2018)](https://pubs.aip.org/asa/jasa/article/144/2/981/950002/Wind-turbine-low-frequency-and-infrasound)

### Inferences
- Summary of capability at grazing, from the sources above:

  | Model | Ground | Refraction | Turbulence | Low-elevation treatment |
  | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
  | AEDT / INM, Doc 29 | implicit in NPD + AIR 5662 | implicit, averaged | implicit | one empirical curve, all bands |
  | NOISEMAP | empirical A(β) | implicit | implicit | constant 10.06 dB below 2° |
  | ANOPP | Chien–Soroka, D–B | none | coherence coefficient | physical, straight ray |
  | AAM / RNM | Chien–Soroka, D–B | curved rays, stratified with wind | Chessell decoherence (optional) | physical |
  | HELENA | impedance ground (agrees with AAM) | not established here | not established here | physical |
  | sonAIR | physical, ground cover classes | yes (temperature stratification) | not established here | physical, ±1 dB mean to 2.8 km |
  | Nord2000 | spherical-wave, Fresnel-zone weighting | linear profile, curved rays | coherence loss | validated to 1 km, flat |
  | Harmonoise / CNOSSOS | similar, ground-curvature analogy | linear profile | coherence loss | no multiple reflections |
  | ISO 9613-2 | empirical A_gr | downwind by construction | none | under-predicts beyond 1 km at 63 Hz |

- NICEOPS already contains more propagation physics than any model in the table except that none of the engineering models' empirical downwind bias is present: ISO 9613-2, Nord2000's validated cases and the AIR 5662 data are all "moderate downwind / average" conditions, whereas a physics model driven by a measured profile predicts whatever that profile implies. If the profile used is smoother or more neutral than the real near-surface layer, the model will sit below all of them.
- The Volpe remark that turbulence is "substantial" only at very shallow reflected-ray grazing angles at long range is the engineering-model statement of the same regime NICEOPS is weak in.

### Gaps
- AzB, CNOSSOS-EU's aircraft part, FLULA2 and IMAGINE documents were not read; the FLULA2 lateral term is known here only through Krebs & Thomann.
- sonAIR's treatment of turbulence and its meteorological inputs were not confirmed (the 2025 Acta Acustica sensitivity paper returned HTTP 403).
- No model reports validation statistics by third-octave band at 1–5 km for an elevated source; all quoted figures are A-weighted event levels.

## 6. Measured long-range datasets with elevated sources usable as grazing-propagation benchmarks

### Takeaway
Usable benchmarks exist in three families: NASA Wallops/Denver aircraft experiments (full appendices of tracking, weather and spectra in TP-1747), wind-turbine and loudspeaker campaigns with towers (Høvsøre, the WES 2023 database, Medicine Bow), and airport monitoring sets at grazing (Empa, to 2.8 km). Their common finding is good agreement in the lit and downwind regimes and gross failure of non-turbulent models in upwind shadows.

### Cited Findings
- [FT] **NASA Wallops T-38A (1980).** Source: turbojet at 10–160 m altitude. Receivers: 12 microphones over grass (1.2 m and others) and 8 along a concrete runway; slant-range groups 230, 440, 900, 1600 m; elevation 0.5–40°. Finding: ground effect peaks at 200–400 Hz (> 20 dB), vanishes above 10–15°, saturates with range beyond ~750 m; EPNL lateral attenuation spreads over 20 dB at small angles. The report's appendices list emission time, position, velocity and weather per run and microphone "in a form which will allow independent analysis". — [NASA TP-1747](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19810006274/downloads/19810006274.pdf)
- [FT] **NASA Wallops propeller aircraft (1982).** Source: four-engine turboprop at 15–500 m. Receivers: 22 microphones over 1800 m of grass, about half at ground level and half at 1.2 m; laser/radar tracking and detailed weather. Finding: source variability (9.5 dB in 2 s at BPF) dominates; OASPL lateral attenuation < 5 dB above 10°, up to 10 dB below; ground microphones less scattered than 1.2 m. — [NASA TM 89035](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19870008049/downloads/19870008049.pdf)
- [FT] **Denver International (Wyle/NASA, 2000).** Source: in-service jet departures, heights from about 30 ft upward. Receivers at 0, 4 and 12 ft, 666–2000 ft lateral (203–610 m); elevation 0–27°; 10 m met tower; prairie ground modeled at 200 cgs rayls. Finding: impedance theory matches the mean, empirical models over-attenuate, strong downwind removes the ground dip. — [NASA/CR-2000-210111](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20000057531/downloads/20000057531.pdf)
- [FT] **Medicine Bow WTS-4 (NASA, 1984).** Source: 80 m diameter, 4 MW downwind-rotor turbine, hub 80 m; low-frequency source height taken as ~40 m, broadband ~80–120 m. Receivers: ground-level low-frequency microphones at nine positions, 300 m to 10 147 m downwind; elevation 8.0° down to 0.2°. Ground: "dirt with little vegetation", modeled at 1000 cgs rayls. Wind 12–15 m/s at hub. Finding: 6, 8 and 11 Hz harmonics decay at 3 dB per doubling beyond 450 m; the 63, 250 and 1000 Hz bands (independent measurements, absorption-corrected) show spherical spreading out to the 1000 m measured. — [Willshire, NASA TM 86409](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19850018007/downloads/19850018007.pdf)
- [FT] **Høvsøre and Hitra loudspeaker tests (DELTA, Nord2000 validation).** Source: loudspeaker at 30 or 50 m. Receivers at 2 and 5 m, at 500, 1000 and 1500 m, upwind and downwind, with simultaneous meteorology; spectra 31.5 Hz–8 kHz relative to free field. Conclusions: "Good agreement between measured and predicted values of ground effect"; "Apparent overestimation of the noise in the shadow zone" [as written on the slide]; "Difficulties in predicting the exact occurrence of the shadow zone"; "Good results with negative shear". — [Plovsing/DELTA in IEA Wind Task 11 Topical Expert Meeting 58 proceedings](https://iea-wind.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/58_Noise.pdf)
- [SEC] **WES 2023 wind-turbine database (Germany).** Source: turbine with 119 m hub, 114 m rotor. Receivers at 1.70 m, at 178, 535 and 845 m; 100 m met mast (29, 57, 76, 100 m). Flat grassland, σ = 200 kPa·s/m², Delany–Bazley–Miki. Model: Crank–Nicolson PE, 80 Hz–2 kHz. Finding: mean absolute differences of about 1.6–1.8 dB (178→535 m) and about 3.2 dB (178→845 m) downwind; agreement degrades with distance; in strong upwind cases deviations exceed 20 dB at 845 m "because turbulence is not considered" — the PE creates shadow zones that the measurements do not show. (Numbers are from a tool-generated summary of the open-access article and should be checked against it before use.) — [Wind Energy Science 8, 639 (2023)](https://wes.copernicus.org/articles/8/639/2023/)
- [ABS] **Health Canada wind-turbine study.** Spectral peaks identifiable "up to 10 km away from wind turbines at frequencies from 0.5 to 70 Hz"; measurements with on-site meteorology "were in agreement with calculations using Parabolic Equation (PE) and Fast Field Program (FFP)". — [Keith et al., JASA 144, 981 (2018)](https://pubs.aip.org/asa/jasa/article/144/2/981/950002/Wind-turbine-low-frequency-and-infrasound)
- [ABS] **Empa grazing-incidence set.** Commercial approaches and F/A-18 departures, measurement distances to 2.8 km. — [Wunderli et al., JASA 2022](https://pubs.aip.org/asa/jasa/article/151/5/3140/2838395/Aircraft-noise-in-situations-with-grazing-sound)
- [FT] **Washington National over-water set (US Navy/Wyle 2004).** 349 departures at 4–6° elevation over the Potomac against over ground; ~2 dB SEL ground-impedance effect. — [ACRP 02-44](https://onlinepubs.trb.org/onlinepubs/acrp/docs/ACRP02-44_FR.pdf)
- [FT] **Eglin 2003/2005 rotorcraft tests.** Crane-suspended vertical arrays to 175 ft with ground boards between and ground boards at 0, 500, 1000 and 2000 ft ahead of a down-range crane; tethered weather balloon. — [Conner, Burley & Smith 2006](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20060020242/downloads/20060020242.pdf)

### Inferences
- [CALC] Elevation angles of these benchmarks: Høvsøre 50 m at 1500 m = 1.9°, 30 m at 500 m = 3.4°; WES 119 m at 845 m = 8.0°; Medicine Bow 0.2–8°; Denver 0–27°. Høvsøre and Medicine Bow span the 2–10° band of the NICEOPS residual at ranges of 0.5–1.5 km and 0.3–10 km respectively; Høvsøre is the closest controlled analogue (known source, two source heights, up- and downwind, per-band excess attenuation).
- TP-1747 is the most reusable aircraft set because its raw tables are published; it would let NICEOPS's ground and refraction models be run against a jet source at 0.5–40° over grass with documented weather.
- The WES and Høvsøre conclusions point in opposite directions on shadow zones as summarized (PE with no turbulence far too quiet upwind; Nord2000 "apparent overestimation of the noise in the shadow zone"). Both say the shadow is where models fail; the sign depends on whether the model has a turbulence floor.

### Gaps
- Bondville (NASA/USACE), the Japanese (Konishi) long-range studies and the Norwegian Finnskogen trials were not located in the time available. The DELTA report "Validation of the Nord2000 propagation model for use on wind turbine noise" returned 404; only the IEA slide summary was read, so no mean/standard-deviation figures by distance are given here.
- No tethered-balloon or tower dataset over hard, dry ground (the lake-bed analogue) was found.

## 7. Low-frequency (below ~500 Hz) behavior at long range: is slower-than-predicted decay a known phenomenon?

### Takeaway
Yes. Decay slower than spherical-plus-ground is documented for elevated sources beyond roughly 0.5–1 km, most clearly at very low frequency, and the literature attributes it to downward refraction (multiple ground-reflected arrivals, ducting), explicitly not to the surface wave; at mid frequencies the equivalent observation is that a modest downwind component removes the ground dip. Turbulent scattering is invoked for the opposite case, filling upwind shadows.

### Cited Findings
- [FT] Medicine Bow: the 6, 8 and 11 Hz turbine harmonics follow 3 dB per doubling from 450 m to 10 km. "Surface wave amplitude predictions were more than 20 dB below the measured results. As a result downwind refraction is considered the most probable cause." The surface wave (Donato's model, Delany–Bazley at 1000 cgs rayls) shows cylindrical enhancement only to about 1000 m, after which its exponential decay dominates. — [Willshire, NASA TM 86409](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19850018007/downloads/19850018007.pdf)
- [FT] The mechanism offered: beyond "the shortest second reflection distance" multiple rays reach a ground receiver and spreading departs from spherical; "when the source height is increased, the ray-tracing pattern is magnified and the closest second reflection distance is increased. Sound from a higher source height would be expected to exhibit spherical spreading for larger distances than from a lower source height." The low-frequency source (≈40 m) departed from spherical between 450 and 1000 m; the mid-frequency source (80–120 m) had not by 1000 m. Ray tracing "is not considered applicable to frequencies as low as 20 Hz"; a normal-mode solution (Chunchuzov) has cylindrical spreading in the far field. — [Willshire, NASA TM 86409](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19850018007/downloads/19850018007.pdf)
- [ABS] Wind-turbine infrasound and low-frequency sound measured to 10 km agree with PE/FFP using on-site meteorology; "beyond 1 km, ISO based calculations could underestimate sound pressure levels" at 63 Hz. — [Keith et al., JASA 144, 981 (2018)](https://pubs.aip.org/asa/jasa/article/144/2/981/950002/Wind-turbine-low-frequency-and-infrasound)
- [SEC] A paper titled "Cylindrical spreading of noise from a wind turbine" (J. Wind Eng. Ind. Aerodyn., 2016) and a companion "Cylindrical spreading due to downwind refraction" attribute 3 dB-per-doubling behavior to refraction by low-level wind shear. — [JWEIA 2016](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S016761051500255X)
- [FT] Mid-frequency analogue in aircraft data: with a 10–15 kt downwind component at Denver "there is virtually no ground attenuation" at elevation below 30° and ranges to 610 m; the impedance model is then too quiet by its own predicted dip. — [Plotkin et al., NASA/CR-2000-210111](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20000057531/downloads/20000057531.pdf)
- [FT] Range saturation of the ground dip: measured attenuation at fixed elevation stops growing with slant range beyond about 750 m. — [Willshire, NASA TP-1747](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19810006274/downloads/19810006274.pdf)
- [FT] Turbulence: Volpe caps soft-ground attenuation at 20 dB because at shallow incidence "atmospheric effects, including turbulence, tend to be the most significant propagation phenomena, effectively masking any ground effects"; Volpe's Nord2000 study found turbulence raises levels substantially only where the reflected ray's grazing angle is very shallow at long range. — [Fleming et al., Volpe](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/9996/dot_9996_DS1.pdf); [Ahearn et al., DOT/FAA/AEE/2012-5](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/9687/dot_9687_DS1.pdf)
- [SEC] Upwind: a non-turbulent PE is more than 20 dB too quiet at 845 m in strong upwind conditions. — [Wind Energy Science 8, 639 (2023)](https://wes.copernicus.org/articles/8/639/2023/)
- [FT] Profile shape matters at a few hundred meters already: "for propagation over 300 m we found considerable differences between reference solutions for a logarithmic profile and the linearized profile". — [Salomons et al., NAG/DAGA 2009](https://pub.dega-akustik.de/NAG_DAGA_2009/data/articles/000250.pdf)

### Inferences
- Ranking of the candidate causes for NICEOPS's 1–2 dB/km shortfall in decay at 63–630 Hz, 2–10°, beyond 1.2 km, on the evidence above:
  1. **Downward refraction near the surface (most supported).** It is the cause named by Willshire (1985), Plotkin (2000) and Keith (2018), it strengthens with range, and it acts first on the lowest source heights and lowest elevations. Two distinct mechanisms are in the literature: the ground dip being removed because the reflected ray's grazing angle steepens (mid frequencies, short range), and additional multiply-reflected arrivals beyond the second-reflection distance (any frequency, long range). A model that solves one eigenray per pair includes the first and not the second. NICEOPS's stratified model returns "one arrival per pair" by design, so under a morning inversion it would under-predict in exactly this way. Multiple arrivals add energy that grows with range, which looks like a decay-rate error.
  2. **Ground harder than assumed.** A dry lake bed is not 200 kPa·s/m² grass. With a harder surface the 250–500 Hz dip at 2–5° in the table in section 1 shrinks and shifts upward in frequency. This gives a range-independent offset per elevation angle (the dip saturates beyond ~750 m per Willshire), so it explains a level bias better than a slope bias; mixed lake bed and scrub along the path would make it range-dependent.
  3. **Turbulent decorrelation of the direct and reflected paths.** It fills interference minima and is the accepted reason measured dips rarely exceed ~20 dB. At 63–250 Hz with a flush receiver there is no interference minimum to fill (path difference is zero), so for plate microphones this is a weak candidate; for 1.2 m poles it matters from about 250 Hz up.
  4. **Surface wave.** Ruled out as a significant contributor by the one study that tested it quantitatively (more than 20 dB too small), and it decays exponentially beyond about 1 km.
  5. **Source directivity below the measured hemisphere.** Not a propagation cause, but the literature's standard explanation for lateral residuals (727 tail engines, propeller asymmetry, Willshire's warning that lateral attenuation is ill-defined for rotorcraft). A sphere extended by constant-value fill below ~10° will be wrong in a range-correlated way for a level flyover, because elevation and range are tied by geometry: lower elevation is reached only at longer range. A sphere that is too quiet near the horizon and an atmosphere that is too attenuating are therefore not separable from sideline data alone.
- The published mid-frequency aircraft experiments stop at 0.6–1.6 km and the wind-turbine data with band resolution stop at about 1.5 km. NICEOPS's residual regime (1.2–5 km, 63–630 Hz, 2–10°) sits at the edge of what has been measured with a controlled source; the only data beyond it are infrasonic.

### Gaps
- No source quantifies a mid-frequency (63–630 Hz) decay-rate excess in dB/km for an elevated source at 1–5 km; the inference that the Medicine Bow mechanism extends to these bands at longer range is mine, supported only qualitatively by Willshire's source-height argument.
- The cylindrical-spreading wind-turbine papers and the Aalborg low-frequency report were not read in full.
- No study was found that separates inversion ducting from a harder-than-assumed ground using the same dataset.
