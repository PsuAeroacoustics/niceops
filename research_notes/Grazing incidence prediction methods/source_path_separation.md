## 1. How NASA, FAA/Volpe and European programmes measure or model rotorcraft directivity near the rotor plane, what elevation coverage they trust, and how they handle the rim

### Takeaway
Every programme reviewed treats the region within roughly 10° of the horizon as unmeasurable from ground microphones at trustworthy range. NASA says plainly that "data within 10° of the plane of the horizon are not available". NLR/EASA/NORAH stop at ±60° lateral, keep only the 10 dB-down window, and extend the hemisphere by constant values from the nearest bin. AAM/ART leaves the rim empty. The rim has been measured directly only with elevated arrays: cranes (Eglin, Wallops), NLR's 100 m vertical array, and QinetiQ's HAMSTER towers. Each of these used beamforming or height to remove the ground reflection, and each kept slant ranges short (NLR: under 450 m). The one physics-based route is FRAME: fit an analytical rotor model to the measured below-plane data, then use it to fill the directions that were not measured. NASA states that "no data is currently available with which to validate these above-the-plane noise predictions".

### Cited Findings
**NASA/Army (Greenwood, Sim, Boyd, Schmitz, Sickenberger, Conner, Watts, Pascioni, Stephenson)**
- [FT] AS350 hemispheres from three test sites (sea level to 7000 ft MSL): "Elevation angles are plotted from in the plane of the horizon, at 0° elevation to below the helicopter at −90° elevation. Data within 10° of the plane of the horizon are not available because the acoustic measurements are made from the ground." The near-in-plane analysis therefore uses observers "11R away from the rotor hub and 10° below the plane of the horizon", with waveforms built "by averaging the 80 blade passages nearest the target 10° emission angle". — [Greenwood, Sim & Boyd, "The Effects of Ambient Conditions on Helicopter Harmonic Noise Radiation: Theory and Experiment", AHS 72 (2016)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160009056/downloads/20160009056.pdf)
- [FT] Same paper: "For this flight condition [110 KIAS level], the predicted tip-path-plane of the main rotor is tilted 12° below the horizon, nearly in the plane of the observer" (the observer being 10° below the horizon, ahead). FRAME matches RNM/ART hemispheres "very good ... near the plane of the horizon"; "out-of-plane noise levels are generally within 3 dB of measured levels"; FRAME under-predicts aft, where non-harmonic noise dominates. — [Greenwood, Sim & Boyd 2016](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160009056/downloads/20160009056.pdf)
- [FT] FRAME-QS (Bell 430): "the magnitude and directivity of the lower-harmonic noise radiation on the upper hemisphere is different than that of the lower hemisphere ... models which assume noise radiation to be symmetric about the rotor tip-path-plane may lead to inaccurate results ... However, no data is currently availible with which to validate these above-the-plane noise predictions." BVI, a dipole, "radiates with equal amplitude above and below the rotor disk, although the phase of the BVI impulses is reversed"; lower-harmonic noise is a coherent sum of thickness (monopole) and loading (dipole), which is what makes it asymmetric about the TPP. The tail rotor's thickness noise "also contributes in the plane of the tail rotor". — [Greenwood, Rau, May & Hobbs, "A Maneuvering Flight Noise Model for Helicopter Mission Planning", AHS 2015](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160005979/downloads/20160005979.pdf)
- [FT] Sickenberger, as summarized in the same paper: "a major cause of the large increases in noise radiation observed during transient maneuvering flight was due to the re-orientation of the rotor tip-path-plane with respect to observers on the ground ... the noise radiated above the rotor plane is poorly approximated by the noise radiated below the plane of the rotor, such as that measured by ground-based microphones". — [Greenwood et al. 2015](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160005979/downloads/20160005979.pdf)
- [FT] FRAME thesis: rotor periods are ensemble-averaged within small angular windows along each virtual observer's track. "At positions near the horizon, the rate of angular motion is small, since the ground-based microphones were far from the helicopter", so "more rotor revolutions may be included in the average for virtual observers oriented towards the horizon". The observer set used for model identification is deliberately "biased away from the underside of the helicopter and towards the dominant radiation directions". A conventional interpolation "cannot yield extrapolated values outside the range of measured data". — [Greenwood, FRAME PhD dissertation, Univ. Maryland 2011](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/items/ef7460bc-4c99-45ad-87fb-af5288cda77a)
- [FT] Bell 206B BVI hemisphere (60 kt, −7.5°): contours "at all locations except near the center and in-plane positions of the hemisphere. These data voids are caused by not having enough microphones at these locations or not having enough microphone positions to confidently interpolate." At Moffett Field the background was ~10 dBA higher than at remote sites, so far microphones were moved inward, "thereby limiting angular coverage on the RNM hemisphere ... at or near the in-plane position". — [Schmitz, Greenwood, Sickenberger, Gopalan et al., "Measurement and Characterization of Helicopter Noise in Steady-State and Maneuvering Flight", AHS 2007](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20070021458/downloads/20070021458.pdf)
- [FT] Gilroy (Bell 206B-3) used 4 ft tower microphones; Sickenberger's model therefore carries a specular image path. For low-frequency loading noise 30° below the TPP, direct and reflected "combine together producing a wave nearly twice the amplitude"; for impulsive BVI the reflection "appear[s] as a second vortex interaction". — [Sickenberger, "Modeling Helicopter Near-Horizon Harmonic Noise due to Transient Maneuvers", PhD, Univ. Maryland 2013](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/items/255f1df7-59bb-496b-a486-2e767895fb6a)
- [FT] NICEOPS as NASA presented it (Pascioni 2020): steady flight-test hemispheres; nondimensional (μ, CT, γe, MH); heuristics to close the operating envelope; loading scaling ΔSPL = 20 log10(CT/CW). Validated on an S-76D −7.5° decelerating approach from 60 KIAS: "within 2 dBA with few exceptions", "higher uncertainty at large lateral distances". — [Pascioni et al., "Medium-Sized Helicopter Noise Abatement Flight Test" slides, Acoustics Technical Working Group, April 2020](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205008035/downloads/Pascioni_VFS2020.pdf)
- [FT] "At the highest airspeed, peak levels are associated with near in-plane emission angles", while GNEL stays nearly constant across speeds because "noise radiated at elevation angles of −30° are attenuated by approximately 6 dB more than −90°" and duration weighting is about 5 dB. — [Pascioni, Greenwood, Watts et al., VFS Forum 76 (2020)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205003711/downloads/VFS2020_Paper_Pascioni_V11.pdf)
- [ABS] Snapshot arrays (Army/NASA/Navy): "Sideline predictions of SEL compared well (within 0.1 dBA) between traditional and snapshot arrays, while center line locations were less favourable with a difference of 1.6 dBA"; "a minimum of 51 microphones is likely necessary". — [Stephenson et al., CEAS Aeronautical Journal 2023](https://consensus.app/papers/details/bb3903fd8fcf582dabbbdecda80000a6/?utm_source=claude_desktop). [ABS] The 2024 MD530F test added a mathematically uniform hemisphere-coverage "snapshot" array and a 117-channel phased array (ROSI beamforming to isolate main-rotor sources). — [Houston et al., VFS 81 (2025)](https://consensus.app/papers/details/be5ccb59d81053e1bf27b6d584953c9c/?utm_source=claude_desktop); [Pascioni et al., VFS 81 (2025)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250003322/downloads/VFS2025_Pascioni_Finalv2.pdf)

**NLR / EASA / NORAH (Europe)**
- [FT] NLR's AH-64 hemisphere design rules: R ≫ helicopter size; altitude "sufficiently high to stay clear of any influence of the ground surface"; "to minimize measurement inaccuracies (e.g. due to meteorological conditions) R ought to be less than 450m"; "the use of ground microphones should be avoided for lateral angles beyond ±60° for the same reason. Instead, a vertical array of microphones ... is desirable". Setup: a 96 m vertical array of 93 microphones at 1 m spacing (4–96 m) plus 13 ground microphones; passes at 85 m altitude, 90 m stand-off; ±5.5° polar-angle uncertainty from the 18 m airframe. Delay-and-sum over clusters of six microphones removes the ground reflection; it agreed with a reflection-free ground microphone "remarkably well" up to 200 Hz, with discrepancies at 200–500 Hz (coherence loss, GPS/time-sync error: "at 100kts, 0.1s mismatch results in a 5m position error"). Data are "about 4 times less" dense near the in-plane (90°) polar angle. — [Tuinstra & Sijtsma, "Measurement of Helicopter Noise Hemispheres Utilizing a 100m Vertical Array", ERF](https://dspace-erf.nlr.nl/bitstreams/10f99d37-2225-43e9-9bf1-ac584ac6cdd7/download)
- [FT] European method (NORAH; NLR/Anotec/SINTEF): the stored hemisphere spans lateral −60° ≤ φ ≤ 60° and only polar angles inside the EPNL 10 dB-down window. "Measurement of higher lateral angles is deemed impractical, as it requires flat open terrains exceeding 1km or complex measurement setups. In such cases, data quality cannot be ensured due to the long distance, propagation near parallel to the surface and relatively low helicopter noise levels when compared with the background noise." Beyond the data, "constant value extrapolation should be applied from the nearest filled data bin" (energetic average where several bins tie). Bilinear interpolation is done on 10^(L/10), i.e. in energy, not in dB. — [Tuinstra, Stevens, van Oosten & Olsen, "Towards a European Helicopter Noise Calculation Method", ERF 44 (2018)](https://dspace-erf.nlr.nl/bitstreams/86fda2c5-17f9-4cca-87e4-d72bc394ef34/download)
- [FT] Same: the hemisphere set should cover "angle variations of 3 degrees and of at least 4 different velocities" in descent; across the EC130 envelope, levels differ by up to 15 dB but "vary little (generally within 1dB) as function of rate of climb". — [Tuinstra et al. 2018](https://dspace-erf.nlr.nl/bitstreams/86fda2c5-17f9-4cca-87e4-d72bc394ef34/download)

**QinetiQ HAMSTER, Airbus, DLR**
- [FT, describing HAMSTER] HAMSTER targets long-range detectability, so "an accurate description of the in-plane noise ... is desired. In addition to a ground-based array of microphones, HAMSTER uses tower-mounted beam forming arrays to measure noise in the plane of the horizon ... the influence of interfering acoustic reflections off of the ground can be suppressed. However, because of the narrow angle of the beam, the helicopter must be flown across the array at several altitudes". Output is 1/3-octave only, typically "for several level flight speeds, and one climbing and one descending flight condition". — [Greenwood FRAME dissertation §1.3.2.4](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/items/ef7460bc-4c99-45ad-87fb-af5288cda77a)
- [FT] Airbus Helicopters' tool separates flight-test hemispheres into four contributors (main-rotor tonal, anti-torque tonal, engine, residual) and propagates each. — [Guntzer et al., "A Comprehensive Helicopter Acoustic Modeling Tool Based on Simulation and Experiment", ERF](https://dspace-erf.nlr.nl/bitstreams/9d783005-a5d5-46a4-97fc-595a3a18f2a4/download)
- [SEC] DLR's Hemisphere code transforms ground data to a lower hemisphere "by correcting back spherical spreading, atmosphere absorption and ground reflection", with "special interpolation schemes to allow using total angular coverage". — [DLR elib 19886 (search summary)](https://elib.dlr.de/19886/)

**FAA/Volpe (AEDT/INM)**
- [FT] AEDT helicopter mode: E = E_NPD + NF_ADJ + DUR_ADJ − LA_ADJ + MN_ADJ + LD_ADJ. "Helicopter NPD data for modes L, D, and A are provided at elevation angles of ϕ = 0 and ±45°. The lateral directivity adjustment interpolates the data between these angles and uses the ±45° data for angles greater than that." There is also an advancing-tip-Mach adjustment (MN_ADJ). — [Letica & Rizzi, NASA (2024)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240003615/downloads/main_v6.pdf)
- [FT] INM 6.2: SAE AIR 1751 "was developed principally for commercial jet aircraft. Military aircraft and helicopters are not addressed by this document", so INM applies class-dependent lateral attenuation. "The capability to turn off lateral attenuation for helicopter and propeller aircraft has been added ... This feature simulates propagation over acoustically hard ground." — [Volpe, "Assessment of Tools for Modeling Aircraft Noise in National Parks"](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/9811)
- [FT] Volpe order-tracking source separation: main-rotor harmonic, tail-rotor harmonic and broadband residual, de-Dopplerised to a 100 ft hemisphere and Shepard-IDW interpolated "without any smoothing"; for an 80 kt level case, analysis at azimuth 180°, elevation −10°. — [Rachaprolu et al., "Helicopter Noise Source Separation Using an Order Tracking Filter", JAHS (2023)](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/72623/dot_72623_DS1.pdf)

### Inferences
- [CALC] The TPP tilt puts the far-forward residual zone above the rotor plane. With the TPP 12° nose-down at about 110 kt (AS350 figure), an observer far ahead at 2–6° below the horizon is 6–10° *above* the TPP. Ground microphones sample the above-TPP forward sector only at long range and near grazing. A fast condition's forward rim is therefore exactly the region NASA calls unvalidated, and where Greenwood/Sickenberger say lower-harmonic noise is asymmetric about the TPP. For a TPP-referenced sphere this also means the far-ahead frames of a fast pass read a different part of the sphere than its sidelines do.
- European practice would discard most of the far-ahead data on two grounds: the 10 dB-down rule, and the ±60° and R < 450 m rules (NLR). NASA's ART leaves it empty. NICE-OPS's choice to fill the forward rim from low passes at short range has no published precedent. The nearest analogue is HAMSTER, which also flew several altitudes past an elevated array.
- Energy-domain interpolation (NORAH) and dB-domain interpolation (NLR regression; Shepard IDW in ART, which the papers do not specify) give different rim levels wherever scattered samples disagree. See section 4 for the size of that bias.

### Gaps
- No paper found states a NASA minimum elevation cut-off with a numerical justification beyond "within 10° ... not available from the ground".
- The HAMSTER tower arrays' heights, ranges and results, and any QinetiQ comparison of tower-derived versus ground-derived in-plane levels, were not retrievable.
- Whether ART's Shepard IDW averages in dB or in energy is not stated in the papers read.

## 2. How near-in-plane levels change with advance ratio, flight path angle, deceleration and height above ground (FRAME, Q-SAM, physics)

### Takeaway
Near the rotor plane ahead, the main rotor's lower-harmonic field is thickness-dominated. It rises steeply with advancing-tip Mach number: Gopalan's effective hover tip Mach number M_He = M_AT/[1 + μ(1 − M_AT)] is the governing parameter. Loading noise takes over more than ~15° below the TPP (Sickenberger). In-plane levels therefore depend on true airspeed, ambient temperature and TPP tilt, all of which vary with altitude flown at constant IAS. BVI depends on inflow and on the TPP's orientation to the observer. Q-SAM maps 0.1 g of deceleration to about 6° of steeper descent. No source was found showing that height above ground changes the radiated source in forward flight at the heights at issue (≥ 2 rotor diameters). Rotor ground effect is a hover/low-speed phenomenon, below roughly one diameter and below ~1.5 v_h of forward speed.

### Cited Findings
- [FT] Sickenberger (Bell 206B-3, observer ahead): "At elevation angles from approximately 30° above the tip-path plane to 15° below the tip-path plane, the thickness noise is louder than the loading noise. As the advance ratio increases, the acoustic levels from thickness noise also increase. Out of the plane of the main rotor, the low frequency loading noise is exposed to the observer." The thickness "fading term" dominates the unsteady term. Loading-noise phasing depends on the inflow model (uniform vs Beddoes), more so out of plane: "one inflow distribution could produce little in-plane acoustics while the other produces a lot", with potentially "important implications ... for heavier aircraft with larger disk loadings". — [Sickenberger 2013](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/items/255f1df7-59bb-496b-a486-2e767895fb6a)
- [FT] Sickenberger: thickness and low-frequency loading during maneuvers "was found to depend on the orientation of the tip-path plane relative to the observer"; BVI "on the inflow through the main rotor and the orientation of the tip-path plane". Higher disk-loading aircraft keep the wake further below the TPP: "a 2011 flight test with a Bell 430 ... had to descend at steep angles to produce notable BVI noise". — [Sickenberger 2013](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/items/255f1df7-59bb-496b-a486-2e767895fb6a)
- [FT] M_He "was identified by Gopalan as the key scaling parameter for rotor thickness noise". The measured peak negative pressure (observer ahead, near the rotor plane) scatters closely about FRAME's M_He trend. At matched M_He, a 24% decrease in CW raised out-of-plane lower-harmonic loading by "nearly 6 dB", so loading noise does "not simply scale directly in proportion to changes in rotor thrust". — [Greenwood, Sim & Boyd 2016](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160009056/downloads/20160009056.pdf)
- [FT] At constant IAS, TAS rises with altitude, raising μ and M_AT: "the increase in noise levels with altitude is greater for higher indicated airspeeds. The simple monopole thickness noise calculation used in the FRAME analytical model is known to underpredict noise levels at high advancing tip Mach numbers; the increase ... is likely to be even higher in reality." BVI becomes "more intense at high altitudes where advancing tip Mach number increases". — [Greenwood & Schmitz, "The Effects of Ambient Conditions on Helicopter Rotor Source Noise Modeling", AIAA (2011)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20110011597/downloads/20110011597.pdf)
- [FT] HSI directivity (model and full-scale UH-1H, M_AT = 0.84, μ = 0.26): "highly directional"; the peak negative amplitude "decreases rapidly at increasing longitudinal directivity angles"; maximum intensity "directed forward but to the advancing side". The in-plane far field begins beyond 3 radii (1/r decay). — [Boxwell, Schmitz, Splettstoesser & Schultz, NASA TM-85850 (1983)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19840001953/downloads/19840001953.pdf)
- [FT] Q-SAM equivalence: "every 0.1 g (less than 2 kts/s) of longitudinal deceleration has the same effect as descending at constant speed along a flight path angle 6° more steeply than the nominal". Envelope closure replicates level-flight (no-BVI) spheres at γe = −24° and +35°. Load factors above one get 20 log10(CT/CW); "for load factors less than one, no scaling ... is performed". — [Pascioni et al., VFS 76 (2020)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205003711/downloads/VFS2020_Paper_Pascioni_V11.pdf)
- [FT] Bell 206B: steady −7.5° at 60 kt holds BVI peaks to about ±0.6 dB over 15 s. Accelerating at 0.084 g from 47 to 67 kt on the same glideslope reduces BVI "on average, by up to 9 dB". TPP angles "vary linearly with the square of the velocity; linearly with flight path angle; and linearly with acceleration" (a 2° constant bias against theory, blamed on air-data boom placement). — [Schmitz et al. 2007](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20070021458/downloads/20070021458.pdf)
- [FT] Bell 430 pitch-up (from 6° descent at 80 kt, ~330 ft, observers about 10° below the horizon): FRAME-QS with variable CT captured the transient "within 2 dB in all three directions"; constant-CT missed the rise, which was close to 20 log10 nz ≈ 3.5 dB. The conventional (horizon-fixed, nz = 1) model's level fell "as the observers move farther out of the plane of the horizon". Raised thrust changes BVI miss distances, "attenuating those radiating towards the advancing side observer and amplifying those radiating towards the center and retreating side". — [Greenwood et al. 2015](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160005979/downloads/20160005979.pdf)
- [FT] Tail rotor: Bell 206B3 at 60 kt level, "tail rotor thickness noise radiates ahead of the helicopter, setting the directivity pattern"; the main-rotor periodic noise "radiates ahead ... but at a lower elevation angle than that of the tail rotor thickness noise", and the tail-rotor pattern is "very similar" in descent, "as tail rotor thickness noise should not be affected by descent angle". — [Schmitz & Greenwood, "Separation of Main and Tail Rotor Noise Sources ... Using Time-Domain De-Dopplerization", ERF 35 (2009)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20090035729/downloads/20090035729.pdf). [FT] In an 80 kt level case the extracted tail-rotor noise "reaches a maximum directly ahead of the helicopter, at an azimuth angle of 180°", 15–20 dB above the residual at every blade-passage frequency. — [Rachaprolu et al., JAHS (2023)](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/72623/dot_72623_DS1.pdf)
- [FT] Turning Bell 206B: hemispheres show the tail rotor "has a strong influence" on directivity in turns (more tail-rotor thrust, tilted disk). — [Schmitz et al. 2007](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20070021458/downloads/20070021458.pdf)
- [SEC] Rotor ground effect: Cheeseman & Bennett (1955) gave an approximate method for ground effect at any forward speed, in reasonable agreement with flight tests. A search summary (source paper not opened) states that "ground effect decreases rapidly with increases in either height above the ground or forward speed. The major part of the decrease ... occurs at speeds less than 1.5 times the hovering mean induced velocity." Recirculation and ground-vortex regimes occur only "in a very narrow region of the advance ratio", with extra thrust at μ < 0.04 and z/R < 0.5. — [Cheeseman & Bennett, ARC R&M 3021 record](https://aerade.cranfield.ac.uk/handle/1826.2/3590?show=full); [search summary of ground-vortex literature](https://elib.dlr.de/141956)
- [SEC] Hover IGE recirculation ("fountain effect", XV-15) noise is "dominant at the rear of the aircraft with increased intensities 45 degrees below the rotor disk planes". — [Rutledge, Coffen & George, NASA (1991), search summary](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19920002774/downloads/19920002774.pdf)

### Inferences
- [CALC] Rotor ground effect is negligible for residual (3). For a Bell 407 (R = 5.35 m, about 2270 kg): v_h = √(T/2ρA) ≈ 10 m/s ≈ 19.5 kt, so 1.5 v_h ≈ 29 kt; and 75 ft is h/D ≈ 2.1. On the published picture, a pass at speed below 75 ft is aerodynamically out of ground effect. A 3–5 dB forward reduction there is unlikely to be inflow or wake-ground interaction. Within residual (2) (under 40 kt, under 100 ft, h/D < 2.9), ground effect becomes plausible only in the last tens of feet and below about 30 kt.
- [CALC] Altitude at constant IAS shifts M_AT. Take B407 ΩR ≈ 231 m/s (413 rpm), 100 kt. Going from about 100 ft to 2000 ft ISA at the same IAS raises TAS by ~3% and lowers c by ~0.7%, so M_AT rises from ≈ 0.831 to ≈ 0.842. Gopalan/Greenwood put thickness-noise sensitivity in exactly this parameter. The sign is right for residuals (1) and (3): high states louder ahead. Whether 0.011 in M_AT is worth 1.5–4 dB was not found. The NICE-OPS database is indexed on μ, γ and CT, not M_AT or M_He, so a state-to-state Mach difference at matched μ is invisible to it. With NASA's own nondimensionalization (μ, CT, γe, MH; slide 15 of Pascioni 2020), MH would absorb part of this.
- In-plane noise ahead is the sum of main-rotor thickness (peaking near the TPP, rising with μ) and tail-rotor thickness (peaking ahead). Both are strongly directive within a few degrees of their planes. A rim built from short-range, low passes and a rim heard from far ahead by high passes can differ if the two sample slightly different rotor-frame angles across a steep gradient. A TPP-tilt error of the size Schmitz reports (a constant 2°) is then enough to move the level.
- Height above ground can still enter through the measurement rather than the source. For a low source heard at short range at 1–5°, the extended source spans about ±1° of elevation at 300 m [CALC: 10.7 m disk / 300 m]. The flight-path angle and TPP of low passes are also more variable (pilots hold altitude near the ground).

### Gaps
- No measurement was found of a forward-flight helicopter's in-plane source level as a function of height above ground at fixed emission angle (e.g. 50 ft against 300 ft passes past an elevated array). The NLR vertical array and the Eglin/Wallops cranes are the facilities that could produce one; none of their published results breaks out height.
- No quantitative dB-per-ΔM_AT slope for near-in-plane thickness noise around M_AT 0.80–0.85 was found in full text (Greenwood's Fig. 8 trend was not digitisable from text).
- Main-rotor/tail-rotor interaction (tail rotor ingesting main-rotor tip vortices) is well documented qualitatively in the search results, but no near-horizon, full-scale level change versus flight state was found.

## 3. Decelerating approaches against steady-state spheres, and hover/IGE directivity near the horizon

### Takeaway
NASA's position is that steady spheres plus Q-SAM (γe from the net acceleration vector, TPP re-orientation, 20 log CT/CW) reproduce decelerating approaches and transient maneuvers within about 2 dB at ground stations, including near-horizon observers about 10° down. Deceleration is the dominant variable: "even small longitudinal accelerations can have a substantial influence". What Q-SAM cannot cover is the low-μ end. There no steady sphere exists, and envelope-closure heuristics (replicated level spheres at γe −24°/+35°, no scaling below nz = 1) stand in for data. That is where residual (2) lives. Hover/IGE directivity near the horizon is essentially unmeasured in the open literature reviewed. EASA notes that hover offsets derived from inverted microphones on plates "may not be valid" for 4 m microphones.

### Cited Findings
- [FT] "It was found that even small longitudinal accelerations can have a substantial influence on the noise generation processes during approach and must be modeled appropriately." For the S-76D, the largest 65 dBA SEL footprint came from a normal decelerating approach whose deceleration (x = −5000 to −2000 ft) "causes the effective flight path angle to pass through the noisy" BVI region. For the AW139, "a moderate deceleration quickly steepens the effective flight path angle beyond −10°". — [Pascioni et al., VFS 76 (2020)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205003711/downloads/VFS2020_Paper_Pascioni_V11.pdf)
- [FT] NICEOPS validation slide: S-76D −7.5° decelerating approach from 60 KIAS, "within 2 dBA with few exceptions", "higher uncertainty at large lateral distances". — [Pascioni et al. 2020 slides](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205008035/downloads/Pascioni_VFS2020.pdf)
- [FT] Sickenberger: Q-SAM "can be used to reliably predict the noise radiated during transient maneuvers"; dynamic against quasi-static flapping and wake made little difference. — [Sickenberger 2013](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/items/255f1df7-59bb-496b-a486-2e767895fb6a)
- [FT] FRAME-QS needed load-factor variation to reach 2 dB; the conventional model "neither changes the orientation of the sphere to radiate out-of-plane BVI to observers near the horizon, nor models the effective change in flight path angle ... due to the deceleration". — [Greenwood et al. 2015](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160005979/downloads/20160005979.pdf)
- [ABS] MD530F maneuvers: BVI appeared in rolls to either side and in pitch-ups, and "the strength of the interaction noise was found to be strongly dependent on the acceleration of the vehicle". — [Stutz et al., VFS 81 (2025)](https://consensus.app/papers/details/2eb0bcd8970751e8bf01009f1df52b03/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- [FT] EASA/NLR: hover offsets "were derived from measurements with inverted microphones on ground plates. Recently EMPA found that these values may not be valid for other microphone setups (e.g., 4 m height over hard or soft ground)." — [EASA D1.5d (via earlier notes)](https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/downloads/132005/en)
- [FT] DLR (Olsman & Lummer): wind changes the landing-approach footprint. Their SELENE flight-mechanics code is given the wind conditions, so the flight state is solved relative to the air; footprint contours show caustics "near the shadow zone boundary". — [Olsman & Lummer, "Influence of Wind on the Noise Footprint of a Helicopter Landing", ERF](https://dspace-erf.nlr.nl/bitstreams/61aeceb7-6f3b-486f-8d7e-d335cd4db426/download)
- [FT] AEDT carries separate hover-IGE and hover-OGE operations, but directivity for L/D/A modes only at 0° and ±45° sideline. — [Letica & Rizzi 2024](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240003615/downloads/main_v6.pdf)

### Inferences
- Residual (2) (slow, low, ahead +3–4 dB, behind −0.5 to −1.8 dB) has the pattern of a source assigned too much forward-radiated BVI or loading. During the final deceleration Q-SAM drives γe steeply negative: −6° per 0.1 g, so 0.2 g on a −7.5° glideslope gives γe ≈ −19.5° [CALC]. Below the lowest measured μ, the database can only return a replicated level sphere or a BVI sphere from a faster condition. NASA's published decelerating validations stop at 60 KIAS starts and say nothing about the sub-40 kt flare. Its envelope-closure heuristics were designed to bound γe, not μ → 0.
- During the flare the TPP tilts aft (Schmitz: TPP angle linear in acceleration). Observers ahead at 1–5° below the horizon then sit *below* the TPP by more than in steady flight, and observers behind sit closer to or above it. A fore/aft asymmetry of the observed sign follows if the borrowed sphere's forward rim is louder below-plane than the true near-hover source.

### Gaps
- No published measured-versus-Q-SAM comparison for the last segment of an approach (below about 40 kt, below 100 ft) was found.
- No open-literature hover-IGE directivity measurement within 10° of the horizon was found. FAA helicopter noise data reports (1977) list HIGE/HOGE levels, but the full text was not retrieved ([rosap dot 10026](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/10026), [ABS]).

## 4. Separating a uniform propagation bias from source directivity: test designs, inverse methods, and what lateral-attenuation programmes did

### Takeaway
The programmes that cleanly separated installation directivity from ground effect did it by design rather than by inversion. Volpe/NASA Wallops 2000 elevated microphones to 24 ft poles and 230 ft cranes, which pushed the ground dip below the A-weighted band. They matched emission angles between microphones, removed spreading, ISO absorption and an Embleton–Piercy–Daigle ground term, and called the remaining level difference against the overhead microphone the installation effect. NLR and HAMSTER beamform on elevated arrays to reject the reflection; Jayanthi et al. use an array's reflection-free spectrogram to quantify ground effect per flyover at two microphone heights. Statistical fits (NLR's regression, Empa/Pietrzko polynomial directivity, J-FRAIN regression, FRAME parameter identification) constrain the *source* given an assumed propagation. No study found estimates source directivity and ground/propagation parameters jointly in one statistical model. Two biases are directly relevant to "far grazing samples depropagate too loud": signal-to-ambient gating (censoring) and dB-domain averaging of scattered samples.

### Cited Findings
**Lateral attenuation and installation effects (Volpe/NASA Wallops 2000)**
- [FT] Four aircraft (767-400, DC-9, Falcon 2000, King Air) through a 20-microphone U-array "mounted such that ground effects were minimized and low elevation angles were observed". Hardware: two 230 ft cranes at a projected 485 ft from centerline, highest microphone 200 ft AGL, five microphones each; nine 24 ft poles; one ground-plane microphone; hourly tethered weather balloons; dGPS plus video tracking. Microphones "oriented for grazing incidence at the average of the nominal elevation angles". Aircraft flew "at the same altitude above ground level as the highest microphone". Altitude/offset combinations were chosen to give "the maximum number of unique elevation angles with a minimal number of flyovers"; constant-altitude passes avoid configuration-dependent climb angles. — [Volpe, "Engine Installation Effects of Four Civil Transport Airplanes: Wallops Flight Facility Study"](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/9817/dot_9817_DS1.pdf)
- [FT] Same, method: each non-reference microphone's spectra are taken "at the same (or closest) emission angle" as the reference microphone. Known effects are removed: spherical spreading, ISO 9613-1 absorption, and ground effect by Embleton–Piercy–Daigle, at 20 000 cgs rayls over runway and 150 over grass. "Differences in normalized sound levels between each microphone and the one directly under the flight is termed the engine installation effect." Microphones were high enough "so that ground effects would occur at frequencies lower than those that significantly contribute to A-weighted sound levels". 95% confidence intervals per 10° band were roughly constant with elevation, but configuration series diverged only at 0–10°. Conclusions: wing- and tail-mounted installations differ substantially; propeller aircraft "do not exhibit engine installation effects". The ground term is then the exponential term of AIR 1751's long-range equation, which they "can be considered to be the ground effects term" (leading constant −9.9), rescaled (to about −10.5; the scanned text is OCR-garbled). The installation term is a separate least-squares fit of the form Ae^(−kβ) + Cβ + D, and lateral attenuation = ground effect + installation effect. — [Volpe Wallops study](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/9817/dot_9817_DS1.pdf)

**Beamforming and array-based separation**
- [FT] NLR delay-and-sum on vertical-array clusters removes the ground reflection, validated against a ground microphone that sees no reflection, to 200 Hz (degrading to 500 Hz). The hemisphere is then a least-squares regression model in (polar, lateral) angle per 1/3-octave band (Taylor or truncated Fourier series), fitted in SPL: an energy-based fit "produces erroneous results", even negative energies, under strong directivity. A model of "only 13 empirically determined coefficients" captured "40000 responses" well; higher orders reveal tones but add scatter with frequency. — [Tuinstra & Sijtsma, ERF](https://dspace-erf.nlr.nl/bitstreams/10f99d37-2225-43e9-9bf1-ac584ac6cdd7/download)
- [FT] NLR's estimate of the dB-versus-energy averaging bias: for their scatter classes, "low and medium scatter ... negligible ... (0.03dB and 0.15dB respectively). For strong scatter a maximum accuracy is found of approximately 0.6dB"; "direct averaging of SPL therefore tends to underpredict" the energy mean. Their "strong" class is σ(p²) = 0.52 p²rms, labeled "±6 dB". — [Tuinstra & Sijtsma](https://dspace-erf.nlr.nl/bitstreams/10f99d37-2225-43e9-9bf1-ac584ac6cdd7/download)
- [FT] Jayanthi, Snellen & Amiri-Simkooei: a 64-microphone phased array gives a spectrogram with ground reflections "to a large extent eliminated". A two-ray ground model (Delany–Bazley, an effective flow resistivity for mixed terrain) is applied and checked against single microphones at 1.75 m and 4.5 m. The ground effect quantified this way reduces LA by up to 2.3 dBA for higher-elevation flyovers and about 1.8 dBA at lower elevation, "in contrast with the common corrections applied for lateral attenuation in best-practice modeling". — [Jayanthi et al., CEAS Aeronautical Journal (2026)](https://repository.tudelft.nl/file/File_9a77e490-1b2e-45f2-9699-ecf03914b4c8)
- [FT] Main-/tail-rotor separation by de-Dopplerisation to virtual in-flight microphones plus periodic averaging on each rotor's period (wavelet-detected). This is a source-side separation; propagation is still assumed. — [Schmitz & Greenwood ERF 2009](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20090035729/downloads/20090035729.pdf)

**Statistical and parametric source models built on assumed propagation**
- [FT] FRAME: "measured external noise is used together with parameter identification techniques" (a neural-network parameter estimator plus analytical thickness/loading/BVI models) to make "a hybrid between theory and experiment". Fit within 1.5 dB BVISPL at all observers, against 1.0–2.0 dB run-to-run standard deviation across three repeats of 60 kt/−6°. — [Greenwood FRAME dissertation](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/items/ef7460bc-4c99-45ad-87fb-af5288cda77a)
- [ABS] Greenwood (MS 2008): existing RNM hemisphere interpolation "lead[s] to high levels of inaccuracy when using sparse microphone arrays"; radial-basis-function interpolation "significantly improved accuracy". — [Greenwood 2008](https://consensus.app/papers/details/d2f0166508d35f77b207b51cfcaff4a9/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- [ABS] Empa (Pietrzko): directivity as a polynomial/cosine series with 32 coefficients fitted to flyover spectra corrected for spreading, absorption and delay, extended with a velocity term for convective amplification. — [Pietrzko et al. 1996](https://consensus.app/papers/details/9b0f8be1a68c5987a41d23aefc933e6b/?utm_source=claude_desktop); [Pietrzko 1988](https://consensus.app/papers/details/dead5019d4215784a4e9ae4bb877aaec/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- [SEC] sonAIR: back-propagated spectra account for "propagation time, Doppler shift ... ground effect, and atmospheric stratification"; separate engine and airframe models whose energetic sum is the directional sound power; SEL validation +0.1 dB mean, 0.9 dB standard deviation. — [Zellmann dissertation record, TU Berlin](https://depositonce.tu-berlin.de/items/b583b9a9-f7ed-45db-b923-b9c0d33a0802/full)
- [ABS] J-FRAIN (JAXA): a 30 m array under the approach gives component source powers; regression coefficients minimize RMS error. Time histories agree "within 2 dB if ground effect was properly considered". — [Takaishi et al., NCEJ (2024)](https://consensus.app/papers/details/3c2cfa67e8a4542c8cba6e95ee40cc23/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- [ABS] DLR PANAM against ATRA flyovers with explicit source-model uncertainty: systematic deviations in two of eight configurations attributed to one (slat) source model. — [Lößle et al., Aerospace (2025)](https://consensus.app/papers/details/0e863173789b52aeb8c40722e541ee7d/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- [ABS] UAM hemisphere (KNAC, 84 microphones): "by combining data from tests with different flight directions and altitudes under low wind conditions, dense scattered data can be obtained for a wide angular range". — [Cho et al., AIAA/CEAS (2024)](https://consensus.app/papers/details/473208349f6d5063b5d07f94777f8431/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Gating**
- [FT] NASA ART applies a 5 dB signal-to-ambient criterion; "high frequencies at emission angles close to the rotor plane may be attenuated beyond ambient levels by the atmosphere upon traveling long distances". — [Pascioni et al. VFS 76](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205003711/downloads/VFS2020_Paper_Pascioni_V11.pdf)

### Inferences
- [CALC] dB-averaging bias. If a bin's samples are log-normal with standard deviation σ (dB), the energy mean exceeds the dB mean by (ln 10/20) σ² = 0.115 σ² dB: 1.0 dB at σ = 3, 1.8 at σ = 4, 2.9 at σ = 5. Sphere bins gridded in dB (Shepard IDW in dB) and then compared with energy-mean measured frames are biased low by this amount. The bias is largest where scatter is largest, which near grazing is where turbulence and refraction scatter is largest. It is flat across frequency to the extent σ is. That matches residual (1)'s 1.5–4 dB, flat over 63–630 Hz, in sign and size. NLR's smaller estimate (≤ 0.6 dB) is for their own scatter definition and many averages, not for σ_dB ≈ 4–5. This is a check NICE-OPS/panam can run directly: grid the rim in energy and see whether residual (1) moves.
- [CALC] Censoring bias acts in the opposite direction, and explains why far samples "depropagate too loud". Keeping only samples ≥ 5 dB above ambient at long range, where the mean is near the floor, retains the upward turbulent fluctuations and drops the downward ones. For a Gaussian dB variable with mean m at the threshold, the truncated mean is m + 0.80σ (≈ 3–4 dB at σ = 4–5). This is the known way pushing the rim out to 10,000 ft makes it too loud. Both biases go away if the rim is fitted, censoring-aware (Tobit-style), in energy, rather than averaged.
- A design that separates a uniform grazing propagation bias from directivity, following the Wallops logic: the same rotor-frame emission angle reached at two or more slant ranges and source heights (multi-altitude passes at fixed stand-off), received on microphones at two heights (flush and elevated, e.g. 1.2 m and ≥ 10 m), over the same ground. A propagation bias scales with range and grazing geometry and changes between microphone heights; source directivity does not. The 2017 data's flush-board microphones are an advantage: they remove the interference term, so the remaining height-and-range dependence at fixed emission angle is refraction, turbulence or source.
- Reciprocity gives a controlled check of the propagation model at the residual geometry independent of the helicopter: a loudspeaker on a mast or crane at the source height, with the ground microphones as receivers. No published rotorcraft programme was found doing this. The Eglin and Wallops cranes and the NLR array point the other way (elevated receivers).

### Gaps
- No published joint (simultaneous) Bayesian or hierarchical estimation of rotorcraft source directivity and ground impedance/refraction from flight data was found. Every method reviewed fixes one side.
- The Wallops report gives the method but its tables of per-band ground terms against measurement were not extracted here.

## 5. Reported accuracy of rotorcraft footprint models at long range near grazing

### Takeaway
Published rotorcraft accuracies are almost all for SEL at stations within about 2 km and mostly above 10–15° elevation: about 2 dB (NASA ART/AAM, NICEOPS, FRAME-QS), 1–2 dB repeat-run spread (NORAH/R22), within 1.5 dB BVISPL for FRAME fits. Every source flags the outermost, lowest-elevation stations as worse. None gives errors binned by elevation in the 0–10° range at 1–4 km. Model-to-model comparisons out to 25,000 ft (INM against NMSim, within 2 dB SEL) and AAM against HELENA against INM (diverging with lateral distance at 492 ft altitude) measure ground-model disagreement, not truth.

### Cited Findings
- [FT] NASA 2019: predicted against measured SEL "within 2 dBA SEL for all positions except the outmost microphones and the microphone overflown"; hemisphere repeatability 1–2 dB (up to 3) at −30°. — [Pascioni et al. VFS 76](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205003711/downloads/VFS2020_Paper_Pascioni_V11.pdf)
- [FT] FRAME-QS near-horizon (about 10° down, ~1,500 ft ahead, 850 ft lateral): within 2 dB OASPL over a 1.5 g pitch-up. — [Greenwood et al. 2015](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160005979/downloads/20160005979.pdf)
- [FT] NORAH (R22): against HELENA, "95% of all grid points yielded results within 1dB and 99.9% within 2dB" over a 2 × 1 km grid; against measurement, a prediction standard deviation of "between 1dB and 2dB, achieving its maximum where the noise levels are highest"; EPNL and SEL within 0.3 dB for a hemisphere built from one run (loop-back). — [Tuinstra et al. ERF 2018](https://dspace-erf.nlr.nl/bitstreams/86fda2c5-17f9-4cca-87e4-d72bc394ef34/download)
- [FT] INM 6.2 against NMSim (national parks): SEL "within 2 dB out to a distance of 25,000 ft, and maximum sound levels are within 3 dB", except the DHC-6 at almost 6 dB. A model-to-model statement. — [Volpe dot 9811](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/9811)
- [FT] Fixed wing at grazing (from the earlier notes): Denver large single-event variability at elevations up to 10–15°; empirical lateral-attenuation models over-attenuate. — [Plotkin et al., NASA/CR-2000-210111](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20000057531/downloads/20000057531.pdf)
- [ABS] Snapshot against linear array: SEL within 0.1 dBA at sidelines, 1.6 dBA on the centerline. — [Stephenson et al. 2023](https://consensus.app/papers/details/bb3903fd8fcf582dabbbdecda80000a6/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

### Inferences
- NICE-OPS's residuals (±0.7 dB on near/steep frames; 1.5–4 dB on far-ahead, 2–6° frames) are already at or inside the published accuracy envelope everywhere except the regime no programme validates. The far-ahead grazing regime is unvalidated territory rather than a known failure of standard practice.
- Because the PE agrees with the ray models at fixed emission angle, the literature points to the source side or the gridding statistics: rim asymmetry about the TPP, M_AT and altitude dependence, and the dB/energy and censoring biases. A uniform grazing ground bias would have to be large and range-independent, which the Wallops/Denver experience does not support for flush receivers.

### Gaps
- No rotorcraft validation with errors binned by emission elevation (0–5°, 5–10°, 10–15°) at 1–4 km was found.
- AAM/RNM long-range helicopter validations (e.g. Volpe's national-park Bell 407/R44 source measurements) exist, but their measured-versus-predicted comparisons at grazing were not located in full text.
