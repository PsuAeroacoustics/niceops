# Range-dependent (mixed) ground, gentle terrain, and in-situ ground inference at grazing incidence

Scope: elevated sources (10–700 m), receivers flush (0 m, ground boards) and 1.2 m poles, 0.3–4 km, 50 Hz–5 kHz, 0–10° elevation. Builds on, and does not repeat, `../Grazing incidence soft ground propagation/ground_impedance_models.md` §6–8 (Fresnel-zone sizes, AT2026 weighting on pressure squared, seasonal ratios) and `aircraft_low_elevation_practice.md` §4 (ground boards).

Tags: **[FT]** read in full text; **[ABS]** abstract or catalogue entry only; **[CALC]** my own calculation (not from a source); **[INF]** my inference.

Calculation basis for every [CALC] below (scripts were throwaway, in /tmp): plane-wave reflection at a flush receiver, level re free field = 20 log|1 + R_p(ψ)|; for a 1.2 m pole, |1 + R_p e^{ik·2h·sinψ}|² averaged over 41 points across each third-octave band; Delany–Bazley impedance (ρf/σ form). Delany–Bazley was used for illustration only. NICEOPS's variable-porosity model at σe = 200 kPa·s/m² gives different absolute numbers, but the sensitivities are similar in kind. No turbulence decorrelation and no refraction. The plane-wave form is adequate for a flush receiver with an elevated source (prior notes: within 0.1 dB).

## 1. Mixed-impedance ground models: De Jong, Rasmussen, Fresnel-zone weighting, strip models, BEM/FEM, PE with range-dependent impedance — accuracy at 0–10°, and the case of a change inside a flush receiver's first Fresnel zone

### Takeaway
Every validated mixed-ground method was validated at short range (≤ 10–60 m) with low sources and receivers. None was validated for an elevated source at 0–10° over hundreds of meters, which is the NICEOPS geometry. The semi-empirical De Jong family is accurate for one hard-to-soft step but is known to degrade toward grazing. Its errors come from the empirical admittance correction, and the original multi-discontinuity extensions break reciprocity by up to 8 dB. Fresnel-zone weighting on pressure squared is the engineering choice, and it is what sonAIR uses for aircraft (±1 dB at 0.7–7° to 2.8 km, though not as an isolated test of the ground term). PE with a range-dependent impedance is the cheap full-wave reference, because only the ground row of the march changes from step to step.

### Cited Findings
**Semi-empirical (De Jong and successors)**
- [FT] De Jong's model adds to the two homogeneous solutions a diffraction term at the discontinuity: (Q₂ − Q₁) times the hard half-plane diffracted field, written with Fresnel integrals F₂. The (Q₂ − Q₁) factor "is empirically constructed by physical arguments … its limitations are not mathematically explicit." — [Lam & Monazzam 2006, JASA 120, 686](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.2216905)
- [FT] "Daigle et al. … found that the model has difficulties dealing with situations when the grazing angles are small, but they were not able to formulate firm criteria for the use of the model." The errors at small grazing angle "are not caused by the approximation of the hard half-plane diffracted field, but rather by the empirical correction for the finite impedances." — [Lam & Monazzam 2006](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.2216905)
- [FT] The original De Jong formula assumes a hard-to-soft transition. Extensions that apply it to every discontinuity in turn (Bassiouni; Boulanger et al.) violate reciprocity: "differences of up to 8 dB." Lam & Monazzam's reciprocity-corrected multi-discontinuity model (nMID) "agrees well with … BEM" for single and multiple strips of porous ground. "The accuracy decreases as the source or receiver height decreases (nearer grazing incidence) and when the width of the strips or the distance between [them] … decreases." — [Lam & Monazzam 2006](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.2216905)
- [FT] Lam & Monazzam, summarizing Zhu, Stinson & Daigle: Rasmussen's approximate solution for an impedance jump agrees well with BEM, even for a jump that generates a surface wave. Rasmussen treats the space above the discontinuity as a screen of apertures, solved with ray theory. — [Lam & Monazzam 2006](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.2216905); Rasmussen's original, [JSV 84, 598 (1982)](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0022-460x(82)80042-4), was not read.
- [ABS] Daigle, Nicolas & Berry 1985: controlled measurements to 10 m outdoors (source over asphalt, receiver over grass) and 2.4 m indoors. Agreement with the semi-empirical and Green's-theorem solutions is "good … in most cases", but "in more extreme situations, including the 'soft–softer' ground case, the semiempirical solutions are in difficulty." — [Daigle et al. 1985](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.392276)
- [ABS] Boulanger, Waters-Fuller, Attenborough & Li 1997 (anechoic, point source, discontinuities perpendicular to the path). For a single hard/finite step, De Jong, the Fresnel-zone model and BEM all give "satisfactory agreement". The frequency of the first attenuation maximum is highest at about **70 % hard cover rather than 100 %**, which they attribute to edge diffraction. With multiple discontinuities De Jong "performs poorly", Nyberg's theory and BEM agree well, and the Fresnel-zone model is only "qualitative". Under Nyberg's constraints the area-averaged impedance reproduces the ground effect. — [Boulanger et al. 1997](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.420101)
- [ABS] Hothersall & Harriott 1995: BIE, De Jong and Fresnel-zone methods compared for steps perpendicular and parallel to the path and for strips. BIE cost was "several orders of magnitude greater" than the formula methods. De Jong "agreed well … except in one case of propagation over a strip when the grazing angle was very small". The Fresnel-zone method "adequately described the general trends." — [Hothersall & Harriott 1995](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.412136)

**Fresnel-zone weighting in engineering and aircraft models**
- [FT] Harmonoise: the ground attenuation for each section between diffraction points is "a weighted average between two solutions, one for relatively flat ground and one for valley-shaped terrain", with Fresnel weights equal to "the fraction of the Fresnel ellipse … covered by the segment". Refraction is handled by conformal mapping of the ground vertices for a linear c(z). Against BEM for 60 m of irregular absorbing terrain (case 7), Nord2000 "deviates a few dB at low frequency"; Harmonoise did better. — [van Maercke & Salomons, NAG/DAGA 2009](https://pub.dega-akustik.de/NAG_DAGA_2009/data/articles/000250.pdf)
- [FT] Nord2000: flat ground with one surface type uses ray theory. Flat ground with several surface types uses "a modified Fresnel-zone method". Terrain is reduced to straight-line segments, with diffraction coefficients for terrain features. Validation covered 0–400 m with terrain and weather variation, but 400–1000 m only over flat terrain. — [Ahearn et al., Volpe DOT-VNTSC-FAA-12-07 (2012)](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/9687/dot_9687_DS1.pdf)
- [FT] Nord2000 against 544 cases (61 measured to 200 m, PE/benchmark references to 1000 m): the standard deviation of differences was 1 dB to 400 m and 2 dB at 600–1000 m over flat ground. "Fresnel-zones introduced in Nord2000 have lead to essential improvement … and solved problems of discontinuity in computation results." — [Kragh, Vejdirektoratet Rapport 335 (2011)](https://www.vejdirektoratet.dk/api/drupal/sites/default/files/publications/nord2000.pdf)
- [FT] sonAIR, the Empa aircraft model, computes ground reflection for spherical waves after Chessell, "with an extension for uneven terrain and varying ground properties using a Fresnel-zone approach (Hothersall and Harriott, 1995) and accounting for specific land cover". It uses a 5 m × 5 m DTM and the swissTLM3D land cover, with no lateral-attenuation term. At Payerne (F/A-18 departures, receivers at elevations 7.2°, 2.8°, 1.3°, 1.2° and 0.7°, out to 2.8 km) and Zurich approaches, deviations were "on the order of only ±1 dB at all measurement locations". AEDT was off by up to −4.5 dB and FLULA2 by 1.4–6.5 dB without lateral attenuation. — [Wunderli et al., JASA 151, 3140 (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1121/10.0010419)
- [ABS] sonAIR's large validation (~20,000 flights, 1.8–53 km): mean differences below ±1 dB, SD 1.7–2.4 dB. "A sensitivity analysis on the input data found the quality and level of detail of the land cover data to be critical for the simulation accuracy"; atmospheric profiles and buildings were not significant. — [Jäger et al., Noise Mapping 8 (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1515/noise-2021-0007)
- [ABS] ACRP Web-Only Document 32 (2017) developed single- and mixed-impedance ground methods for AEDT. Its body (theory, validation data, findings) could not be retrieved. — [TRB/ACRP WOD 32](https://crp.trb.org/acrpwebresource13/?p=7494); [NAP 24822](https://www.nap.edu/catalog/24822)
- [FT] A Volpe model-to-model study ran Nord2000 for aircraft over mixed ground (0–600 m hard then soft, and the reverse). Hard ground everywhere raised LAMAX by up to 2.0 dB over soft. Nord2000's effective flow resistivity classes are A 12.5, B 31.5, C 80, D 200, E 500 (compacted field and gravel), F 2000 (gravel road, parking lot), G 20,000 (most asphalt) and H 200,000 kPa·s/m² (dense asphalt, concrete, water), all through Delany–Bazley. — [Ahearn et al. 2012](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/9687/dot_9687_DS1.pdf)

**Full-wave references**
- [ABS] Craddock & White 1992 (finite-element WAPE): solving each range step with a UL rather than an LU decomposition "allows the change in impedance to affect only a single element in the decomposition", so the earlier work is reused at every step. Results "match the published data quite well" for abrupt impedance changes. — [Craddock & White 1992](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.403758)
- [ABS] Robertson, Schlatter & Siegmann 1996: NASA implicit finite-difference PE with range-variable impedance, with an accuracy benchmark, applied to paths crossing a flat lake. — [Robertson et al. 1996](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.414653)
- [ABS] Kirby 2022: a semi-analytic FEM (exact wave equation in a stratified atmosphere) extended to multiple impedance discontinuities by point collocation of the axial boundary conditions, and compared with other methods. — [Kirby 2022](https://consensus.app/papers/details/eaea50a1859755f9acaee3cc7c2d6ad8/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- [ABS] Lihoreau et al. 2006: mean-wind WAPE with impedance jumps, thin screens and topography, coupled to a micrometeorological model and compared with field data. — [Lihoreau et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.2204455)
- [ABS] Rosenbaum et al. 2010 (Volpe/FAA): a hybrid PE–FFP aviation-noise model over uneven terrain with mixed impedance and range-dependent meteorology, for a moving broadband source. — [Rosenbaum et al. 2010](https://consensus.app/papers/details/d40ad26526f35696b6fac1445e2fbdc6/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

### Inferences
- **[CALC] Magnitude of a hard patch inside a flush receiver's zone**, pressure-squared Fresnel weighting (AT2026's form). A fraction w of the zone at σ = 20,000 inside σ = 200 raises the level over all-soft ground by:

  | elev | w | 63 | 125 | 250 | 500 | 1k | 2k | 4k Hz |
  | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
  | 2° | 0.10 | 0.6 | 1.2 | 2.2 | 3.8 | 5.9 | 8.0 | 9.6 |
  | 2° | 0.25 | 1.4 | 2.5 | 4.2 | 6.6 | 9.1 | 11.5 | 13.3 |
  | 5° | 0.10 | 0.2 | 0.4 | 0.7 | 1.3 | 2.3 | 3.7 | 5.2 |
  | 5° | 0.25 | 0.5 | 0.9 | 1.6 | 2.7 | 4.4 | 6.4 | 8.3 |

  A 2–6 dB excess at 200–2000 Hz near grazing, which is what line x = −5115 ft (mics 42–44) shows, therefore needs only about 10–25 % hard or compacted cover in the relevant zone. Two things make that plausible: at 2° the 1 kHz zone is only 34–130 m long (F = 1/16 to 1/4, prior notes), and a few meters wide, so a vehicle track, a graded pad or a taxiway shoulder that close to the microphones would do it.
- **[INF] How to tell a hard patch from a uniform hard site.** A patch gives an excess that varies from line to line and depends on bearing: the zone is a thin ellipse pointing at the source, so a patch up-path for one track azimuth may be outside the zone for another. A uniformly harder site moves every line the same way. Because each band's zone has a different length, a patch at distance d from the microphones affects only bands whose zone reaches d: the low bands at the shallowest angles for far patches, every band for near ones. The band/elevation pattern of the excess on lines 42–44 therefore encodes the patch's distance.
- **[INF] Flush receiver inside the transition.** All the studied geometries have the discontinuity between a source and a receiver well above the ground. For a flush receiver, the specular point coincides with the receiver and the zone starts there, so any discontinuity up-path is inside the zone at low frequency. Boulanger's 70 % result says edge diffraction moves the effective transition. Lam & Monazzam say semi-empirical methods degrade exactly in this regime (low receiver, grazing). A ray/footprint code can do the Fresnel weighting cheaply. The PE is the right arbiter, since the NICEOPS PE already carries the impedance condition as a ground row.
- **[INF] PE implementation cost.** In NICEOPS's finite-difference PE, the ground admittance enters only the ground row. Craddock & White's point carries over: a range-dependent β changes one row per step. The split-step Padé march already re-solves a tridiagonal system per term per step, so a per-step β should cost almost nothing. Crank–Nicolson's once-per-run factorization would need either refactoring or the UL trick. (Inferred from the CLAUDE.md description of `pe.cpp`; not checked in code.)

### Gaps
- No published validation of any mixed-ground method for an elevated source at 0–10° and ranges of hundreds of meters, against PE, BEM or measurement, was found. sonAIR's ±1 dB is an end-to-end aircraft result, not an isolated test of mixed ground.
- Full texts of Rasmussen 1982, Daigle et al. 1985, Boulanger et al. 1997, Hothersall & Harriott 1995 and Harriott, Chandler-Wilde & Hothersall 1991 ("Long-distance sound propagation over an impedance discontinuity", [JSV](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-460x(91)90472-v)) were not obtained. The IOA proceedings copies returned 404 or a block.
- The ACRP WOD 32 body (the only aircraft-specific mixed-ground study) and the Nord2000 comprehensive model report (exact F_λ and terrain rules) could not be downloaded; both hosts blocked scripted access.
- No quantified comparison of the Fresnel-zone method with PE for a flush receiver was found.

## 2. Gentle terrain undulation and small slopes (tens of cm to a few m over hundreds of meters) at grazing

### Takeaway
The published terrain work concerns hills, valleys and berms (slopes of 5–22°, relief of tens to hundreds of meters). No study was found that quantifies relief of tens of centimeters to a few meters at grazing incidence. For a flush receiver the dominant first-order effect is simple: the local mean ground plane within the Fresnel zone tilts the effective grazing angle. By my calculation, a ±0.3° tilt (about 0.5 m over 100 m) moves the level by about ±1 dB at 2° elevation over soft ground, and a ±1° tilt by +3/−5.5 dB. This is the same size as the 0.7–1.7 dB line-to-line spread at 2–5°.

### Cited Findings
- [ABS] Parakkal, Gilbert, Di & Bass 2010: the generalized polar-coordinate PE handles terrain whose local radius of curvature is much larger than a wavelength. A numerical example "shows that complex terrain can have a significant effect on low-frequency sound propagation, even when the slope angles are small." — [Parakkal et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.3495941)
- [ABS] Parakkal et al. 2012 (Beilis–Tappert PE): terrain becomes a multiplicative phase screen. The approximate flat-ground impedance condition "is sufficiently accurate" for slopes under 15–20°; beyond about 20° the narrow-angle approximation is the limit. — [Parakkal et al. 2012](https://consensus.app/papers/details/0b375494f9f153e0ac84ebf1705b77f8/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- [ABS] Sack & West 1995: the generalized terrain PE (GT-PE), a terrain-following transformation, agrees closely with an effective-barrier model for a simple hill. — [Sack & West 1995](https://consensus.app/papers/details/4c12fdc00a9c57cf9d8c1c0bbd531f68/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- [ABS] Nyborg et al. 2023 compared BTPE and GTPE for wind-turbine noise over terrain of varying complexity, ground impedance and wind, validated spectrally against loudspeaker and turbine measurements (open access; the body could not be fetched). — [Nyborg et al. 2023](https://consensus.app/papers/details/358d7b992d045b94b6ee29f60b55e8ee/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- [ABS] Khodr et al. 2020: a 3-D terrain-following PE checked against BEM over a Gaussian hill. "Transversal scattering occurs in the shadow zone of the obstacle where the two-dimensional parabolic equation underestimates the pressure amplitude." — [Khodr et al. 2020](https://consensus.app/papers/details/ec2387b75aee51cdbe7a79a49f7341dd/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- [ABS] Van Renterghem, Botteldooren & Lercher 2007 (rotated-Green's-function PE in an Alpine valley): within 3 dBA of measurement to 1 km in windless conditions. Terrain raised distant elevated points by up to 30 dBA against flat ground, and temperature profiles changed levels by −3 to +10 dBA. — [Van Renterghem et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.2717765)
- [ABS] Waxler et al. 2022 (infrasound PE with topography; impedance condition with vertical and radial components): reflections from steep slopes can move a path between atmospheric ducts. — [Waxler et al. 2022](https://consensus.app/papers/details/aabc7f71600a5e7abcbbf128e571da7d/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- [ABS] Guibard et al. 2022: a PE with topography, ground and meteorology, validated in the French Alps; "topography being the most significant factor". — [Guibard et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1121/10.0011545)
- [FT] Harmonoise treats an arbitrary profile with Fresnel weights per ground segment between diffraction points, blending a flat solution with a valley solution. Its BEM comparison for a 60 m irregular absorbing profile showed Nord2000 off by "a few dB at low frequency". — [van Maercke & Salomons 2009](https://pub.dega-akustik.de/NAG_DAGA_2009/data/articles/000250.pdf)
- [FT] sonAIR's terrain handling is the Fresnel-zone approach on a 5 m DTM. Its definition of elevation angle uses the terrain below the propagation path, and it gives the same angle as ECAC Doc. 29 only on flat terrain. — [Wunderli et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1121/10.0010419)
- [ABS] Berthelot, Pierce et al. (NASA grant reports, Georgia Tech, 1986–87) studied "irregular topography" for "low-altitude air vehicles … at angles close to grazing incidence", including curved surfaces of finite impedance, by scale-model spark experiments at 5–40 kHz. — [NASA 19870018047](http://hdl.handle.net/2060/19870018047)

### Inferences
- **[CALC] Tilt sensitivity, flush receiver, σ = 200 (Delany–Bazley).** Level change when the local ground plane inside the zone tilts toward (+) or away from (−) the source by δ, i.e. ψ_eff = ψ ± δ:

  | ψ | δ | 63 | 250 | 1k | 4k Hz |
  | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
  | 2° | 0.1° | +0.18/−0.20 | +0.30/−0.32 | +0.37/−0.39 | +0.40/−0.42 |
  | 2° | 0.3° | +0.50/−0.64 | +0.85/−1.04 | +1.05/−1.25 | +1.13/−1.33 |
  | 2° | 0.6° | +0.89/−1.50 | +1.55/−2.34 | +1.95/−2.77 | +2.11/−2.93 |
  | 2° | 1.0° | +1.31/−3.23 | +2.33/−4.74 | +2.98/−5.47 | +3.24/−5.73 |
  | 5° | 0.3° | +0.10/−0.11 | +0.22/−0.25 | +0.36/−0.39 | +0.43/−0.46 |
  | 5° | 1.0° | +0.29/−0.43 | +0.67/−0.94 | +1.09/−1.43 | +1.32/−1.66 |

  The effect scales roughly as δ/ψ, so it is three to four times larger at 2° than at 5°. That matches the observed pattern: line-to-line spread of 0.2–0.3 dB at 10–40° but 0.7–1.7 dB at 2–5°. 0.3° is 0.5 m of rise over 100 m, and 0.6° is 1 m. "Desert airfield flat" relief easily reaches this over a 100–900 m zone.
- **[INF] The mean plane is band-dependent.** The relevant tilt is that of the mean plane through the Fresnel zone for that band and elevation, not the tilt at the microphone. Low bands average over hundreds of meters, high bands over tens. A cross-track line built on a gentle swale or crown sees a different tilt for every bearing, which gives line constants that vary with source azimuth.
- **[INF] Survey requirement.** To keep the tilt term below about 0.3 dB at 2°, the zone's mean slope has to be known to about 0.1°, i.e. ±0.2 m over 100 m along each propagation bearing. A 1–5 m DEM (lidar or USGS 3DEP where available) or an RTK survey of each line's up-path ground resolves this. Microphone heights alone do not.
- **[INF] Cheapest implementation in a ray/footprint code.** For each microphone and each pair (ψ, f), fit a least-squares plane to the DEM inside the band's Fresnel ellipse and evaluate the reflection there: ψ_eff, plus the mean plane's height for a pole microphone. This is the Harmonoise/Nord2000 "mean plane per segment" idea reduced to one segment. It can be tabulated per microphone by bearing and elevation, and costs nothing in the loop.

### Gaps
- No quantitative study of relief of decimeters to meters at 0–10° from an elevated source was found, either in PE/BEM or in measurements. The tilt table above is mine.
- The Nord2000 terrain-ground interaction rules (how Fresnel zones are combined over concave/convex segments) and Harmonoise's flat/valley weighting formula were described in the sources but not quoted; the primary reports were not obtained.

## 3. Desert, dry-soil and airfield surfaces: flow resistivity, porosity, variability, and acoustic measurements at desert ranges

### Takeaway
No acoustic ground-impedance measurement at a desert test range (Edwards, White Sands, Yuma, Nevada, a playa) was found in any accessible source; it remains a gap. The usable analogues span two orders of magnitude: dry fine sand about 300 ± 150 kPa·s/m² (Ω ≈ 0.3), compacted soil 1500 ± 700 (Ω ≈ 0.2), crusted arable land 2250, gravel road or parking lot about 2000, sealed and compacted surfaces ≥ 20,000. A flyover-fitted "concrete runway" came out at only 750, which shows how a finite hard strip inside the zone reads as an intermediate effective σ.

### Cited Findings
- [ABS] Embleton, Piercy & Daigle 1983: effective flow resistivity ranges from new snow (10–30 cgs rayl, i.e. kPa·s/m²) through grass (150–300) to mature asphalt (30,000). The viscous and thermal boundary layer caps even the hardest surface at 10⁵–10⁶ rayls, "appropriate to … the paint-sealed surface of a thick slab of reinforced concrete". — [Embleton et al. 1983](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.390029)
- [FT] AT2026's proposed classes (porosity / effective σ in kPa·s/m² / layer depth): 4 forest floors and coarse sand 0.5 ± 0.1 / 50 ± 40 / 0.06 ± 0.04 m; 5 uncompacted grass 0.25 ± 0.15 / 250 ± 100 / 0.05; **6 fine sand (dry) 0.3 ± 0.1 / 300 ± 150 / 0.05**; **7 compacted soil or maintained grass 0.2 ± 0.1 / 1500 ± 700 / ∞**; 8 compacted gravel road, hot-rolled asphalt, sealed concrete ≤ 0.1 / ≥ 20,000 / ∞. NORDTEST site 24 "arable" fitted 2250 with Ω = 0.5, "suggests that it might have had a surface crust due to weathering". For gravel and sand, Delany–Bazley fitting error was 15.2 against 9.4–9.8 for slit-pore and variable-porosity models. — [Attenborough & Taherzadeh 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apacoust.2025.111067)
- [FT] Nord2000 classes E 500 ("compacted field and gravel"), F 2000 ("gravel road, parking lot, ISO 10844 asphalt"), G 20,000 ("most common asphalt"). — [Ahearn et al. 2012](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/9687/dot_9687_DS1.pdf)
- [FT] **Willshire's T-38A flyover experiment (NASA Langley, 28 runs at 10–160 m altitude, 1.2 m microphones over grass parallel to a 2500 m concrete runway with 8 microphones along it).** Fitting the Chien–Soroka/Delany–Bazley ground term to near/far spectra gave σ = **62.5 kPa·s/m²** for the grass (search range 30–500) with coherence constant a = 0.1. Fitting the grass/concrete pair differences gave σ = **750 kPa·s/m²** for "concrete" (search range 125–1000). Willshire attributes the scatter to atmospheric gradients and fluctuations, sampling, "the finite dimensions of the test surfaces", and different atmospheric conditions over grass and runway. — [Willshire, NASA TP-1747 (1980)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19810006274/downloads/19810006274.pdf)
- [ABS] Dickinson & Doak 1970: normal impedance at 200–1000 Hz ranged from 3 − 3i on soft grass to 7 − 17i on "rock covered with chippings". A little moisture slightly increased the absorption of sand. — [Dickinson & Doak 1970](https://consensus.app/papers/details/f6bd40734c59582b88d59f53536c2a67/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- [ABS] ARL's ANSI S1.18 template measurements at Blossom Point (grass, asphalt, gravel road; 150 Hz–2.7 kHz; the gravel road was saturated during the test). Over grass the closest templates were 1-parameter σ = 50 and 2-parameter σe = 32 kPa·s/m², αe = 3 m⁻¹. — [Alberts, Coleman & Noble, ARL report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-D101-PURL-gpo4858/pdf/GOVPUB-D101-PURL-gpo4858.pdf)
- [FT] Moisture (from prior notes, not repeated in detail): fitted σ rises with moisture. Wet/dry ratios are 1.1–3.5 for fine sand and 5–9.8 for coarse sand. Cramond & Don report up to 7.5x between 10 % and 35 % moisture. — [AT2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apacoust.2025.111067); [Cramond & Don 1987](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.395565)

  Going from 200 (NICEOPS's site value) to compacted soil at 1500–2000 is worth +3.4 to +10 dB at 2° across 63 Hz–1 kHz, and +1.5 to +7 dB at 5°. A desert airfield with compacted or crusted areas is therefore a strong candidate for "ground harder than modeled near grazing".
- [INF] Willshire's 750 kPa·s/m² for a concrete runway, against ≥ 20,000 for real sealed concrete, is what Fresnel-zone mixing predicts. A runway about 45 m wide, with grass beyond, cannot fill a zone hundreds of meters long and tens of meters wide at low elevation, so the single-σ fit lands in between. (The runway width is not stated in the text read; the figure is inferred.) It is the clearest published flyover demonstration that a fitted effective σ is a property of the zone, not of the surface under the microphone.
- [INF] Desert soils are typically dry, which by the moisture literature should make them softer than the same soil wet. But crusting, desert pavement (packed gravel lag) and vehicle compaction push them toward classes 7–8. Without a survey the prior should be broad: about 200–5000 kPa·s/m², Ω about 0.1–0.4.

### Gaps
- No acoustic impedance or flow-resistivity measurement for desert soil, a playa or desert pavement, and no grazing ground-effect measurement at a US desert range (Edwards, White Sands, Yuma, Nevada), was found in the searched literature, NTRS or DTIC.
- Embleton et al. 1983's full table (it lists intermediate surfaces such as roadside dirt and hard-packed sandy silt) was not obtained; only its abstract's three anchor values are cited.
- Seasonal or diurnal variability of dry desert soils (dew, rain events) was not found.

## 4. In-situ inference of ground impedance from long-range or flyover data

### Takeaway
There is direct precedent for exactly this: Willshire 1980 fitted σ for grass and for concrete from flyover data, using a near reference microphone and same-emission-angle pairing. Modern inversions (MLE/MAP and Bayesian, direct-impedance and spectral-band fitting) consistently find three things. Single-octave or low-frequency data identify impedance poorly. Priors and multi-band fitting stabilise the inversion. The fitted parameters are tied to the impedance model used. The cleanest source-independent observable available in the 2017 data is the level difference between a flush board and a nearby 1.2 m pole at the same emission time. By my calculation it varies by 6–20 dB between σ = 200 and 2000 at 0.8–2 kHz at 2–5° elevation, but by less than 3 dB at or below 250 Hz.

### Cited Findings
- [FT] Willshire's two analysis methods: (1) **near/far**: a reference spectrum from a 10 m microphone over concrete near the track, corrected for its own ground effect, spreading and absorption, then compared with down-range 1.2 m microphones at the same source emission angle (122.5°); (2) **direct comparison** of equidistant microphones over grass and over concrete. The near/far method was more reliable, and the direct method was "the inferior of the two", with large scatter at 1600 m. Predicted frequencies of maximum attenuation matched (200–400 Hz, more than 20 dB at low elevation), but predicted amplitudes were generally smaller than measured. Ground effects were near zero above 10–15° elevation, and attenuation grew with slant range below 750 m. — [Willshire, NASA TP-1747](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19810006274/downloads/19810006274.pdf)
- [ABS] Embleton, Piercy & Olson 1976: near-grazing measurements from 1 to 1000 ft over grass "depend on, and conversely provide a means of estimating, the values of ground impedance for waves at near-grazing angles", in agreement with conventional measurements at other angles. — [Embleton et al. 1976](https://consensus.app/papers/details/dfafdc49bf97598e8e2efc3e5d3ba405/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- [ABS] Soh et al. 2010: a direct method that measures impedance at several locations along a propagation path using the experiment's own broadcast signals. It agreed with model-based least squares at 10 m and was demonstrated over 400 m of an agricultural field. — [Soh et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.3501116)
- [ABS] Li, Attenborough et al. 1991: inverting the near-grazing ground effect (50–2000 Hz, 5–8° grazing, 50–175 m, two sites with different impedance) in a linear-gradient atmosphere for *source height*, given range. — [Li et al. 1991](https://consensus.app/papers/details/9cac5bebc004596d94623f499b068423/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- [ABS] Parry et al. 2020 (MLE vs MAP over grassland, homogeneous atmosphere): a prior "greatly improves inference accuracy". "The use of a single octave band frequency window does not improve the quality of inference, whereas combinations of several low frequency octave bands do." The exact ground values matter less beyond 500 m. — [Parry et al. 2020](https://consensus.app/papers/details/1fe3a4f52abf57b2b1e64c5506ab7cfa/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- [ABS] Li et al. 2014: inverting the measured transfer function for impedance is non-unique, because the boundary-loss factor F(w) admits several solutions for w. They map the zeros and saddle points of F to separate the basins of plausible solutions. — [Li et al. 2014](https://consensus.app/papers/details/c8eedb1795ca5a01bab56c4df7b44611/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- [ABS] Nyborg et al. 2021: the NORDTEST geometry works at 1.75 m but "gives inconsistent results" at double the separation and heights. A flow resistivity "obtained with an impedance model can only be used with that specific impedance model". They derive a Delany–Bazley ↔ Attenborough four-parameter relation. — [Nyborg et al. 2021](https://consensus.app/papers/details/35ccadf5fc735b84a722d69996b815f7/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- [ABS] Ma et al. 2026: per-third-octave ("spectral-band") effective σ, against one global-band fit, "significantly" reduces the misfit of SPL differences, with an empirical global↔spectral relation. — [Ma et al. 2026](https://consensus.app/papers/details/97cc94cdfd8554798b6fcf99407373aa/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- [ABS] Guillaume et al. 2015: narrow-band fitting of σ and effective thickness from two-microphone spectra on three grounds in winter and summer, analyzed for spatial and temporal variability and its impact on SPL. — [Guillaume et al. 2015](https://consensus.app/papers/details/1430765faebd5124a549aa623cb230da/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- [ABS] Eser et al. 2023: Bayesian free-field inference of surface admittance with sequential frequency transfer. At least four independent measurements gave accurate absorption and uncertainty, "particularly for frequencies above 600 Hz". — [Eser et al. 2023](https://consensus.app/papers/details/d6ec84273e7159b0a9a6d0992e40f533/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- [ABS] Jayanthi, Snellen & Amiri-Simkooei 2026: a microphone array yields a flyover spectrogram with ground reflections largely removed. A ground-effect model then builds semi-synthetic spectrograms, checked against single microphones at two heights, and the ground effect is quantified per flyover. Corrections reduced level differences by up to 2.3 dBA at higher elevations and about 1.8 dBA at lower. — [Jayanthi et al., CEAS Aeronaut. J. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13272-026-00947-1)
- [ABS] Nesbitt, Lan & Hunkler 2020: ground-plane corrections to 1.2 m microphone levels for certification. "One of the key motivations … is to reduce dependence on ground characteristics." — [Nesbitt et al., AIAA 2020-2613](https://doi.org/10.2514/6.2020-2613)
- [FT] (from prior notes) Kruse: the two-microphone method is reliable only above about 400 Hz and not on hard ground, because the level difference "will not depend much on the flow resistivity" below that. — [Kruse dissertation](http://www.akustik.uni-oldenburg.de/literatur/Kruse/Dissertation_Kruse_Ohne_Artikel.pdf)

### Inferences
- **[CALC] Identifiability from the existing pole/board pairs.** Pole (1.2 m) minus flush receiver, third-octave averaged, plane wave, DB model, dB:

  | elev | σ | 125 | 250 | 500 | 800 | 1k | 1.6k | 2k | 3.15k Hz |

  | 2° | 200 | −0.9 | −2.8 | −1.1 | 7.0 | 10.5 | 16.5 | 18.4 | 17.6 |
  | 2° | 500 | −0.5 | −1.6 | −4.4 | 0.2 | 4.1 | 11.2 | 13.4 | 13.5 |

  At 0.5–3 kHz and 2–5° the difference swings by 5–15 dB between σ = 200 and 2000. At or below 250 Hz it moves by under about 3 dB, which is Kruse's low-frequency blindness again. The pair therefore identifies the impedance at mid and high frequency well, and the low-band impedance (where the flush-receiver discrepancy is largest) mainly through the model's frequency extrapolation. Hence the prior notes' emphasis on admissible models.
- **[INF] Why the pair difference is the right observable.** At one reception time the pole and the board see essentially the same emission angle and spreading, so source directivity, source-model bias and the trajectory cancel. That is the "uniform path effect" the line-constant comparisons cannot separate. Three caveats. (a) The board is a 38 cm plate, so use NICEOPS's plate table, not a bare-soil flush model, for the board side. (b) The pole's interference dips are filled by turbulent decorrelation from about 250 Hz up (prior notes), so a coherence parameter has to be fitted jointly, as Willshire's a = 0.1 was. (c) The two receivers sample different ground. The pole's specular point is 34 m (2°) or 14 m (5°) in front of the mast, and its 1 kHz zone is 45–180 m long, while the board's zone starts at the board. A joint fit gives the impedance of the up-path strip shared by both zones.
- **[INF] Second observable: a near/far reference, after Willshire.** A board directly under or near the track (elevation over 30–40°, where the ground term barely depends on σ) gives the source spectrum at the same emission angle. Down-range boards at 2–10° then give the absolute ground effect per line. This is a direct analogue of the existing sphere-building, so an elevation-dependent residual there is already this measurement. Fitting σ, Ω (or σe, αe) and a coherence factor per line, with a prior centerd on AT2026 classes 6–7, is the in-situ inversion. Per Parry, combine several bands; per Nyborg, fit in the impedance model NICEOPS will use.
- **[INF] What a cheap site survey should add,** in order of value per cost: (1) a DEM or RTK profile along each line's up-path ground (section 2: the tilt term); (2) a photographic or GIS map of hard and compacted areas within about 1 km up-path of each line (section 1: the hard-patch term); (3) a few ANSI S1.18/NORDTEST level-difference measurements on each surface type, at the same season and time of day as the flights. Item 3 constrains the 400 Hz–3 kHz impedance only (Kruse) and should be treated as a prior, not as the answer for 63–250 Hz.

### Gaps
- The 2017 flight-test set-up (actual pole/board co-location and spacing) was not examined here. Whether pole–board pairs exist on the lines in question, especially 42–44, needs checking against the flight cards.
- No paper was found that inverts ground parameters from flyover data with a modern admissible model and quantified posterior uncertainty. Willshire 1980 (single-parameter, least squares) is the only flyover precedent found.
- The full texts of Jayanthi 2026, Nesbitt 2020 and Go et al. 2024 (next section) were paywalled.

## 5. Ground-board (flush plate) microphones near grazing over different ground types

### Takeaway
The plate's departure from +6 dB depends on the surrounding ground. Over grass, narrow-band deviations exceed 3 dB; the microphone body itself matters from 3 kHz. No published correction curve was obtained for hard or compacted surroundings. Physically, the deviation should shrink as the surrounding ground hardens, so a uniform plate correction derived for soft ground would bias boards that sit on harder patches.

### Cited Findings
- [ABS] Go, Kingan, Schmid & Hall 2024: a 0.4 m circular thin metal board on simulated grass (anechoic) and on an outdoor grass field. Edge diffraction, from the board's thickness or the "finite impedance of the surrounding ground", makes the level vary over the board's top surface and depart from +6 dB. Narrow-band deviations exceed 3 dB, and the microphone body affects measurements from 3 kHz. Flush, inverted and side-set mountings were compared. — [Go et al., JSV (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsv.2024.118432)
- [ABS] Nesbitt et al. 2020: ground-plane measurements corrected to 1.2 m equivalents, from laboratory, flyover, analytical and numerical work; the motivation is reduced ground dependence. — [Nesbitt et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.2514/6.2020-2613)
- [FT] (prior notes) 2017 and 2019 NASA practice: GRAS 67AX flush in a 15 in (38 cm) board, offset from center, after ARP 4055. panam's BEM plate-on-soft-ground model raised the B407 spheres by 0.8–2.6 dB at 0.5–2 kHz, mostly toward the sidelines. — see `aircraft_low_elevation_practice.md` §3–4.

### Inferences
- [INF] NICEOPS's plate table (field at the plate top, plus K = plate over bare ground, per band and azimuth) assumes the site impedance around the board. If a board sits on a compacted patch (σ 1500–2000) while the table assumes σ = 200, two errors add. The ground-effect term is too soft (section 3 table: +3 to +10 dB at 2°). K is also too large, because over hard ground the plate adds almost nothing above the ground's own near-+6 dB. The two partly cancel, so the net error is smaller than the ground term alone. Computing the plate table at two σ values would bound this.
- [INF] Because the board's own Fresnel zone begins at the plate, ground a few meters up-path of a board matters most to its high bands at low elevation (the 1 kHz zone at 5° is about 6–22 m long). A pad of disturbed or compacted earth from installing the board would raise those bands, and only for the boards it surrounds.

### Gaps
- No correction curves for a plate on hard or compacted ground, or on sand, were obtained. The Acoustics Australia 2026 correction-curve paper and the AIAA 2023-4165 paper (both cited in the prior notes) were again inaccessible.
- No study was found of how a plate behaves at 0–5° elevation specifically, as opposed to the 15–90° typical of certification.
