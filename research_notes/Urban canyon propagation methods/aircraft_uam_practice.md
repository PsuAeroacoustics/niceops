# Urban effects in aircraft, rotorcraft, UAM/eVTOL, drone and sonic-boom noise prediction: tools, methods and measured/predicted magnitudes

Scope note: these notes cover what production and research *aerial-source* tools do about buildings, and what dB effects have been measured or predicted. Generic urban-acoustics methods (street-canyon theory, PSTD, radiosity for road traffic) are covered by other researchers and appear here only where they were applied to aerial sources. Several primary PDFs were read in full (sonAIR/Acta Acustica 2025, Ramseier et al. 2024, Lugten et al. Inter-Noise pilot, NASA/TP-2020-5007433, Yunus et al. 2023/2024, Malbéqui 2023, Caillet et al. 2025, Cebrián Gómez & Banitsas 2026, Feng & Kang 2026); others are cited from abstracts only, and that is flagged.

## 1. Production tools: what algorithm each uses for buildings, and their stated limitations

### Takeaway
No regulatory/best-practice aircraft or rotorcraft noise tool (AEDT, FLULA2, ECAC Doc 29 / CNOSSOS-EU aircraft, NORAH2 as used, RNM, AAM) models buildings in routine use; they do terrain line-of-sight at most, plus an empirical lateral-attenuation term. The one production tool that can do buildings, Empa's sonAIR (via its sonX propagation model), does it with Maekawa/Pierce barrier diffraction in the vertical plane only (no lateral diffraction around building edges), incoherent third-order building reflections, and a precomputed attenuation database on an airspace grid, and its own authors recommend *not* using buildings for large-scale mapping because of cost (~2 weeks per airport for the base database) and rough, receiver-grid-dependent contours.

### Cited Findings

**sonAIR / sonX (Empa, Switzerland) — the reference production case**
- sonAIR is a spectral (1/3-octave, 25 Hz–5 kHz) time-step model with 3D (longitudinal and lateral) emission directivity; propagation is "precalculated for all combinations of sources and receivers within a grid" using the physically based model sonX, which handles geometric divergence, absorption, ground reflection and "shielding from terrain or buildings." — [Schalcher, Zellmann, Wunderli, Schäffer, Acta Acustica 9, 54 (2025), CC-BY](https://www.dora.lib4ri.ch/empa/dload/empa:42543/PDF/view) (DOI 10.1051/aacus/2025035)
- sonX is modular: BASIC (homogeneous atmosphere; divergence, ISO 9613-1 absorption, foliage, ground effect, shielding by terrain and/or buildings), REFLECT (reflections at buildings/noise barriers), FOREST (diffuse reflections from forests and cliffs), METEO (stratified atmosphere, shadow zones). Ground reflection uses spherical waves extended to uneven terrain and varying ground; barrier effects use "either the Maekawa or Pierce method." — [Schalcher et al. 2025](https://www.dora.lib4ri.ch/empa/dload/empa:42543/PDF/view)
- "For aircraft noise, the Maekawa approach is preferred as it is computationally less demanding." — [Ramseier, Schalcher, Wunderli, Schäffer, TR Part D 133, 104279 (2024), CC-BY](https://www.dora.lib4ri.ch/empa/dload/empa:38392/PDF/view)
- REFLECT builds on Heutschi's (2009) coherent and scattering reflection solutions; for aircraft the **incoherent** model is used (intensities, all frequencies at once, coarser facade discretization), justified because "reflecting facades behave diffusely starting from around 600 Hz and above." **Visibility checks instead of diffraction calculations** are used for the reflected paths; "energy exchanges up to the third order" (source–reflector, reflector–reflector, reflector–receiver). — [Ramseier et al. 2024](https://www.dora.lib4ri.ch/empa/dload/empa:38392/PDF/view)
- **No lateral diffraction**: "no lateral sound propagation paths around buildings are considered. In reality, the level behind the building increases earlier due to sound paths diffracted laterally at the building edge. These contributions are not considered in sonAIR for performance reasons, even more so as fast moving sources tend to average out this contribution over time." — [Ramseier et al. 2024](https://www.dora.lib4ri.ch/empa/dload/empa:38392/PDF/view)
- **Hybrid simple/complex propagation with a 15° elevation criterion**: "For high-altitude aircraft with direct and unshielded sound propagation paths, 'on-the-fly' attenuation is calculated during simulations, without precalculating cell grid points. In complex propagation situations, attenuation values are precalculated. The simplified model applies when the aircraft angle exceeds 15° above the horizon" (horizon = line of sight over obstacles, terrain and/or buildings, plus a 15° offset). — [Schalcher et al. 2025](https://www.dora.lib4ri.ch/empa/dload/empa:42543/PDF/view)
- **Airspace cell grid / attenuation database**: airspace is divided into cells along the imported trajectories; attenuations are computed at cell corners (virtual source positions) and, during single-flight simulation, "weighted by the aircraft position within the cell. Cell sizes are dynamically adjusted based on altitude and horizontal distance to receivers." ~4 million cell grid points for Geneva (GVA); the database is reusable and incrementally extendable, but changed inputs (ground cover, settings) require a full recalculation. — [Schalcher et al. 2025](https://www.dora.lib4ri.ch/empa/dload/empa:42543/PDF/view)
- **Cost**: GVA2017 setup: 57 × 54 km area, 25 m terrain/ground-cover raster, 210 tiles of 4 × 4 km, 136,800 receivers (150 m grid), 43,037 trajectories. "The precalculation of attenuation for an airport the size of GVA requires approximately two weeks" on two 10–14-core Xeon workstations plus a shared 2 × 24-core machine; incremental additions "take at most a few days." Adding METEO doubles it: "from 2 weeks (using only the BASIC module) to 4 weeks." — [Schalcher et al. 2025](https://www.dora.lib4ri.ch/empa/dload/empa:42543/PDF/view)
- Per-flight cost at Meiringen: BASIC (with or without buildings) "a few minutes for the propagation calculation of a single flight on a single receiver point"; REFLECT adds 10–20 min; FOREST raises it to ~2 h; attenuations for 490 trajectories took "about two to three weeks" on an 8-core i7 workstation; the reflection implementation "is not fully optimized for aircraft noise." — [Ramseier et al. 2024](https://www.dora.lib4ri.ch/empa/dload/empa:38392/PDF/view)
- **Stated limitations/recommendation**: buildings create "irregular 'noise islands'"; "the coarse receiver grid (150 m × 150 m), similar in size to buildings, contributes to this irregularity"; buildings "may be of interest for special investigations, when receiver points on facades are calculated, while they are not to be recommended for large-scale noise mapping." In the 2025 study, the building version included shielding only, no reflections. — [Schalcher et al. 2025](https://www.dora.lib4ri.ch/empa/dload/empa:42543/PDF/view); Ramseier et al. likewise: "extending the same calculations to areas of tens by tens of kilometers, while keeping appropriate domain resolutions is presently not feasible." — [Ramseier et al. 2024](https://www.dora.lib4ri.ch/empa/dload/empa:38392/PDF/view)
- sonX itself (outside sonAIR) uses a Fresnel-zone approach for uneven terrain/varying ground with frequency- and distance-dependent coherence loss, and is also used for shooting, road and railway noise models. — [Empa sonRAIL model description](https://www.empa.ch/web/s509/sonrail_model_description)
- License: no open-source license found; sonAIR/sonX are Empa tools (the papers are CC-BY, not the code). — [Empa sonAIR page](https://www.empa.ch/web/s509/sonair)

**FLULA2 (Swiss best-practice model)**
- Empirical model of ground exposure vs longitudinal emission angle (2D directivity) and distance; point source; A-weighted metrics only; propagation = spreading, absorption, **terrain shielding**, and lateral attenuation per SAE AIR 5662; cannot vary ground properties; "does not account for any shielding effects" by buildings. — [Schalcher et al. 2025](https://www.dora.lib4ri.ch/empa/dload/empa:42543/PDF/view)

**AEDT (FAA)**
- "Best-practice tools such as AEDT ... or FLULA2 ... are able to take shielding from terrain into account, however shielding or reflections from buildings cannot be simulated." — [Ramseier et al. 2024](https://www.dora.lib4ri.ch/empa/dload/empa:38392/PDF/view)
- AEDT terrain treatment is "limited to line-of-sight blockage and simple slant range adjustment," whereas NoiseMap/AAM has a more complex terrain/ground method. — [Volpe/DOT NoiseMap and AEDT Gap Analysis](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/35489/dot_35489_DS1.pdf) (from search summary; PDF not read in full)
- ACRP Web-Only Document 43 (Downing, Calton, Page, Rochat, 2019), "Improving AEDT Modeling for Aircraft Noise Reflection and Diffraction from Terrain and Manmade Structures," develops and recommends methods for AEDT and user guidance; finding: obstructing terrain/structures attenuate "with this attenuation increasing with the terrain and structures' size and proximity to either the source or the receiver," and "If gaps exist ... the potential benefits of acoustical shielding will be substantially reduced." — [NAP catalog 26897](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/26897/improving-aedt-modeling-for-aircraft-noise-reflection-and-diffraction-from-terrain-and-manmade-structures); [TRB project page](https://trb.org/ACRP/Blurbs/180058.aspx?em=1)
- For UAM, AEDT has no UAM performance models; fixed-point profile mode does not support helicopter lateral directivity (left/center/right). — [Rizzi et al., NASA/TP-2020-5007433](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205007433/downloads/NASA-TP-2020-5007433.pdf)

**RNM and AAM (NASA / Wyle / Blue Ridge Research; US DoD)**
- RNM predicts propagation "to receiver locations located on flat ground or varying terrain"; sources are measured/computed sound hemispheres. — [Lucas, Rotorcraft Noise Model, NASA NTRS 20040110391](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20040110391/downloads/20040110391.pdf) (search summary)
- AAM evolved from NMSim and RNM: source directivity, vectored thrust, curved-ray propagation, nonlinear propagation, Doppler, "interaction with varying terrain and ground reflections" — no building capability stated. — [search summary of AAM sources](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/35489/dot_35489_DS1.pdf)
- NASA/TP-2020-5007433 lists AAM (with NMAP) as the DoD tool for helicopters/tiltrotors and identifies as a gap: "There is a need to better integrate advanced propagation tools capable of handling wind and temperature effects (refraction) and urban environments (reflection and diffraction) in system noise prediction and auralization tools." — [Rizzi et al. 2020](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205007433/downloads/NASA-TP-2020-5007433.pdf)

**ECAC Doc 29 / CNOSSOS-EU (aircraft), NORAH2, Nord2000**
- CNOSSOS-EU standardizes road/rail/industry propagation but "when it comes to aircrafts, it explicitly defers to ECAC Doc. 29"; Doc 29 = segmented NPD method with corrections for distance, absorption and ground effects. — [Cebrián Gómez & Banitsas, Aerospace 13, 37 (2026)](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/32588/1/FullText.pdf)
- NORAH2 (EASA rotorcraft model): hemisphere-based source plus database; "fully compliant with CNOSSOS in its propagation treatment," integrating "a CNOSSOS-EU-based diffraction scheme within a newly combined propagation block (with ground reflection)"; validation includes "a like-for-like comparison with a Nord2000-based reference," agreement "generally good," "with larger differences limited to heavily screened or long-range, low-noise situations." EASA's upgrade brief targets extending propagation "to urban/terrain/vegetation and weather contexts." — [Cebrián Gómez & Banitsas 2026](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/32588/1/FullText.pdf)
- In the first published urban NORAH2 application (Cannes, 556 operations, 8.3 × 2.5 km grid, receivers 1.2 m): "Explicit building geometry was not included in the terrain representation; while building heights could in principle be incorporated, meaningful façade-level modelling would require grid resolutions incompatible with town-scale noise mapping and standard computational practice." — [Cebrián Gómez & Banitsas 2026](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/32588/1/FullText.pdf)
- Nord2000 "is a propagation framework (not a rotorcraft source model); it is often used as the reference outdoor propagation scheme in validations." — [Cebrián Gómez & Banitsas 2026](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/32588/1/FullText.pdf)

**Airbus Helicopters / Bruitparif / Univ. Gustave Eiffel (MOTUS, Paris Issy heliport) — industrial urban helicopter chain**
- Simulations used "an AH tool coupled with Noise Modelling software, which is based on CNOSSOS-EU standard, and which have been adapted so as to take into account the effects of buildings on sound propagation (masking, reflections, refractions…) of aerial sources, such as helicopters"; prior AH work "showed that taking into account buildings (e.g. screening effect) can significantly affect noise levels compared with standard ways of computing aviation noise impacts." — [Caillet et al., Research Square preprint 2025 (CC-BY 4.0), published CEAS Aeronaut. J. 2026](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-7047184/latest.pdf)
- NoiseModelling (the open-source engine) is **GPL-3.0**; it implements CNOSSOS-EU propagation with diffraction over and around obstacles (vertical and horizontal edges) and specular reflections of configurable order. — [NoiseModelling GitHub](https://github.com/Universite-Gustave-Eiffel/NoiseModelling)

**Commercial road-noise engines used for UAM studies**
- Feng & Kang (2026) modeled drone and eVTOL routes as **elevated line sources** with octave-band spectra in **CadnaA**, ISO 9613-1/-2 propagation, 3D buildings and terrain, hard ground (G = 0), facade reflection loss 1 dB, 2 km search radius; limitation: ray approach "better suited to mid and high frequencies and represents low frequency diffraction less accurately"; no directivity, hover, transition or meteorology. — [Feng & Kang, arXiv:2609.29763 (2026)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.29763)
- ONERA used CSTB's commercial ray code **Mithra** for a source 500 ft above the Airbus Helicopters Marignane site. — [Malbéqui, Forum Acusticum 2023 (CC-BY 3.0)](https://dael.euracoustics.org/confs/fa2023/data/articles/000104.pdf)

### Inferences
- The sonAIR design is the closest production analog to what NICE-OPS would build: per-(source cell, receiver) precomputed attenuation, with a cheap on-the-fly path for unobstructed geometries above a 15° margin over the obstacle horizon. NICE-OPS's existing per-state source frame and per-observer loop would map to this by caching a building transfer only for (observer, direction) pairs whose line of sight is near or below the local skyline.
- Every production tool that touches buildings uses vertical-plane (over-the-top) diffraction plus incoherent specular reflection; lateral (around-the-edge) diffraction is consistently dropped for cost, and Empa justifies it by time-averaging over a moving source.
- The repeated statement that building effects are "local" and produce grid-scale artifacts means a footprint tool needs either facade receivers or a statistical treatment (per-receiver urban correction), not a 150 m grid with explicit buildings.

### Gaps
- HELENA (helicopter environmental noise analysis, used in UK/EU studies) and ANOPP2/AARON: no source found describing any building treatment; I could not confirm whether ANOPP2 has an urban propagation module.
- ACRP WOD 43's specific recommended algorithms and dB results could not be retrieved (NAP reader is JS-only; the TRB PDF URL failed). Worth retrieving manually — it is the most directly relevant US document on aircraft barrier/building diffraction.
- Harmonoise's aircraft-specific treatment: no source found; NORAH2's CNOSSOS diffraction scheme details (lateral paths or not) were not documented in the Cannes paper.

## 2. NASA and academic UAM/eVTOL urban propagation work: methods and results

### Takeaway
NASA's UAM work so far frames urban propagation as a gap rather than delivering a tool; academic groups (TU Delft/Casalino, SNU, ONERA, Georgia Tech) use ray, Gaussian-beam or image-source tracing from a noise sphere/hemisphere. Reported magnitudes: about +5 dB in illuminated zones from multiple reflections, +8 dB near a building corner and up to +14 dB with source and receiver both inside a canyon, and shadow-zone reductions of 10 to over 20 dB, which ray methods overpredict because they omit diffraction.

### Cited Findings
- NASA/TP-2020-5007433 (Rizzi et al., UAM Noise Working Group): UAM environments "will likely include vertiports, urban canyons, and densely populated areas, there is more in common with rotorcraft operations than with aircraft operations in the vicinity of airports"; vertiports "will include reflective structures (e.g., buildings) close to aircraft"; "Urban canyons will have a large variation in length scales, and geometrical features will result in reflection, reverberation, and diffraction, and may act as waveguides"; measurements are needed "for developing and validating multipath propagation tools," and "canonical urban geometries may expedite development." No dB magnitudes given. — [Rizzi et al. 2020](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205007433/downloads/NASA-TP-2020-5007433.pdf)
- Yunus, Casalino, Avallone, Ragni (Aerosp. Sci. Technol. 2023): noise stored on a sphere around the aircraft and propagated with the Gaussian beam tracer UYGUR (3D terrain, wind). "Compared to flat terrain ... the building blocks increase on-ground noise levels by 5 dB in the illuminated zone due to multiple reflections; they also shield the incoming sound field by creating shadow zones behind the building. The shielding increases with increasing frequency"; BPF1 vs BPF2 directivity changes the footprint by up to 40 dB; versus a straight-ray propagator the GBT "reduces the prediction error by 5 dB in the illuminated zones and 35 dB in the terrain shadow zones." — [Yunus et al. 2023 abstract (OA)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ast.2023.108410)
- From the full text of the same paper: the GBT "does not account for diffracted wavefields behind the building blocks," so the straight-ray-vs-GBT shadow difference of "more than 20 dB" would be smaller with diffraction; wind changes the footprint by up to 23 dB; noise on the vertiport rises up to 30 dB in one wind case; straight-ray model ran in 3.7 s, "15 times faster than the GBT"; GBT cases 46.6–122.5 s on a Xeon Gold 6140; multiple reflections added 0–5 dB in the illuminated zone. — [Yunus et al. 2023, PoliTo repository](https://iris.polito.it/bitstream/11583/2978683/1/2023YunusAST.pdf)
- Yunus, Varriale, Snellen (AIAA 2024-3335): vertiport on a 130 m roof, rigid ground; pitch-down at approach raised levels "by 10 dB" in a flat region 200–400 m away (a directivity effect); buildings "further shield the incoming noise and further decrease the noise levels at receivers distributed behind the building blocks," less so at low frequency due to edge diffraction. — [Yunus et al. 2024, TU Delft](https://pure.tudelft.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/213600077/yunus-et-al-2024-efficient-noise-footprint-computation-for-urban-air-mobility-maneuvers-in-vertiport-environments.pdf)
- Malbéqui (ONERA, Forum Acusticum 2023): ray tracing validated against image-source references for a corner and a canyon, octave bands 63 Hz–8 kHz, elevated VTOL source: "close to the corner an increase of about 8 decibels can be observed vs the free field. When both the source and the microphone are in the canyon, an increase up to 14 decibels is found"; destructive interference at 250 Hz 4 m from a wall; canyon acts as a channel radiating from its ends with "pronounced diffraction effects by the edges." Stated next steps: finite-height buildings, moving sources, aerology around buildings. — [Malbéqui 2023](https://dael.euracoustics.org/confs/fa2023/data/articles/000104.pdf)
- Kim & Lee (SNU, JASA 155, 2024): hemisphere from a multirotor framework, ray tracing with absorption and multiple reflections into randomly generated 3D urban areas; a CNN trained on 45,000 maps reproduces ray tracing with RMSE 2.56 dB at more than 1800× speed. — [Kim & Lee 2024 abstract via OpenAlex](https://doi.org/10.1121/10.0024242)
- Kim, Ko, Yu & Lee (JASA 158, 2025): that surrogate drives deep-RL low-noise approach trajectories to a vertiport in a simplified urban environment. — [Kim et al. 2025](https://pubs.aip.org/asa/jasa/article/158/1/476/3353178/Low-noise-trajectory-optimization-of-urban-air)
- Gao, Porcayo & Clarke (TR Part D 120, 103794, 2023), "virtual acoustic terrain": applies "acoustic ray tracing and the principle of reciprocity in a complex urban environment to convert different noise constraints in the city into 3D exclusion zones" that UAM trajectories avoid. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1361920923001918) (abstract/summary only). The follow-on Gao et al. (2024) paper reverts to NPD data, noting high-fidelity ray tracing is needed for downtown terrain but NPD/noise spheres are used "on larger scales." — [Gao et al., arXiv:2401.00806](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.00806)
- Goyal, de Prenter, Ragni, Casalino (JASA 159, 2026): LBM-VLES of a tonal AAM-like source downstream of an isolated building; wake turbulence gives temporal SPL fluctuations "exceeding 3 dB in localized regions." — [Goyal et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1121/10.0042402)
- Feng & Kang (2026), ten 2 × 2 km districts on six continents, 100 m level flyovers: aircraft-induced Lden increases "above 20 dB(A)" in some semi-enclosed London and New York courtyards that were quiet before (road-shielded but open to the sky); facade exposure varies with height and "acoustic visibility to the flight route"; spectral attenuation varied little with frequency ("most slopes below 1 dB per octave"). — [Feng & Kang 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.29763)
- Casalino, Barbarino & Visingardi, "Simulation of Helicopter Community Noise in Complex Urban Geometry," AIAA J. 49 (2011): early CAA/FW-H plus ray-tracing chain for helicopters in urban geometry. — [DOI 10.2514/1.J050774](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/258475225_Simulation_of_Helicopter_Community_Noise_in_Complex_Urban_Geometry) (title/authors only; no numbers retrieved)

### Inferences
- The academic chains are structurally the same as NICE-OPS (noise sphere, then propagation) and use ray or beam tracing directly from the sphere. Their headline magnitudes (+5 dB from reflections, shadows of 20–40 dB) are model results without diffraction and should be treated as upper bounds on shadow depth.
- The ONERA +8 dB corner and +14 dB in-canyon figures are coherent, image-source, single-frequency/octave results with perfectly rigid walls; incoherent energy summation would give about +3 dB per reflecting plane instead.

### Gaps
- I found no NASA-released urban propagation tool for UAM; no Pettingill or Stephenson urban ray-tracing results were located (the "Stephenson" helicopter urban ray-tracing reference may be the Casalino et al. 2011 paper). DLR UAM urban studies were not found in this pass.
- Gao et al. 2023's quantitative results (exclusion-zone sizes, ray counts) were not accessible.

## 3. Rotorcraft-specific and aircraft measurements in cities: shielding, courtyards, facade reflection, drones

### Takeaway
Measured shielding behind buildings from aircraft is large: 10–16 dB(A) at peak (Lugten, Schiphol), 8–14 dB(A) depending on courtyard shape and flight phase, and up to 12 dB in annual LAeq right behind low buildings near a runway (Meiringen). Exposed facades gain about +3–4 dB from reflections, and an exposed-vs-opposite facade at an urban helicopter route differs by about 10 dB. Low-frequency shielding is much weaker (0–7 dB below 125 Hz).

### Cited Findings
- Lugten, Steemers & Kang (Schiphol pilot, 45 flyovers, front vs rear facades): "mean shielding effect of around 11 dB(A) for landings and 14 dB(A) for ascending airplanes" at peak; per-site front-vs-rear means 12 dB(A) (median 13, range 8–16), 10 dB(A) (range 6–16) and 14 dB(A) (median 16, range 12–16); spectrally, landings 0–7 dB in octave bands below 125 Hz versus 8–14 dB above, and take-offs about 4 dB below 125 Hz versus 8–12 dB at 125–4000 Hz. They also suggest "shielding increases when the flight height goes up," though ambient noise obscures the low-flight data. — [Lugten, Steemers, Kang, Inter-Noise pilot study (Cambridge repository)](https://api.repository.cam.ac.uk/server/api/core/bitstreams/044505b4-6042-47fe-a02f-a6ac4a5f70b9/content)
- Lugten, Wuite, Peng, Tenpierik (Building and Environment 2024, shipping-container courtyard field lab at Schiphol, 10 microphones): mean differences between facades with and without line of sight to the aircraft ranged from −1.3 to 5.0 dB(A) for arrivals and 8.7 to 13.6 dB(A) for departures; in SEL, −0.8 to 4.3 dB(A) for arrivals and 8.1 to 11.6 dB(A) for departures. Slanted roofs perpendicular to the flight direction "deflect incident sound, substantially reducing the sound levels inside courtyards"; insets on the sides facing away did not help significantly. — [Lugten et al. 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360132324002427) (abstract via search). The Inter-Noise 2022 version reports 11 dB(A) for a straight-facade courtyard up to 14 dB(A) with a slanted roof and overhang. — [Lugten 2022, DOI 10.3397/IN_2022_0976](https://ince.publisher.ingentaconnect.com/content/ince/incecp/2023/00000265/00000001/art00055)
- Courtyard opening: an opening increased courtyard noise "up to 7.2–10 dB(A) compared to a closed courtyard." — [search summary of courtyard literature](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360132324002427) (secondary; original study not identified)
- Ramseier et al. (Meiringen F/A-18 airbase, low ~10 m buildings beside the runway, measured validation): shielding by buildings reduced annual LAeq by more than 2 dB over more than half the calculation area and "up to 12 dB directly behind buildings"; building reflections raised levels on runway-facing facades by 3–4 dB, but only locally; forest and cliff reflections added 1–2 dB, locally up to 5 dB; for LAeq ≳ 80 dB differences were negligible because direct sound dominates. At a shielded microphone (G1), adding REFLECT raised simulated levels by about 5 dB and brought the median error to about 1 dB. Facade microphones 0.5 m from the wall were corrected by −6 dB for pressure doubling, "a common approach." — [Ramseier et al. 2024](https://www.dora.lib4ri.ch/empa/dload/empa:38392/PDF/view)
- Schalcher et al. (Geneva annual, shielding only): buildings caused significant reductions "particularly within 2–3 km of the runway," largest around 64 dB and in the 50–70 dB range; versus FLULA2, mean −0.9 dB in the moderate range with local reductions down to −21.3 dB (σ up to 1.9 dB); 60 dBA day contour area 12.23 km² with buildings versus 13.37 km² without (−8.5%). — [Schalcher et al. 2025](https://www.dora.lib4ri.ch/empa/dload/empa:42543/PDF/view)
- Paris Issy-les-Moulineaux heliport (MOTUS; Airbus Helicopters, Bruitparif, Univ. Gustave Eiffel): roof-mounted monitors placed to avoid facade reflection; per-participant **transfer functions** (dB(A) and dB(C)) from three Delaunay-selected measurement sites to each respondent's facade, derived from a building-aware CNOSSOS/NoiseModelling simulation along a standard trajectory. Two neighbors on opposite sides of one block differed by "approximately 10 dB" (LAmax, LAeq, Lden) because of facade orientation. Helicopter Lden median 44 dB (max 51); median LAmax 67.6 dBA; median emergence 18.2 dBA. — [Caillet et al. 2025](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-7047184/latest.pdf)
- Facade correction for aircraft: ISO 1996-2 uses +3 dB (near a reflecting surface) and +6 dB (flush on it); Flores et al. tested these for aircraft and found "front façades, U topologies, and greater line-of-sight (LOS) angles result in higher levels on façade," and proposed separate correction factors with and without direct transmission. — [Flores, Asensio, Gagliardi, Licitra, Appl. Acoust. 145 (2019)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0003682X1830598X); [Flores et al., Acoust. Aust. 2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40857-017-0102-y) (abstracts/summaries only; numerical factors not retrieved)
- Hao & Kang (Appl. Acoust. 84, 2014): in low-density areas, spatial attenuation of flyover noise correlates mainly with building frontal area index and the horizontal distance of the first-row building to the flight path; within 1000 m horizontal distance the variance of average attenuation between sites was about 4.6 dB at 3150 Hz. — [Hao & Kang 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apacoust.2013.12.001) (via search summary)
- Drones and canyons: the ONERA canyon result (+14 dB with source and receiver in the canyon) and the Yunus +5 dB are the only drone/UAM canyon magnitudes found; a 2026 field study reported some UAV LAeq 5.0 dB higher under tall tree canopies. — [Malbéqui 2023](https://dael.euracoustics.org/confs/fa2023/data/articles/000104.pdf); [Forests 17, 907 (2026)](https://doi.org/10.3390/f17080907) (search summary)

### Inferences
- The +6 dB facade rule is a measurement-correction convention (pressure doubling at a rigid surface), not a prediction that the facade receiver is 6 dB louder than free field in an energy sense. For SEL/LAeq at a 1.5–4 m receiver in a canyon, the sonX/Ramseier evidence (+3–4 dB on exposed facades) is the more relevant magnitude.
- Shielding is strongly phase- and elevation-dependent. Departures and low-elevation pass-bys give 8–16 dB(A); arrivals at Schiphol, which are more overhead, gave only −1 to 5 dB(A). This is the key dependence for a footprint metric dominated by near-overhead states.

### Gaps
- Manhattan/New York helicopter measurement studies quantifying building effects were not found in this pass, nor were London heliport (Battersea) facade studies.
- Kawai and Green drone-canyon measurements could not be located. Schäffer's Empa drone work concerns annoyance, not canyon propagation, as far as I found.
- Numerical facade correction factors from Flores et al. (2019) by elevation/LOS angle were not retrieved (paywalled).

## 4. Sonic booms in cities: methods and findings on reflection and diffusion

### Takeaway
Sonic-boom urban work uses 2D/3D Euler or FDTD (Dragna et al.; Cho & Sparrow) and ray tracing plus radiosity (Riegel & Sparrow). The consistent finding is that facade reflection, corner diffraction and canyon resonances change waveforms strongly, but the median perceived level in canyons stays within about 1 dB of flat ground. Shadow zones behind buildings are real, and diffuse reflection matters in canyons but not for an isolated building.

### Cited Findings
- Dragna, Emmanuelli, Ollivier, Blanc-Benon (JASA 151, 3792, 2022): 2D Euler with high-order finite differences, N-wave and low-boom. An isolated building's influence is "limited to an illuminated region at its front and a shadow region at its rear, whose size depends on the building's height and the Mach number"; in street canyons, facade reflections and corner diffraction add arrivals and low-frequency canyon resonances appear, but "these low-frequency oscillations have little impact on the perceived noise"; for periodic buildings, "variations in perceived noise at ground level are moderate for large streets, but become noticeable as the street width reduces." — [Dragna et al. 2022a](https://doi.org/10.1121/10.0010452)
- Dragna et al. (JASA 152, 3323, 2022): ten urban geometries from the local-climate-zone classification; for compact (high aspect ratio) geometries, levels and peak pressure vary strongly between canyons; for open ones they follow the isolated-building pattern; "the median of the perceived noise levels mostly differs by less than 1 dB from the value obtained for flat ground," with a wider range for open geometries; canyon resonance amplitude, frequency and decay vary greatly. — [Dragna et al. 2022b](https://doi.org/10.1121/10.0016442)
- Riegel & Sparrow (JASA 145, 2019): ray tracing plus radiosity against NASA SonicBOBS (2009, Edwards AFB) data at a large building; general agreement; diffuse reflections "does not have a dramatic effect" and "is not worth the additional computational cost for an isolated building." — [Riegel & Sparrow 2019](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.5097582)
- Riegel & Sparrow (JASA 155, 1527, 2024): urban canyons by ray tracing plus radiosity (no diffraction): "strong shadow zones where no sound was detected," one to four boom arrivals elsewhere, PLdB from 0 (shadow) to 94 dB; "Diffuse reflections was the parameter that had the greatest impact," and more reflections gave "a dramatic reduction in the PLdB." — [Riegel & Sparrow 2024](https://doi.org/10.1121/10.0024980)
- Cho & Sparrow (JASA 129, 2011): FDTD plus 2006 NASA flight test, "building spiking": front-shock amplification and a post-tail-shock spike on the incident facade, attributed to frequency-dependent diffraction; longer rise times (shaped booms) reduce it. — [Cho & Sparrow 2011](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.3543984)

### Inferences
- The sonic-boom result that canyon *median* levels sit within 1 dB of flat ground, while individual locations vary widely, is useful framing for a footprint tool: for a high-elevation source the population-averaged urban correction may be small even where local effects are large.
- The Riegel 2024 zero-PLdB shadows show what omitting diffraction does, and are a caution for any NICE-OPS ray-only implementation.

### Gaps
- Loubeau's and Pierce's specific urban work was not retrieved in this pass; the Dragna 2022 full texts (dB ranges per geometry) were not retrieved (HAL served HTML).

## 5. Which urban effects matter most for footprint metrics, and how they scale with source elevation angle

### Takeaway
For SEL, LAeq and Lden, the dominant urban effect in every quantified aircraft study is shielding at receivers without line of sight (rear facades, courtyards, ground behind buildings): typically 2–12 dB, up to about 21 dB locally. Facade reflection gain on exposed sides is smaller (+3–4 dB computed, +5 dB in a ray model, +8 to +14 dB coherent worst case in a canyon). Both effects shrink as the source climbs, because near-overhead incidence gives most receivers a direct view. Empa's 15° threshold over the local obstacle horizon is the only operational elevation criterion found.

### Cited Findings
- Shielding dominates annual metrics: "The large-scale noise-reduction by building shielding clearly dominates the local noise-increase due to reflections"; reflections matter mainly "in areas without direct noise incidence. However, such situations are rare in the case of aircraft noise." For LAeq ≳ 80 dB (closest, highest-angle exposure) differences are negligible. — [Ramseier et al. 2024](https://www.dora.lib4ri.ch/empa/dload/empa:38392/PDF/view)
- Elevation threshold: sonX treats propagation as "simple" (no precomputation, unshielded) when the aircraft is more than 15° above the line-of-sight horizon formed by terrain/buildings. — [Schalcher et al. 2025](https://www.dora.lib4ri.ch/empa/dload/empa:42543/PDF/view)
- Building and meteorological effects are concentrated near the runway where "aircraft are either on the ground or at low altitudes, leading to small source-receiver angles"; below 50 dB, effects were negligible "as the aircraft ... are already high above ground and the sound incidence angles are steep." — [Schalcher et al. 2025](https://www.dora.lib4ri.ch/empa/dload/empa:42543/PDF/view)
- Low buildings (~10 m) shield strongly when "the aircraft are mostly still on the runway during pass-by." — [Ramseier et al. 2024](https://www.dora.lib4ri.ch/empa/dload/empa:38392/PDF/view)
- Phase dependence (Schiphol): departures 8.7–13.6 dB(A) Lmax-type and 8.1–11.6 dB(A) SEL; arrivals −1.3 to 5.0 dB(A) and −0.8 to 4.3 dB(A) SEL. — [Lugten et al. 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360132324002427)
- Classical lateral-attenuation data: excess attenuation factors varied "from a maximum at small elevation angles to zero at an elevation angle of approximately 40 degrees." — [search summary of NASA lateral-attenuation literature](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19790044953) (secondary; ground effect, not buildings)
- Low frequencies are shielded less (0–7 dB below 125 Hz versus 8–14 dB above); the Yunus papers likewise note low-frequency diffraction into shadows. — [Lugten et al. pilot](https://api.repository.cam.ac.uk/server/api/core/bitstreams/044505b4-6042-47fe-a02f-a6ac4a5f70b9/content); [Yunus et al. 2024](https://pure.tudelft.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/213600077/yunus-et-al-2024-efficient-noise-footprint-computation-for-urban-air-mobility-maneuvers-in-vertiport-environments.pdf)
- Courtyards open to the sky but shielded from roads get the largest *relative* increase from overflights (>20 dB(A) Lden in London/NY at 100 m altitude). — [Feng & Kang 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.29763)
- Facade orientation alone gives about a 10 dB difference in LAmax/LAeq/Lden between neighbors for an urban helicopter route. — [Caillet et al. 2025](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-7047184/latest.pdf)
- Building wake turbulence adds >3 dB temporal variability locally (AAM tonal source). — [Goyal et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1121/10.0042402)

### Inferences
- For SEL integrated over the 10-dB-down window, the peak is usually set by the highest-elevation states, which are least affected by shielding; urban effects therefore act mostly on the window's shoulders and on far-lateral receivers. This is consistent with the Schiphol arrival/departure contrast and with Empa's "negligible above 80 dB" finding. A NICE-OPS urban model would change SEL most for receivers lateral to the track and for low hover/approach segments near vertiports, where elevation angles are below about 15–30°.
- A practical magnitude envelope for planning: exposed facade +3 dB (incoherent, one plane; up to +6 dB at the surface), canyon multi-reflection +3 to +5 dB (incoherent) or up to +8 to +14 dB (coherent, rigid), shielded rear facade or courtyard −8 to −16 dB(A) at low elevation and −0 to −5 dB(A) for near-overhead arrivals.

### Gaps
- No published table of building attenuation versus source elevation angle for aircraft was found, apart from the qualitative and phase-based contrasts above and the 15° sonX criterion. This would have to be derived, for example from canyon geometry (H/W ratio) and the line-of-sight cutoff angle.
- No study was found that reports urban effects specifically on DNL for rotorcraft.

## 6. Precomputed per-receiver transfer functions indexed by direction (reciprocity), and attenuation tables

### Takeaway
Two production-adjacent precedents exist. sonAIR precomputes attenuation per (airspace cell corner, receiver) and interpolates by aircraft position, which is source-position indexed rather than direction indexed. MOTUS/Airbus used per-receiver dB(A)/dB(C) transfer functions from measurement sites to each facade. Gao, Porcayo & Clarke explicitly use reciprocity (tracing from receivers) to build 3D exclusion zones. No tool was found that stores a per-receiver transfer function indexed by incidence direction (azimuth and elevation), which is the form NICE-OPS's sphere lookup would most naturally consume.

### Cited Findings
- sonAIR: attenuation precalculated at airspace cell corners for each receiver, "weighted by the aircraft position within the cell," cell size adaptive to altitude and horizontal distance; above 15° over the obstacle horizon, computed on the fly. — [Schalcher et al. 2025](https://www.dora.lib4ri.ch/empa/dload/empa:42543/PDF/view)
- MOTUS: "Transfer functions take the form of ... corrections both in dB(A) and dB(C), between the noise levels at each microphone and each participant"; clustering by altitude, route and speed showed acceptable error, so "a unique transfer function per participant based on a standard trajectory" was used. — [Caillet et al. 2025](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-7047184/latest.pdf)
- Gao, Porcayo & Clarke 2023: "acoustic ray tracing and the principle of reciprocity in a complex urban environment" convert receiver noise constraints into 3D airspace exclusion zones. — [Gao et al. 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1361920923001918)
- Kim & Lee 2024: a learned surrogate (CNN, RMSE 2.56 dB, >1800× faster) replaces per-scenario ray tracing in randomly generated urban areas. — [Kim & Lee 2024](https://doi.org/10.1121/10.0024242)

### Inferences
- Reciprocity suits NICE-OPS's structure: trace once from each observer (or observer class) over a direction grid (elevation × azimuth) to form a per-band urban gain table G(observer, θ, φ, band), then look up the incidence direction per state, just as the sphere is looked up by emission direction. sonAIR's source-cell indexing is the alternative, and costs ~4 million cell points per airport.
- The MOTUS evidence that one transfer function per receiver was adequate across trajectory clusters suggests a coarse direction table could hold A-weighted accuracy for SEL/Lmax.

### Gaps
- No published direction-indexed per-receiver urban transfer table (incoherent or coherent) for aircraft was found; neither its accuracy versus full tracing nor its sensitivity to moving-source averaging appears to have been studied.
- Licenses: sonAIR/sonX, CadnaA, Mithra and UYGUR are proprietary or unreleased (no public license found); NoiseModelling is GPL-3.0, which would constrain direct code reuse in MIT-licensed NICE-OPS.
